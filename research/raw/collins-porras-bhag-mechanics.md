# Collins/Porras BHAG Mechanics: What Makes a Big Hairy Audacious Goal

## Origin and Definition

The term "Big Hairy Audacious Goal" (BHAG, pronounced "Bee-hag") was introduced in Jim Collins and Jerry I. Porras's *Built to Last: Successful Habits of Visionary Companies* (HarperBusiness, 1994). The book reported on a six-year research project (1988-1994) comparing eighteen "visionary companies" to eighteen "comparison companies" across matched pairs.

Collins and Porras defined a BHAG as:
> "A clear and compelling goal that serves as a unifying focal point of effort, galvanizing people and creating team spirit. It has a clear finish line, is tangible, energizing, and highly focused. People can 'get it' right away with little or no explanation."

## The Five Attributes of a BHAG

1. **Clear finish line**: A BHAG is not an open-ended aspiration. It has an endpoint that can be seen and celebrated. This is what distinguishes a BHAG from a mission statement or vision statement.

2. **Tangible and energizing**: It can be visualized. "Put a man on the moon and return him safely by the end of the decade" is tangible. "Be the best company in our industry" is not.

3. **Highly focused**: It concentrates organizational energy on one thing. A BHAG is a rallying cry, not a strategic plan. It does not describe how; it describes what must be achieved.

4. **Likely to be reached in 10-30 years**: BHAGs are not quarterly targets. They require sustained effort across multiple leadership generations. This timescale forces the organization to build institutional capacity rather than rely on individual heroics.

5. **High likelihood of requiring capabilities the organization does not currently possess**: A BHAG is audacious precisely because it demands transformation. It requires the organization to change, grow, and acquire new competencies.

## BHAG Examples from Visionary Companies

**Boeing (1952)**: "Become the dominant player in commercial aircraft and bring the world into the jet age." Boeing had built military aircraft and a few propeller-driven commercial planes. The 707 was the bet-the-company project. By 1970, Boeing had delivered over 1,000 jetliners.

**IBM (1960s)**: "Build the IBM System/360, a family of computers that would be compatible across all models and would dominate the industry." The 360 project cost $5 billion (more than the Manhattan Project) and nearly bankrupted IBM. It became the foundation of IBM's dominance for two decades.

**Sony (1950s)**: "Change the worldwide image of Japanese products as being of poor quality." Sony was a tiny post-war Tokyo company. The BHAG drove the invention of the transistor radio, the Walkman, and the Trinitron TV.

**Walmart (1970s)**: "Become a $125 billion company by the year 2000." In 1977, Walmart was a $676 million regional retailer. By 1990, it had surpassed Kmart. By 2000, it was at $165 billion.

**3M (1980s)**: "30 percent of sales from products introduced in the last four years." This forced 3M to continuously innovate rather than milk existing product lines.

**General Electric (1980s)**: "Become the most competitive enterprise in the world under Jack Welch's '#1 or #2 in every market' strategy." While not framed as a BHAG in the book, the strategy functioned as one.

**Merck**: "Develop medicines that combat specific diseases." Merck focused on breakthrough research, including the development of the measles vaccine and the statin drugs.

**Disney**: "Build a theme park that adults would enjoy as much as children." Disneyland (1955) was the result.

**Nike (1960s)**: "Crush Adidas." Phil Knight's early BHAG was brutally competitive and specific.

## BHAG Categories

Collins and Porras identified several types:
- **Target BHAGs**: Quantitative (Walmart's $125 billion)
- **Common-enemy BHAGs**: Competitive (Nike vs. Adidas)
- **Role-model BHAGs**: "Become the X of our industry"
- **Internal-transformation BHAGs**: "Transform from a regional to a national player"

## The Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress Paradox

This is the central theoretical contribution of *Built to Last*. Collins and Porras argue that visionary companies are simultaneously conservative and progressive:

- **Preserve the Core**: The core ideology (values and purpose) never changes. It is the anchor. The core is not a product or strategy; it is a set of beliefs about what the organization stands for.
- **Stimulate Progress**: Everything else is open to change. Products, strategies, practices, structures, markets — all are malleable in service of the core.

The BHAG sits at the intersection: it is a mechanism for stimulating progress while preserving the core. A BHAG that is inconsistent with the core ideology will fail because the organization will not commit to it. A BHAG that is merely an extension of current capabilities will not stimulate progress.

This is the paradox: the organization must change everything except its soul. The BHAG is the engine of that change.

## Criticism: Rosenzweig and The Halo Effect

Phil Rosenzweig, in *The Halo Effect... and the Eight Other Business Delusions that Deceive Managers* (Free Press, 2007), devoted significant space to critiquing *Built to Last* and its genre. His key arguments:

1. **The Delusion of Rigorous Research**: Collins and Porras collected extensive data, but the data were tainted by the Halo Effect. When a company is known to be successful, researchers (and managers) attribute positive traits to it. When the same company falters, those traits are reinterpreted as flaws.

2. **Selection on the dependent variable**: By choosing companies that were already successful and retrospectively identifying their traits, the research cannot establish causation. It can only establish correlation.

3. **Survivorship bias**: Companies that had BHAGs and failed are not in the sample. We do not know whether BHAGs cause success or whether successful companies are simply more likely to articulate BHAGs.

4. **Regression to the mean**: Daniel Kahneman, in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), applied this statistical principle to *Built to Last*. He argued that companies selected for extreme performance in one period will tend toward average performance in subsequent periods. The "visionary" companies outperformed the market from 1926-1990, but that outperformance is partly a statistical artifact of selecting companies that had already done well. Kahneman: "The comparison of firms that have been more or less successful is to a significant extent a comparison between firms that have been more or less lucky."

## The Empirical Test: Visionary Companies After 1994

The most damaging criticism is empirical. By 2007 (the 10-year mark), Fast Company noted:
> "Ten years on, almost half of the visionary companies on the list have slipped dramatically in performance and reputation, and their vision currently seems more blurred than clairvoyant. Consider the fates of Motorola, Ford, Sony, Walt Disney, Boeing, Nordstrom, and Merck."

By 2026, the list looks even worse:
- **Motorola**: Acquired by Google (2012), sold to Lenovo (2014). Its mobile phone dominance evaporated.
- **Ford**: Nearly collapsed in the 2008 financial crisis; required a government bailout. Survived but never regained visionary status.
- **Sony**: Lost dominance in electronics to Apple and Samsung. Sold its PC business, spun off its entertainment division. Still large but no longer visionary.
- **Disney**: Acquired Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, and Fox, becoming a media conglomerate. Its BHAG shifted from theme parks to streaming (Disney+), with mixed results and recent struggles in 2024-2025.
- **Boeing**: The 737 MAX crashes (2018-2019) and the Alaska Airlines door panel blowout (2024) revealed catastrophic safety and quality failures. Boeing went from visionary to cautionary tale.
- **General Electric**: Collapsed as a conglomerate. Divested GE Capital, sold appliances, split into three companies (2023). Removed from the Dow Jones in 2018.
- **Wells Fargo**: Not in the visionary list, but the comparison company (American Express was visionary). Wells Fargo's fake accounts scandal (2016) destroyed its reputation.
- **Citicorp**: Required massive government bailout in 2008. Still recovering.
- **IBM**: Declined for decades. Sold its PC division to Lenovo (2005). Failed to dominate cloud computing. Turned to AI (Watson) with mixed results.
- **HP**: Split into HP Inc. and Hewlett Packard Enterprise (2015). Lost its status as a premier tech company.
- **Merck**: Still a strong pharmaceutical company but faced major Vioxx litigation (2004-2007) that damaged its reputation.
- **Nordstrom**: Family buyout (2024), struggling with retail transition.

The companies that have held up best:
- **3M**: Still innovative, though facing PFAS litigation and a 2024 CEO shakeup.
- **Johnson & Johnson**: Strong but spun off its consumer health division (2023).
- **Procter & Gamble**: Still a consumer goods giant.
- **Walmart**: Transformed into an omnichannel retail and logistics giant. Added a new BHAG around e-commerce competing with Amazon.
- **American Express**: Survived and adapted.

Collins's defense: "The books never promised that these companies would always be great, just that they were once great." This defense is technically correct but raises a deeper question: if the traits Collins identified (including BHAGs) do not predict future greatness, what is their practical value?

## The BHAG-OKR Relationship

BHAGs and OKRs occupy different layers of organizational intent:
- **BHAG**: The 10-30 year audacious goal. The "moonshot." Unifying and emotional.
- **Mission**: The organization's ongoing purpose. Why it exists.
- **OKRs**: The quarterly or annual measurable targets that move the organization toward the BHAG.
- **Tasks**: The individual actions that achieve the key results.

Without a BHAG, OKRs become task lists disconnected from meaning. Without OKRs, BHAGs become empty slogans. The relationship is causal and hierarchical: BHAG → Mission → OKRs → Tasks.

## BHAGs in the Intent Architecture

In the framework of mission command and commander's intent:
- The BHAG is the commander's intent at the strategic level
- The mission statement is the standing purpose
- OKRs are the operational orders that translate intent into action
- Tasks are the tactical execution

The critical function of the BHAG is to prevent the cascade problem: without a vivid, tangible strategic intent, operational targets (OKRs) devolve into task lists that optimize locally but miss the strategic purpose.
