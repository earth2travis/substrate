## 1. The claim under examination

INTERPRETATION: design thinking presents itself as a discipline with fifty-plus years of intellectual ancestry, not a 2000s consulting invention. The strongest version of the lineage claim runs: the 1960s design methods movement posed the problem (how to make design rational), Simon named the science, Rittel named the problem type (wicked), Schon and Cross described what designers actually do, Rowe named the phrase, and IDEO plus the d.school carried it into business. Testing that chain of custody is the job of this file.

## 2. Prehistory: the 1960s design methods movement

The design methods movement emerged in Britain in the early 1960s out of a conviction that industrial design, architecture, and engineering needed systematic, teachable methods rather than reliance on individual intuition (BACKGROUND). Its intellectual raw material came from wartime and postwar operations research, systems engineering, and the new computing machinery, imported into design through figures who moved between engineering and architectural worlds (BACKGROUND).

The movement's founding premise was that postwar reconstruction, consumer-product complexity, and the scale of public planning had outrun the craft tradition's capacity, so design needed its own science (BACKGROUND). INTERPRETATION: this premise is identical in structure to Taylorism's premise about manual work and to the Toyota system's premise about production (see [[toyota-production-system]]): a tacit practice is made explicit so it can be taught, measured, and improved. The design methods movement is the design field's own scientific-management moment, and the second-generation critique is its internal rebellion.

The Conference on Design Methods, held in London in September 1962, is generally treated as the founding event of the movement; it was organized by John Page and others and its proceedings were published as "Conference on Design Methods" (1963), edited by J. Christopher Jones and D. G. Thornley (BACKGROUND). The conference's explicit goal was to move design from a craft model to a systematic one, and its attendees included engineers, architects, and industrial designers who would dominate the field's publishing for the next decade (BACKGROUND). INTERPRETATION: the 1962 conference's choice of the word "methods" in its title is itself a claim: it asserts that design has method at all, which was the movement's polemical position against the studio tradition's emphasis on tacit judgment.

J. Christopher Jones published "Design Methods: Seeds of Human Futures" in 1970, the movement's most influential textbook, codifying systematic techniques and later, in its second edition, recording Jones's own disillusionment with over-rationalized methods (BACKGROUND). Jones's trajectory is emblematic: he began as a proponent of systematic method and ended by arguing that design is too complex and human for any fixed procedure, a shift that prefigures the second-generation critique (BACKGROUND).

Christopher Alexander published "Notes on the Synthesis of Form" in 1964, based on his Harvard doctoral dissertation; it proposed a formal, diagram-based method for decomposing design problems into hierarchies of requirements, and it became a foundational text not only for design methods but, later, for software patterns (BACKGROUND). Alexander himself soon repudiated the mechanistic reading of his own method, an early sign of the movement's internal recoil from over-formalization (BACKGROUND).

Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber, both at UC Berkeley, published "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning" in Policy Sciences, volume 4, pages 155 to 169, June 1973 (FACT: verified against SpringerLink, DOI 10.1007/BF01405730, and the PMC retrospective "Fifty years after the wicked-problems conception"). The paper introduced the term "wicked problems" for social-planning problems that have no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and no true/false solutions, in contrast to the "tame" problems of science and engineering (FACT: same sources; the abstract states "They are 'wicked' problems, whereas science has developed to deal with 'tame' problems").

Rittel first presented the wicked-problems idea at a 1967 Berkeley seminar; Webber convinced him to publish six years later (FACT: per the PMC retrospective citing Skaburskis 2008).

The same 1973 paper contains the first-generation versus second-generation split: the "systems-approach of the first generation" (1940s and 1950s operations research, phased understand-then-solve pipelines) is declared inadequate for wicked problems, and "approaches of the second generation" are framed as argumentative processes in which problem and solution emerge together among participants (FACT: quoted text from the paper itself via the urbanpolicy.net PDF and the PMC retrospective).

Rittel elaborated this second-generation position in "The Reasoning of Designers" (1984, unpublished lecture/working paper, IGP Stuttgart) and in his Issue-Based Information System (IBIS), which models design as an argumentation structure of issues, positions, and arguments (BACKGROUND). INTERPRETATION: the first versus second generation split is the hinge of the whole lineage. Everything later called design thinking inherits the second-generation premise that design is argumentative, iterative, and problem-framing rather than a linear optimization pipeline.

Earlier naming candidate: L. Bruce Archer at the Royal College of Art published "Systematic Method for Designers" in 1965 (issued as a series of articles in Design magazine and as an RCA offprint), arguably the earliest self-conscious statement of a systematic design methodology in Britain (BACKGROUND).

## 3. Herbert Simon: design as the core of the professions

Herbert A. Simon published "The Sciences of the Artificial" with MIT Press in 1969 (hardcover January 15, 1969; paperback 1970), with a second edition in 1981 and a third in 1996 (FACT: verified against the MIT Press catalog page, the Wikipedia entry, and the Monoskop scan of the third edition whose prefaces confirm the 1969 first edition and 1981 revision). The book originated as the Karl Taylor Compton lectures at MIT in 1968, and its arguments are inseparable from Simon's simultaneous work on artificial intelligence and bounded rationality (BACKGROUND).

The book's fifth chapter, "The Science of Design: Creating the Artificial," argues that professional schools had been hollowed out by natural-science envy: "schools of engineering became schools of physics and mathematics; schools of architecture became schools of natural science; business schools became schools of finite mathematics" (BACKGROUND: widely quoted passage, wording approximate, not re-verified verbatim this session).

Simon's positive claim: "The proper study of those who are concerned with the artificial is the way in which the adaptation of means to environments is brought about, and the central activity of such study is design" (BACKGROUND: paraphrase of the chapter's thesis; exact wording not re-verified). INTERPRETATION: Simon supplies the movement's epistemological charter. He legitimizes design as a science of the artificial distinct from natural science, which is precisely the warrant later business-school design thinking invokes when it claims design is a rigorous discipline rather than decoration.

Simon's own model of design was strongly information-processing and optimization-flavored, closer to Rittel's first generation than to the argumentative second generation. INTERPRETATION: this is a real tension in the lineage. Design thinking claims Simon's legitimacy while practicing Rittel's epistemology; the two are not the same theory.

Simon's famous definition of design, "Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones" (BACKGROUND: widely quoted from chapter 5, wording approximate, not re-verified verbatim this session), is what lets the business version claim that executives, strategists, and policymakers are all already designers. INTERPRETATION: the sentence does the same rhetorical work for design thinking that "every company is a technology company" does for digital transformation: it universalizes the practice and thereby universalizes the market for teaching it.

## 4. Designerly ways of knowing and the reflective practitioner

Nigel Cross published "Designerly Ways of Knowing" as an article in Design Studies (volume 3, number 4, October 1982), arguing that design has its own epistemological tradition, distinct from science and the humanities, with its own things to know, ways of knowing them, and ways of finding out about them (BACKGROUND). The article was a direct intervention against the design methods movement's original program: Cross argued that the attempt to import scientific method into design had failed on its own terms, and that the better question was what design's native intelligence looks like when observed on its own ground (BACKGROUND).

Cross later expanded the article into the book "Designerly Ways of Knowing" (Springer, 2006), and the phrase became the banner of a research program inside the Design Research Society and the journal Design Studies, treating design cognition as an object of empirical study (BACKGROUND).

Donald Schon published "The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action" in 1983, analyzing architecture studios and other professional practices to argue that competent practitioners work by "reflection-in-action": framing problems, conducting experiments in the situation, and reframing, rather than by applying technical-rational theory to pre-given problems (BACKGROUND). Schon was explicit that his target was the "technical rationality" inherited from Simon and the sciences of the artificial; he argued that professional practice in the "swampy lowland" of messy problems cannot be reduced to the "high ground" of theory-driven technique (BACKGROUND).

INTERPRETATION: Schon and Cross together relocated the interesting question from "what methods should designers follow" to "what do designers actually do when they are good." This empirical, cognition-focused turn is what later let design thinking present itself as a portable cognitive style (empathy, reframing, prototyping) rather than a fixed procedure, which is exactly the form in which business could adopt it.

## 5. Lawson: designers think differently

Bryan Lawson published "How Designers Think" in 1980 (Architectural Press; later editions 1990, 1997, 2005), reporting observational studies of architects and concluding that designers are solution-focused rather than problem-focused: they generate a candidate solution early and use it to understand the problem, the reverse of the scientific method's analysis-then-synthesis sequence (BACKGROUND). Lawson's work, like Cross's, was part of the Design Research Society's shift toward empirical design cognition studies in the late 1970s and early 1980s (BACKGROUND).

INTERPRETATION: Lawson's solution-first finding is the empirical ancestor of the d.school's bias-toward-action and prototyping ethos, and of the claim that design thinking is a different mode of reasoning (later labeled abductive by Roger Martin) rather than applied science.

## 6. Rowe names the phrase

Peter G. Rowe, architect and later dean of the Harvard Graduate School of Design, published "Design Thinking" in 1987 (MIT Press), the book generally credited with establishing "design thinking" as the standard term (BACKGROUND). Rowe's book examined the design process in architecture and urban design, focusing on the episodic, heuristic structure of design reasoning rather than on method prescriptions (BACKGROUND). INTERPRETATION: the phrase existed in scattered earlier use (Rowe himself had used it in a 1982 working paper, per secondary accounts), but the 1987 book is the anchor point; before Rowe, "design methods" dominated; after Rowe, "design thinking" becomes the field's name for its subject. Rowe's own academic lineage matters: he was trained at Rice and later taught at Harvard, placing him at the intersection of architectural theory and urban design rather than industrial design or engineering, which helps explain why his book's tone is phenomenological rather than procedural (BACKGROUND).

## 7. The pivot to business: IDEO and Stanford

David Kelley, a Stanford-trained engineer who joined the Stanford faculty (teaching in the product design program), founded David Kelley Design in 1978 in Palo Alto (BACKGROUND; the 1978 founding of his firm is corroborated by the Wikipedia IDEO entry surfaced in live verification).

IDEO was formed in 1991 by merger in Palo Alto (FACT: verified against the IDEO Wikipedia entry and IDEO's own about page, both confirming 1991 and the Palo Alto founding by David Kelley, Bill Moggridge, and Mike Nuttall).

Important correction to the common three-firm version: Wikipedia records the merger as four firms, David Kelley Design (est. 1978), Moggridge Associates (London, est. 1969), ID Two (San Francisco, est. 1979), and Matrix Product Design (est. 1983), while IDEO's own about page names only the three founders and some secondary accounts compress the story to three firms (FACT: the four-firm composition comes from the live Wikipedia extract; the three-firm compression appears in secondary accounts including OpenCulture and an IDEO redux site). The four-firm version should be treated as the more complete record.

IDEO's famous 1999 Nightline segment (redesigning the shopping cart in five days) and its work on the first Apple mouse (via David Kelley Design, before the merger) made it the public face of applied design process in Silicon Valley (BACKGROUND). The shopping cart segment in particular functioned as a mass-media demonstration of the five-step process a decade before the d.school codified it: multidisciplinary team, rapid ethnography, brainstorm, prototype, test, all compressed for television (BACKGROUND). INTERPRETATION: the Nightline episode is the earliest proof that design process could be packaged as spectacle for a general business audience, which is the packaging insight the HBR article later monetized.

The Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford (the d.school) was founded by David Kelley and Bernard Roth in 2004 and formally inaugurated on 3 October 2005, funded by a US$35 million donation from Hasso Plattner, co-founder of SAP (FACT: verified against the d.school's own history page (2005 founding), the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design Wikipedia entry (2004 founding by Kelley and Roth, 3 October 2005 inauguration, $35 million), a Bloomberg 2005 report ($35 million), and a 2015 thisisdesignthinking interview with Plattner confirming the October 2005 $35 million gift). The 2004 versus 2005 discrepancy is a founding-versus-inauguration distinction, not a conflict.

INTERPRETATION: the d.school is the institutional pivot of the entire lineage. It took the academic design-research tradition (Cross, Lawson, Schon) and re-potted it as an interdisciplinary, project-based curriculum for non-designers: engineers, MBAs, med students. Design thinking stopped being a description of what designers do and became a teachable offering to business.

## 8. Tim Brown: the HBR moment

Tim Brown, IDEO's CEO and chair, published "Design Thinking" as the cover article of the June 2008 issue of Harvard Business Review (FACT: verified against hbr.org, which dates the article to the June 2008 issue, and the HBR June 2008 table of contents listing it).

The article's argument: "thinking like a designer can transform the way you develop products, services, processes, and even strategy," structured around inspiration, ideation, and implementation, with Thomas Edison as the exemplary historical design thinker (FACT: quoted from the article as surfaced by HBR and the readings.design PDF this session).

Brown expanded the argument into the book "Change by Design: How Design Thinking Transforms Organizations and Inspires Innovation" in 2009 (HarperBusiness) (BACKGROUND; the 2009 publication date is consistent with the verified 2008 article and standard bibliographic record). The book's subtitle is worth noting: "transforms organizations" signals that the product being sold is no longer a design process but an organizational change method, and "inspires innovation" ties design thinking to the innovation-management discourse that dominated business publishing in the late 2000s (INTERPRETATION).

INTERPRETATION: the June 2008 HBR article is the single most consequential document in design thinking's business migration. It arrived months before the financial crisis, offered executives a growth narrative when finance-led strategies were discredited, and converted an academic research topic into a management genre. The 2008 to 2009 HBR-plus-book sequence is the exact analog of how lean methodology traveled from Toyota research into the startup mainstream (see [[lean-startup]]). The article's timing is also worth noting: it appeared at the peak of the pre-crash expansion, when many corporations were looking for non-financial sources of advantage, and it was followed within months by a crisis that made cost-cutting and efficiency the dominant boardroom concerns. Design thinking survived the crash because it was already positioned as a growth method, not a cost method, and because its Stanford and IDEO backers were insulated from the immediate financial panic (INTERPRETATION).

## 9. Roger Martin: design thinking inside the business school

Roger Martin, dean of the Rotman School of Management at the University of Toronto from 1998 to 2013, published "The Design of Business: Why Design Thinking Is the Next Competitive Advantage" in 2009 (Harvard Business Press) (BACKGROUND). Martin had spent the prior decade rebuilding Rotman around integrative thinking and design, including a 2004 to 2006 collaboration with IDEO on the school's curriculum and a much-cited 2004 Rotman magazine and conference push around business design (BACKGROUND).

The book's central apparatus is the "knowledge funnel": knowledge moves from mystery (unexplained problems) through heuristic (rules of thumb) to algorithm (codified, repeatable procedure), and competitive advantage comes from either advancing knowledge down the funnel or re-exploring mysteries that rivals have abandoned (BACKGROUND). Martin's recurring case is McDonald's: Ray Kroc converted the mystery of consistent fast food into a heuristic, and successors converted the heuristic into an algorithm, with each stage requiring a different kind of thinking (BACKGROUND).

Martin frames design thinking as abductive reasoning, the logic of what might be, following Charles Sanders Peirce, positioned between deductive and inductive logics (BACKGROUND).

INTERPRETATION: Martin completed the legitimation loop Simon opened. Simon said design belongs in professional schools; Cross and Lawson showed designers have a distinct epistemology; Martin told business schools that epistemology is a competitive asset and gave it a framework (the knowledge funnel) that fits on one slide. The Rotman connection also institutionalized design thinking in management education independently of Stanford, which matters because it shows the pivot was a market movement, not an IDEO monopoly.

## 10. Earlier naming candidates and the phrase's prehistory

L. Bruce Archer's "Systematic Method for Designers" (1965), noted in section 1, is the strongest earlier candidate for a self-conscious design methodology text, and Archer's subsequent career at the Royal College of Art, where he ran the Department of Design Research, made the RCA the movement's institutional stronghold alongside Ulm's Hochschule fur Gestaltung in Germany (BACKGROUND). The Ulm school, founded 1953 by Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher, and Max Bill and closed in 1968, carried its own version of design rationalism, and Rittel taught there before moving to Berkeley, a direct institutional link between the European and American threads (BACKGROUND).

The phrase "design thinking" itself has scattered earlier uses before Rowe's 1987 book. Peter Rowe used the term in working papers in the early 1980s, and variant phrases such as "designerly thinking" appear in British design-research circles in the 1970s and 1980s (BACKGROUND). INTERPRETATION: phrase genealogy matters less here than institutional genealogy. What Rowe's 1987 book fixed was not the words but the referent: design thinking as the name for the cognitive and procedural content of design practice, available for study and eventually for export.

## 11. Where the mapping breaks

INTERPRETATION: The lineage has three strata. First, the 1962 to 1973 British design methods movement and its Berkeley critique (Jones, Alexander, Archer, Rittel, Webber), which ended in the first versus second generation split: methods rationalism versus argumentative design. Second, the 1969 to 1987 epistemological consolidation (Simon, Cross, Schon, Lawson, Rowe), which established design as a legitimate, distinct, empirically studiable way of knowing and gave it its modern name. Third, the 1991 to 2009 commercial translation (IDEO, the d.school, Brown, Martin), which converted the research findings into a business offering and a management bestseller genre.

INTERPRETATION: the deepest fault line is that the business version systematically under-cites the second-generation critique. Rittel's point was that wicked problems resist codified method; the d.school and HBR product is precisely a codified method (empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test) sold for wicked contexts. Critics within design research (notably Nigel Cross himself in later writing, and scholars such as Lucy Kimbell and Kees Dorst in various venues) have noted this flattening; the five-step process is a first-generation artifact wearing second-generation language (BACKGROUND on the existence of this critique; specific citations not verified this session). A second fault line is authorship: the academic tradition is a public, cumulative research record; the business tradition is largely practitioner memoir and case studies from firms that sell the method, so the evidence base for business design thinking is structurally self-interested in a way the design-research literature is not (INTERPRETATION).

INTERPRETATION: The parallel to [[toyota-production-system]] is structural. Both are cases where a practice tradition (Toyota shop floor, design studio) was studied by academics, distilled into a teachable method, then re-exported as management doctrine (lean, design thinking), losing situational depth at each compression. [[lean-startup]] sits downstream of both compressions.

INTERPRETATION: There is also a parallel inside design thinking itself: PDCA's plan-do-check-act cycle and the d.school's empathize-define-ideate-prototype-test loop are both cycles that convert a linear problem-solving pipeline into an iterative learning loop. The difference is epistemic stance. PDCA treats the world as stable enough that each cycle reduces variance against a known standard. The design thinking loop treats the problem frame itself as unstable, so each cycle reframes the problem as much as it improves the solution. This is the same distinction Rittel drew between tame and wicked problems in 1973 (see section 1), and it explains why the two traditions feel similar in practice but diverge in what they consider success.



## 12. Compression points for synthesis

1. The lineage has three strata: the 1962 to 1973 design methods movement and its Berkeley critique (Jones, Alexander, Archer, Rittel, Webber); the 1969 to 1987 epistemological consolidation (Simon, Cross, Schon, Lawson, Rowe); and the 1991 to 2009 commercial translation (IDEO, d.school, Brown, Martin).
2. Rittel and Webber's 1973 wicked-problems paper (Policy Sciences 4:155 to 169, DOI 10.1007/BF01405730, verified) contains the first-generation versus second-generation split that is the hinge of the whole lineage: methods rationalism versus argumentative design.
3. Simon's "The Sciences of the Artificial" (MIT Press, 1969, verified; 2nd ed. 1981, 3rd ed. 1996) supplies the epistemological charter, but Simon's own model was optimization-flavored, closer to Rittel's first generation; design thinking claims Simon's legitimacy while practicing Rittel's epistemology.
4. Peter Rowe's "Design Thinking" (MIT Press, 1987) anchored the phrase; the d.school (founded by Kelley and Roth 2004, inaugurated 3 October 2005, US$35 million from Hasso Plattner, verified) institutionalized it for non-designers.
5. Tim Brown's June 2008 HBR cover article (verified at hbr.org/2008/06/design-thinking) is the single most consequential document in design thinking's business migration.
6. IDEO's 1991 formation merged four firms (David Kelley Design 1978, Moggridge Associates 1969, ID Two 1979, Matrix Product Design 1983, verified via Wikipedia live extract); the common three-firm version is a compression.
7. The deepest fault line: the business version systematically under-cites the second-generation critique. The five-step product is a first-generation artifact wearing second-generation language.
8. Structural parallel to [[toyota-production-system]]: practice tradition studied by academics, distilled into teachable method, re-exported as management doctrine, losing situational depth at each compression.
