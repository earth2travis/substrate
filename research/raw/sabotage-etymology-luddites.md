## 1. The etymology of "sabotage"

**FACT** (verified live against etymonline.com, 2026-09-26): The English noun "sabotage" dates to 1907 (from 1903 as a French word in English), defined then as "malicious damaging or destruction of an employer's property by workmen." It derives from French *sabotage*, from *saboter*, "to sabotage, bungle," literally "to walk noisily," from *sabot*, "wooden shoe."

**FACT** (same source): The famous story, that the word comes from workers throwing their wooden shoes into machinery to jam the gears, is explicitly not supported by the etymology. Etymonline: "the oft-repeated story (as old as the record of the word in English) that the modern meaning derives from strikers' supposed tactic of throwing shoes into machinery is not supported by the etymology. Likely it was not meant as a literal image; the word was used in French in a variety of 'bungling' senses, such as 'to play a piece of music badly.'"

**FACT** (same source): Early documented senses center on deliberately bad work, not destruction. The Liberty Review, January 1907, glossed sabotage as "scamping work... The workman, in other words, purposes to remain on and to do his work badly, so as to annoy his employer's customers and cause loss to his employer." Arturo Giovannitti (1907): "Sabotage consists in going slow with the process of production when the bosses go slow with the same process in regard to wages." The military sense (clandestine destruction to disrupt an enemy) is a World War I extension. The verb form dates to 1912.

**FACT** (same source, deeper root): French *sabot* (13c., a shoe of one piece hollowed out, worn by peasants) was altered by association with Old French *bot*, "boot," from earlier *savate*, "old shoe," ultimately from the same source (perhaps Persian *ciabat*) that produced Spanish *zapata*, Italian *ciabatta*, Arabic *sabbat*, and Basque *zapata*. The bread ciabatta (named c. 1990 for its slipper shape) shares the root.

INTERPRETATION: The folk etymology inverted the word's actual logic. The shoe-throwing story makes sabotage an act of violence against the machine. The documented etymology makes it an act of *presence*: walking noisily, working badly, slowing down while staying on the job. Sabotage-as-recorded is closer to work-to-rule than to frame-breaking. The machine-smashing image won because it is vivid, and because it fits the same caricature that flattened the Luddites into technophobes: resistance as rage against machinery rather than a disciplined labor tactic aimed at the economic relationship the machine embodied.

## 2. The Great Enoch hammers

**FACT** (verified live across multiple independent sources, 2026-09-26): The Luddites' frame-breaking sledgehammers were named "Great Enoch," after Enoch Taylor of Marsden (near Huddersfield), whose firm manufactured both the cropping/shearing frames the croppers destroyed and the hammers they destroyed them with. Sources corroborating: Stuff You Missed in History Class (iHeart, "The Luddites"), the ACM paper "Unmaking as Emancipation: Lessons and Reflections from Luddism" (2023), and documentation of the folk ballad "The Cropper Lads" (Roud TYG 62, 148).

**FACT** (same sources): The ballad itself carries the hammer: "Great Enoch he shall lead the van. / Stop him who dare, stop him who can. / Then forward every gallant man / With hatchet, pike and gun." The hammer is personified as a movement leader alongside the invented General Ludd.

INTERPRETATION: The Enoch detail is the sharpest irony in the whole Luddite record. The same manufacturer profited from the frame that deskilled the cropper and the hammer that destroyed the frame. The tool of resistance was a product of the same industrial supply chain as the tool of displacement. That is a cleaner capsule of the surplus-capture argument in the parent node than any historian's formulation: the mill economy sold both the problem and the protest.

## 3. Why these two artifacts belong together

INTERPRETATION: Both artifacts show how machine-era labor resistance gets misremembered in the same direction. The Luddites become technophobes; sabotage becomes shoe-throwing. In both cases the remembered version centers the *machine* (smashing it, jamming it), and in both cases the documented version centers the *relationship*: who is paid, who owns the surplus, who has standing. The folk versions are more cinematic and less threatening, because a story about people hating machines never has to answer the question the real actors were asking, which was about distribution. This is the same flattening the parent node documents in Hobsbawm's "collective bargaining by riot" correction, extended into etymology and material culture.

## 4. Compression points for synthesis

1. "Sabotage" (English, 1907) derives from *saboter*, "to walk noisily / bungle," from *sabot*, wooden shoe. Documented early senses are deliberately bad or slow work, not destruction; the shoe-in-the-gears story is a folk image the etymology does not support (etymonline, verified live).
2. The military/destruction sense of sabotage is a World War I extension; the verb dates to 1912. The labor sense came first.
3. The Luddites' sledgehammers were named "Great Enoch" after Enoch Taylor, whose firm made both the frames and the hammers: the resistance's tool came from the same supply chain as the displacing machine (multiple independent sources, verified live).
4. The folk ballad "The Cropper Lads" personifies the hammer as a movement leader ("Great Enoch he shall lead the van"), pairing a real manufacturer's name with the invented General Ludd.
5. Both artifacts get misremembered toward the machine and away from the economic relationship, which is the same flattening as the Luddite-as-technophobe caricature. The pattern of misremembering is itself the finding: distribution questions get laundered into machine-hate stories.
