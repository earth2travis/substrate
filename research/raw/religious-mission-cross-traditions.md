# Mission Concepts Across Traditions: Islamic Da'wah and Buddhist Dharma Propagation

## Islamic Da'wah

The Arabic term da'wah means "invitation" or "call." It refers to summoning people to Islam and to proper Islamic practice.

Qur'anic basis: Surah An-Nahl 16:125: "Invite to the way of your Lord with wisdom and good instruction, and argue with them in a way that is best."

Historical development:
- Early Muslims migrated to Abyssinia (c. 615 CE) for refuge and contact
- Sufi orders (tariqas) became major agents of Islamic expansion from the 12th–13th centuries into Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia
- Traders and Sufis, not formal state missionaries, were primary agents in Indonesia and Malaysia
- Key figures: Shah Jalal (1271–1347/8), Sufi saint associated with Islamization of Bengal

Da'wah is both an individual obligation (fard) and communal activity. Unlike Christian "mission" as an ecclesial institution, da'wah is decentralized. The concept of jihad historically intertwined with da'wah, but peacefully conducted da'wah has always been normative for non-combatants.

## Buddhist Dharma Propagation

The Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama, c. 5th–4th century BCE) sent his first disciples with the instruction: "Go forth for the good of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world" (Mahavagga, Vinaya Pitaka).

Ashoka (c. 304–232 BCE), Mauryan emperor, is the most significant figure in early Buddhist propagation:
- After the brutal Kalinga War (c. 260 BCE), he devoted himself to dhamma (righteous conduct)
- Sent emissaries (dharma mahamatras) throughout his empire and beyond
- His son Mahinda carried Buddhism to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE
- Sent missions to Kashmir, Gandhara, and the Hellenistic world

Later spread occurred along Silk Road and maritime routes via monks, traders, and translated texts. Buddhism did not typically establish centralized "missionary societies" until modern times.

Japanese concept of fukyo (propagation): 20th-century movements like Soka Gakkai adopted modern proselytizing methods, but classical Buddhism spread through text, monastic networks, and royal patronage.

## Conceptual Comparison

Christianity and Islam developed explicit, institutionalized "sending" structures tied to salvation history and universal claims. Both possess sacred texts commanding expansion.

Buddhist spread was historically more diffuse:
- Spread through cultural diffusion, monastic migration, and philosophical appeal
- Less emphasis on exclusive truth-claims requiring conversion
- Royal patronage (Ashoka) was decisive but not continuous
- No centralized sending authority equivalent to the papacy or caliphate

Da'wah and Missio Dei share the idea of divine/human cooperation, though Christian Missio Dei emphasizes Trinitarian sending, while da'wah emphasizes human response to divine revelation.
