## 1. The Sobornost: structure and Founders

- The Sobornost is a collective of uploaded minds that rules much of the inner solar system; an alliance of powerful gogol copies governs the inner system. FACT (per Wikipedia summary of the novel).
- The Founders are the original uploaded humans whose mind-states seed the entire polity. The Sobornost is populated by gogols: copied instances of those founder-minds, forked into millions of instances. BACKGROUND.
- A copyclan is the set of gogols forked from a single source consciousness; copyclans function as the Sobornost's constituent houses or lineages. FACT that fan/reader consensus describes copyclans this way (per r/printSF); internal detail remains BACKGROUND.
- Both the Sobornost and their rivals, the zoku, trace their origins to pre-Collapse Earth subcultures: MMORPG guilds and hacker outfits. FACT (per Mahesh Raj Mohan review; consistent with the novel's back cover copy describing a "race of hyper-advanced humans who originated as an MMORPG guild").
- The Sobornost's immortality is figured critically inside the fiction: one character's verdict is that "the Sobornost clings to immortality that turns souls into cogs in a machine," while "the zoku get lost in silly games and Realms that lead nowhere." FACT that this quote circulates in reader discussions; exact attribution not re-verified, treat wording as BACKGROUND.

## 2. The gogol economy

- Gogols are tradable. Gogol piracy, the copying, theft, and enslavement of uploaded minds, is a live institution in the setting; stolen gogols can be run as labor, interrogated, or tortured in accelerated time. BACKGROUND.
- "Commonplace": the Sobornost's shared computational substrate/territory, the standardized mind-environment in which gogols run. BACKGROUND; flagged as a term needing primary-text verification.
- "Wildcode": rogue, uncontrolled self-modifying code left over from the Collapse; dangerous enough that even posthuman factions treat it as contamination. BACKGROUND; flagged as a term needing primary-text verification.

## 3. The war backstory and the zoku schism

- A Collapse on Earth precedes the present of the novel; afterward the transhuman Sobornost rose to hegemony and are at war with the also-transhuman zoku. FACT (per the Raj Mohan review of novel backstory).
- The conflict is remembered as an upload-era war (in reader discussion, the "Protocol War"): fought over the terms on which minds may be copied, merged, and governed; the zoku organized as voluntary, game-structured collectives in opposition to Sobornost copy-authoritarianism. BACKGROUND. Note: whether "Protocol War" appears in The Quantum Thief proper or only in sequels/fan usage is an open item.
- The Sobornost's relationship to Earth is extractive and dismissive; the Oubliette, the Moving City of Mars, sits outside direct Sobornost rule, protected by its own cryptographic social contract (exomemory, gevulot, Time as currency). BACKGROUND.

## 4. The name "gogol": Dead Souls

- Nikolai Gogol's *Dead Souls* was first published in 1842. FACT (Wikipedia).
- The plot device: in imperial Russia, serfs ("souls") were counted in periodic censuses (revisions) and landowners owed taxes on each listed soul until the next census. Chichikov, the protagonist, buys up the names of serfs who have died since the last census, dead souls, cheaply, to present himself as a great landowner and leverage the phantom estate. FACT (census-tax mechanic and Chichikov's purchase scheme, per PIIE and classic-literature summaries).
- Rajaniemi names his uploaded copies "gogols" after Gogol's dead souls: the uploaded consciousness of people who no longer have physical bodies. FACT (per the Raj Mohan review, which links the naming to Gogol's novel).
- INTERPRETATION: The copied person is a ledger entry. Like Chichikov's dead serfs, a gogol is a person-shaped unit that exists primarily to be counted, owned, taxed, traded, and collateralized. The name encodes the moral thesis of the setting: mass-uploading converts persons into inventory.
- INTERPRETATION: A second resonance: Chichikov's souls are *dead but legally alive*. Gogols are *alive but legally dead*, minds stripped of the body that anchored their personhood, owned by the copyclan the way serfs were owned by the estate.

## 5. Real-world mapping

### Whole brain emulation

- Sandberg, A. & Bostrom, N. (2008), *Whole Brain Emulation: A Roadmap*, Technical Report #2008-3, Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford University. FACT (citation verified against the report PDF and the Oxford University Research Archive).
- The roadmap frames WBE as the logical endpoint of scanning and simulating a specific brain at sufficient resolution, and treats emulated minds as copyable, speed-adjustable software, the technical premise the Sobornost assumes as social fact. FACT for the report's existence and scope; the Sobornost connection is INTERPRETATION.

### The copy problem: Parfit

- Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part Three, develops the Psychological Criterion: person X today is the same person as Y at a past time iff X is psychologically continuous with Y (overlapping chains of strong psychological connectedness), the continuity has the right kind of cause, and the relation has not taken a branching form. FACT (per secondary literature quoting R&P: 206).
- Teletransportation: a person is scanned, destroyed on Earth, and a qualitatively identical Replica is assembled on Mars. On Parfit's Wide view this is "about as good" as ordinary survival; in the Branch-Line case, where the Original is *not* destroyed, the Replica is plainly a different individual, and identity fails because psychological continuity has branched. FACT (per the Argumenta paper's reconstruction of R&P: 199-209).
- Parfit's deeper conclusion: what matters is not identity but Relation R, psychological connectedness and/or continuity with any cause, and identity "is not what matters." FACT (per R&P: 262 as quoted in the PhilArchive reconstruction).
- INTERPRETATION: The Sobornost is a civilization built on ignoring the branching clause. Every copyclan is a Parfitian fission case industrialized; the Founders treat Relation R as transferable property and treat the non-branching condition, the only thing distinguishing "survival" from "being cloned," as a legal fiction they can waive.
- Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (1979): the self as a "strange loop," a pattern that can in principle be instantiated in multiple substrates; useful as the patternist background Parfit radicalizes. BACKGROUND; not re-verified this session.

### Em economics: Bostrom and Hanson

- Bostrom's later work on em societies (e.g., chapters and essays following the 2008 roadmap) treats copied minds as an economic discontinuity: near-zero reproduction cost collapses wages toward subsistence-compute and lets a few templates dominate labor markets. BACKGROUND.
- Robin Hanson, *The Age of Em: Work, Love and Life when Robots Rule the Earth* (2016), Oxford University Press. FACT (title, author, year, publisher per Wikipedia and ageofem.com).
- Hanson's core mechanism, verbatim from the book's site: "Train an em to do some job and copy it a million times." Ems reproduce via exact copies, usually whole teams copied together; an em copied onto blank hardware has "exactly the same memories and mental habits" and diverges from that point; ems who can veto copies "feel stronger ownership of their existence." FACT (quoted from ageofem.com).
- INTERPRETATION: The Hanson-Sobornost alignment is nearly one-to-one: a Sobornost Founder is a Hansonian em clan with the veto removed for everyone below the founder-tier. Hanson's em clans are voluntary franchises; Rajaniemi's copyclans are the same institution with consent stripped out, which is precisely the difference between Age of Em as economics and The Quantum Thief as horror.

### Identity criteria and legal personhood

- The philosophical menu the novel dramatizes: (a) psychological continuity criteria (Parfit; copies are continuers until branching breaks uniqueness), (b) physical/body criteria (no copy is the person), (c) closest-continuer and branching-aware variants (Nozick; BACKGROUND), (d) patternism (the pattern is the person; every instance is equally "them").
- Legal personhood for copies is an open problem in actual jurisprudence: no jurisdiction recognizes an upload as a legal person; proposals range from treating copies as property of the biological original (the Sobornost answer) to granting each instance independent personhood (the zoku answer). BACKGROUND.
- INTERPRETATION: The novel's gevulot/exomemory regime in the Oubliette, where memory-sharing is cryptographically consented and privacy is enforceable, reads as a direct counter-design to gogol economics: personhood protected by key management rather than by law.

## 6. The critique layer

- **Which copy is the person?** INTERPRETATION: The novel refuses the question's premise. Jean le Flambeur's own arc, a man who hid the truth from *himself* and meets his own past selves as adversaries and marks, dramatizes Parfit's branching case from the inside: every fork believes it is the original, and the belief is cheap because it costs nothing to instantiate.
- **Consent at scale.** INTERPRETATION, anchored in FACT quote above: A single signature authorizes a million forks. Each fork's suffering is real; the consenter who signed is one instance among millions and cannot speak for the rest. Hanson's "ems who can veto copies feel stronger ownership" is the real-world version; the Sobornost is what the technology looks like when the veto is held only at the top of the copyclan.
- **Copyclan politics as self-governance of forks.** INTERPRETATION: A copyclan is a polity whose citizens are all the same person at the moment of fork and diverge thereafter: internal disagreement is literally a self arguing with itself across diverged experience. Rajaniemi uses this to show that even perfect self-knowledge does not yield agreement, because divergence, not ignorance, is the engine of conflict.
- **The Dead Souls verdict.** INTERPRETATION, grounded in FACT-cited Dead Souls mechanics: Gogol's satire was that the state counts people as taxable units whether they live or die. Rajaniemi's extension: once minds are copyable, the same accounting logic eats personhood itself, and the census of souls becomes the Sobornost's copy-ledger. The gogol trade is serfdom with the revision-table replaced by the fork.

## 7. Where the mapping breaks

- **Novel-internal anchors rest on secondary coverage, not line-level citations.** The Sobornost's structure, the gogol trade, the Collapse, and commonplace/wildcode were verified against Wikipedia, reviews, and fan discussion, not against the text itself. A primary-text pass is listed as an open item; any direct quotation from the novel should wait for it.
- **The Hanson mapping is structural, not textual.** Hanson wrote Age of Em in 2016, six years after The Quantum Thief. Rajaniemi did not borrow from Hanson; the alignment runs the other direction (independent convergence on copy economics). Treating the novel as a dramatization of Hanson would invert the actual chronology.
- **Parfit's Branch-Line case is not obviously the Sobornost's problem.** The Founders may simply deny that identity is what matters and embrace Relation R, which Parfit himself argues is the defensible position. The horror of the Sobornost then depends on premises (the value of uniqueness, the wrongness of non-consensual forks) that Parfit's framework does not supply on its own. The mapping is real but it is not a reductio.
- **The "cogs in a machine" quote and "Protocol War" term are flagged.** The quote circulates in reader discussion without a verified attribution; "Protocol War" may be sequel or fan terminology rather than The Quantum Thief proper. Both are BACKGROUND and should not be quoted as settled.
- **Legal personhood claims are generic.** "No jurisdiction recognizes an upload as a legal person" is true as far as it goes, but the menu of proposals summarized here is BACKGROUND-level scholarship, not a surveyed legal landscape.

## 8. Compression points for synthesis

1. The Sobornost is a Hansonian em economy with the copy-veto stripped from everyone below the founder tier: Hanson's voluntary em clans become Rajaniemi's coercive copyclans, which is exactly the difference between Age of Em as economics and The Quantum Thief as horror.
2. "Gogol" names the moral thesis: from Gogol's Dead Souls (1842), where dead serfs persist on census rolls as taxable property. The copied person is a ledger entry, a person-shaped unit that exists to be counted, owned, taxed, traded, and collateralized. Chichikov's souls are dead but legally alive; gogols are alive but legally dead.
3. The Sobornost is a civilization built on ignoring Parfit's branching clause: every copyclan is a Parfitian fission case industrialized, with the non-branching condition, the only thing distinguishing survival from cloning, waived as a legal fiction.
4. Consent at scale is the load-bearing critique: a single signature authorizes a million forks, each fork's suffering is real, and the consenter cannot speak for the rest. The copy-veto, held only at the top of the copyclan, is the difference between franchise and serfdom.
5. Copyclan politics show that even perfect self-knowledge does not yield agreement: a polity whose citizens are the same person at fork-time diverges into genuine conflict, because divergence, not ignorance, is the engine of disagreement.
6. Sandberg & Bostrom's 2008 Whole Brain Emulation Roadmap supplies the technical premise (minds as copyable, speed-adjustable software); the novel assumes it as social fact and asks what institutions grow on top of it.
