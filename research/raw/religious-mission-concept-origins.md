# Origins of Mission in the New Testament: Apostolos and the Great Commission

## The Greek Root: Apostolos

The Greek word apostolos means "one who is sent," from apostellein ("to send away"). In the New Testament, this becomes the primary term for those commissioned by Jesus to spread his teachings. The Twelve Apostles and the Seventy-Two (Luke 10) are the first explicitly "sent" ones.

## The Great Commission

Matthew 28:16-20 records Jesus instructing his disciples: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." This passage became the theological foundation for the Christian concept of mission as a universal obligation.

## Paul as the Paradigmatic Missionary

Paul the Apostle (c. 5 – c. 64/65 AD), though not one of the Twelve, became the most influential missionary figure in Christian history. He traveled through Asia Minor and Europe, founding Christian communities from the mid-40s to the mid-50s AD. His letters (epistles) constitute approximately half of the New Testament.

Key aspects of Paul's mission theology:
- Romans 10:15: "How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news"
- He saw himself as specifically "sent to the Gentiles" (non-Jews), creating a theology of universal mission beyond ethnic Israel
- His mission was authenticated by suffering and signs (2 Corinthians 11)
- He established communities, then moved on, trusting local leadership to sustain them

## Conceptual Foundation

In the earliest Christian era, mission was not a separate institution. It was intrinsic to the Church's existence. The Church did not have a mission; the Church was the mission, understood as the gathering of all nations into the Kingdom of God. There was no specialized "missionary" role separate from apostleship and local church leadership.
