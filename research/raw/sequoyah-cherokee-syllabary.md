# Sequoyah and the Cherokee Syllabary: Indigenous Literacy from the Inside

## The Man

Sequoyah (c. 1770–1843), also known as George Gist or George Guess, was a Cherokee polymath and neographer. He was not a missionary. He was Cherokee. This is crucial: his work was indigenous invention, not foreign imposition.

## The Invention

In 1821, Sequoyah completed his Cherokee syllabary. It is one of the few times in recorded history that a member of a pre-literate society created an original, effective writing system.

The syllabary had 85 characters (later reduced to 86), each representing a syllable of spoken Cherokee. Sequoyah reportedly spent years working on it, even though his peers initially thought he was practicing witchcraft.

## The Impact

Within a quarter-century, the Cherokee Nation had reached near-universal literacy, surpassing the literacy rates of surrounding European-American settlers. In 1825, the Cherokee Nation officially adopted the syllabary. In 1828, the Cherokee Phoenix became the first newspaper published by Native Americans in the United States, printed in both English and Cherokee using types cast by missionary Samuel Worcester.

The syllabary unified a forcibly divided nation. It gave the Cherokee a tool for political organization, legal documentation, and cultural preservation under pressure of removal.

## The Structural Point

Unlike most missionary linguistics, which is top-down (foreign missionary creates writing system for indigenous people), Sequoyah's work was bottom-up. The Cherokee *owned* their literacy. This made it politically potent in ways that missionary-created scripts rarely were.

## The Shadow: Sequoyah's syllabary is believed to have inspired the development of 21 scripts or writing systems used in 65 languages across North America, Africa, and Asia.
