# Nigel Howard's Metagame Theory, and Its Afterlife

Raw research file, single-concept deep dive. Produced 2026-11-23. The concept is the metagame: a solution concept built in the 1960s and 1970s by the British mathematician and strategic analyst Nigel Howard, which the Substrate met while the operator read William Poundstone, Prisoner's Dilemma (1992, Anchor paperback 1993), and which Poundstone introduces in three pages at pp. 225 to 227.

Scope. This file covers what a metagame is, how Howard built it, what he claimed for it, its place in the lineage that runs to superrationality, program equilibrium and team reasoning, and honestly, why mainstream economics ignored it. It does not re-derive the prisoner's dilemma's formalism (that is [[prisoners-dilemma-anatomy]]), Axelrod and Tit for Tat (that is [[iterated-prisoners-dilemma-axelrod-tournaments]]), the experimental psychology (that is [[prisoners-dilemma-psychology-coordination]]), or the crypto and AI application layer (that is [[faustian-bargain-prisoners-dilemma-crypto-ai]]). The fact that the 2x2 family (chicken, stag hunt, deadlock) is one game under payoff permutations is already stated in prisoners-dilemma-anatomy.md and is not repeated here. The word "meta-game" already appears once informally in prisoners-dilemma-psychology-coordination.md, applied to altruistic punishment; that informal use is not the technical concept below and should not be read as coverage.

## 1. What a metagame is, and the construction, the heart of this file

- [FACT] The construction, stated plainly: a metagame augments the original game by letting each player choose among conditional strategies. A conditional strategy is a policy that specifies what the player will do in response to every strategy the other player might pick. The unit of choice is therefore no longer an action but an intention, a whole contingency plan. The metagame then takes the Nash equilibria of this enlarged game, and among these equilibria are outcomes that maximize joint payoff. That is the sense in which the metagame is said to dissolve the prisoner's dilemma rather than merely restate it. (This formulation is the task brief's, and it matches the worked construction in the source below.)

- [FACT] The clearest contemporaneous statement of the construction is Martin Shubik's, in Cowles Foundation Discussion Paper No. 274, "Game Theory, Behavior and the Paradox of the Prisoners' Dilemma: Three Solutions," Yale University, 2 May 1969, section 3.3, titled "Metagames." Shubik writes: "Howard's solution for the Prisoners' Dilemma is based upon a concept of 'metagame' which allows several levels of conditional strategies." (https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1506&context=cowles-discussion-paper-series)

- [FACT] The concrete build, in Shubik's arithmetic. Take the prisoner's dilemma. Instead of two alternatives for the second player, Howard gives the second player four: (1) play alternative 1 regardless; (2) play 1 or 2 according to whether you think the first player will play 1 or 2; (3) play 1 or 2 according to the opposite guess; (4) play alternative 2 regardless. The first player, knowing this larger choice problem faces the second, now has sixteen alternatives, written as strings such as 1111, 1112, 1121, 1211, 1122, and so on, encoding "play 1 regardless" through "play 2 regardless." A 16 x 4 matrix results. Shubik: "there are now three equilibrium points, two of which are jointly optimal and one is the old jointly unsatisfactory (0, 0)." (same URL)

- [FACT] Two names for the four conditional strategies recur in the secondary textbook literature. Steven Brams's "Game Theory and Politics" prints Figure 1.13, "Metagame Payoff Matrix for Prisoner's Dilemma," with the second player's four rows labelled as unconditional and conditional intentions, the coordinate ones glossed as "Tit-for-tat" and "Tat-for-tit" style policies. (https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~fczagare/PSC%20346/GameTheoryAndPolitics.pdf) [INTERPRETATION] The labels matter: what is being chosen is a response rule, the same species of object as a Tit for Tat program, but chosen before play rather than executed during it. That is the kinship to the iterated literature, arrived at from a different direction.

- [FACT] Shubik draws out the reading of the four strategies that Howard intends. The four strategies of the second player "divide into two types: 1 and 4 are 'inner-directed' whereas 2 and 3 are 'other-directed.' Strategy 2 is a statement of an intent to coordinate. Strategy 3 conveys the intent to avoid coordination." (same URL) [INTERPRETATION] This is the cleanest statement anywhere of what makes the metagame a distinct concept: it is not a new payoff matrix, it is a formalization of intent. The player's move is a declaration of what they intend to do in each contingency, and intentions of the coordinate-me and avoid-me kind are precisely what the one-shot dilemma leaves unspecified.

- [FACT] Shubik's one-sentence characterization of the object: "The metagame can be regarded as an attempt to build complete plausibility for a restricted set of intents within an extension of the usual Prisoners' Dilemma game." (same URL)

- [FACT] The regressions terminate. Shubik: "Howard shows that there is nothing more to be gained by going to the higher regressions of the metagame." (same URL) [INTERPRETATION] The name "metagame" is earned by this: a metagame is a game about the game, the players reasoning not about actions but about each other's conditional policies, "thinking about thinking." Howard's result is that one level of this remove is enough to produce the jointly optimal equilibria, and further levels add nothing.

- [FACT] The metagame is an extension of the model, not a new theorem about the old one. Shubik's summary of the whole class of moves applies: "The 'resolution' or 'solving' of the Prisoners' Dilemma came about through the enlarging of the model rather than the invocation of new mathematical properties of the original model." (same URL) [INTERPRETATION] Read against Poundstone's line that "Fundamentally, game theory is about abstract problems of maximization" (p. 225): Howard's move accepts the maximizing frame and enlarges it, adding a layer of intentions rather than rejecting the maximizing player.

- [FACT] Shubik reports an empirical note on the effect: "Experimental evidence does however indicate that with this type of strategy enlargement ... the degree of cooperation is increased considerably." The footnote behind that clause points to Emshoff's 1968 dissertation, cited elsewhere in the same paper. (same URL) [UNVERIFIED] The Emshoff study itself was not read; the claim is recorded as Shubik's report, not as an independently checked finding.

- [INTERPRETATION] What the metagame is not: it is not the iterated game. The repeated-play resolution appears in Shubik's same paper as a separate solution and needs no intentions at all, only the shadow of future retaliation. The metagame reaches cooperation in the one-shot game by changing the unit of choice. That distinction is the whole reason it earns a node beside [[iterated-prisoners-dilemma-axelrod-tournaments]]: same destination, opposite mechanism, repetition versus reframing.

### 1.1 The metagame as the third of three solutions Shubik surveys

- [FACT] Shubik frames the problem as three ways to resolve the dilemma while keeping two constraints fixed, "communication takes place only through seeing the moves played" and "no enforceable agreements." The three are "the Metagames of Nigel Howard, a treatment of the repeated Prisoners' Dilemma as a game of infinite length and the Games of Economic Survival or Social Survival of Shubik." (https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1506&context=cowles-discussion-paper-series)

- [FACT] Shubik ranks them by how much extra machinery each needs: "The infinite game modification involves the least extra modeling; while Shubik's calls for an additional economic assumption and Howard's for an additional socio-psychological assumption." (same URL)

- [FACT] His verdict on the pure infinite game is also a warning about the whole strategy of widening the solution set: "the conversion of every outcome that satisfies individual rationality ... into an equilibrium point leaves something to be desired and points to a weakness in the model itself." (same URL) [INTERPRETATION] The same warning applies to the metagame. Widening the solution set until cooperation is inside it helps only if a selection principle is supplied too. Howard's theory is one of the earliest instances of the pattern, and an insider flagged the pattern in 1969, which is a large part of why the concept did not take root in the field.

## 2. Howard's own examples and claims

- [BACKGROUND] Howard's central written statement is Nigel Howard, Paradoxes of Rationality: Theory of Metagames and Political Behavior, MIT Press, Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1971. (Cited with that subtitle and publisher by Zagare's 2014 history of the Cuban missile crisis, https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7099/2/1/20, by the Buffalo/Brams course PDF above, and by the bibliographies in the other verified sources below.)

- [FACT] The subtitle is the claim. The book is not titled a theory of games but a theory of "metagames and political behavior." The project is explicitly normative and political: a solution concept for games where what a player really chooses is an intention about how to respond, and where the intended application is political conflict rather than abstract 2x2 practice. (Title and subtitle verified via the sources below; the interior of the book was not read this session.)

- [FACT] A title variant exists and should be flagged so nobody treats a second book as a phantom: the Wikipedia game-theory reference list renders the 1971 book as "Paradoxes of Rationality: Games, Metagames, and Political Behavior" (ISBN 978-0-262-58237-7), where the analytic sources render it "Theory of Metagames and Political Behavior." (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_theory) [INTERPRETATION] Treat them as one work; the discrepancy is a citation variant, not two titles.

- [FACT] Howard's earlier statement of the theory is a journal article: Nigel Howard, "The Theory of Meta-Games," General Systems, vol. 11, pp. 167 to 186, 1966. Shubik's 1969 bibliography gives exactly this reference, which datapoints the metagame to 1966, five years before the book. (https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1506&context=cowles-discussion-paper-series)

- [FACT] Howard's claim, and how it was received by name, from Shubik: "Rapaport regards this work as 'the resolution' of the Prisoners' Dilemma; although I fail to see why." (same URL) The Rapoport item behind the phrase is Anatol Rapoport, "Escape from Paradox," Scientific American, vol. 217, no. 1, pp. 50 to 59, July 1967. (same URL, bibliography)

- [INTERPRETATION] So the claim was strong and it was contested at once. Howard's metagame is presented as a dissolution of the dilemma, one of the three "solutions" Shubik surveys (the other two being the infinite repeated game and Shubik's own games of social survival). Shubik ranks the three by how much extra modeling each needs: the infinite game needs the least, and "Howard's for an additional socio-psychological assumption." That ranking is the first recorded verdict that the metagame buys its result with an assumption about how people think, not with a theorem.

- [BACKGROUND] Howard's later work moved the same project from metagames to "drama theory" and to "confrontation analysis," developed with Peter Bennett. The reference-work chapter "From Game Theory to Drama Theory" exists and lists Howard (1971) in its bibliography, tracing the line from the metagame to drama theory. (Existence and citation read from the search result at https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-030-12051-1_14-4 ; the chapter body could not be fetched, the fetch returned Payment Required.) The applied book is Nigel Howard and Peter G. Bennett, Confrontation Analysis: How to Win Operations Other Than War, CCRP, 1999. [BACKGROUND, supplied by the task brief, not re-verified this session.]

- [BACKGROUND] The bridge text between the two phases is Peter G. Bennett, "Modelling Decisions in International Relations: Game Theory and Beyond" (1995), whose reference list carries Howard (1971) and a joint Howard, Bennett, Bryant and Bradley item. (https://www.jstor.org/stable/222691) [INTERPRETATION] The 1995 Bennett paper is the visible hinge: the metagame concept migrating out of game theory proper and into the operational and international-relations literature.

- [INTERPRETATION] The naming is precise, not marketing. "Meta" is the Greek for "after" or "about"; a metagame is a game about the game, the same construction by which a metalanguage is a language about a language. Howard's object really is a game one level up, whose moves are the original game's contingency plans, which is why the higher regressions in Shubik's account are the same kind of step repeated.

- [BACKGROUND] Howard's institutional setting. He worked as a strategic analyst in the same postwar generation as the RAND theorists and built the metagame to speak to the same questions, deterrence, crisis bargaining and political decision, from a British operational-research vantage rather than an American economics one. [INTERPRETATION] That vantage helps explain the concept's afterlife: it travelled where operational analysis and conflict studies were practised, and not into the economics journals.

- [INTERPRETATION] A plain-language rendering, for synthesis use. The metagame asks each player to answer, before play, "whatever you do, here is what I will do." It then shows that if both answer with coordinating intentions, the jointly best outcome becomes a stable answer. It is a formalization of the intuition that in a dilemma what matters is not the single move but the standing intention behind it.

## 3. The lineage, and where the metagame sits in it

- [INTERPRETATION] The metagame belongs to a family of moves, all of which change the object of choice from an action to something larger, in order to reach a jointly optimal equilibrium that the plain game cannot reach. Read them as siblings:

- [BACKGROUND] Hofstadter's superrationality. Douglas R. Hofstadter's columns on the prisoner's dilemma and its computer tournaments, collected in Metamagical Themas: Questing for the Essence of Mind and Pattern (Basic Books, 1985), introduced superrationality: the idea that a player who knows the opponent is reasoning identically, from the same problem, should regard the two of them as effectively one deciding agent and choose the action that is best if both do it, which yields cooperation. [The task brief supplies the source; it was not re-verified this session.] [INTERPRETATION] Superrationality reaches cooperation by symmetry of reasoners; the metagame reaches it by enlarging what each player chooses. Same destination, different mechanism: Hofstadter needs identical agents and a single reasoning shortcut, Howard needs only that intentions, not actions, be the units of choice.

- [FACT] Tennenholtz's program equilibrium. Moshe Tennenholtz, "Program equilibrium," Games and Economic Behavior, vol. 49, no. 2, November 2004, pp. 363 to 373. The abstract states the result directly: "in a program equilibrium of the one-shot prisoners dilemma mutual cooperation is obtained." Each player submits a program rather than a move, the programs are common knowledge, and each program best-responds to the others. (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/gamebe/v49y2004i2p363-373.html and https://cris.technion.ac.il/en/publications/program-equilibrium/) [INTERPRETATION] This is the metagame's most direct modern heir and its sharpest form: where Howard's player chooses a contingency plan over the other's strategies, Tennenholtz's player literally chooses the source code of a response function. Program equilibrium made the intention-as-strategy move rigorous by making "what I will do given what you submit" a computable object with a fixed point. It is the clearest line from Howard 1966 to the multi-agent literature.

- [FACT] Bacharach's team reasoning, or "we-thinking." Michael Bacharach, Beyond Individual Choice: Teams and Frames in Game Theory, Princeton University Press, 2006, edited posthumously by Natalie Gold and Robert Sugden. The book argues that individuals can reason as members of a team, asking what "we" should do, and that this team-mode reasoning explains cooperation that individual-mode reasoning cannot. (https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691120058/beyond-individual-choice ; the secondary literature on the conditions for team reasoning, for example https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5486527/) [INTERPRETATION] Bacharach relocates the metagame's move from the payoff matrix to the frame: the player does not choose an intention over the other's strategies so much as adopt a collective agency under which the unit that chooses is the team. It is the same dissolution of the dilemma by changing what the choosing subject is.

- [BACKGROUND] Sugden's related work. Robert Sugden's The Economics of Rights, Co-operation and Welfare (1986) gives an evolutionary and convention-based account of how cooperation and property norms arise without a central enforcer, and his later work with Natalie Gold, "Collective Intentions and Team Agency" (2006 working version; published in the Journal of Philosophy, 2007), develops the team-reasoning line alongside Bacharach. [Supplied by the task brief and read only as search metadata this session; not re-verified.] [INTERPRETATION] Sugden is the empirical and evolutionary wing of the same family: where Howard asserts a solution concept, Sugden asks what practices could make team reasoning stable and common.

- [INTERPRETATION] The shape of the lineage in one line: Howard (1966, 1971) choose an intention; Hofstadter (1985) reason symmetrically with a twin; Tennenholtz (2004) submit a program; Bacharach (2006) reason as a team. Four different levers, all trying to make the jointly optimal outcome an equilibrium for rational agents without an external enforcer.

- [INTERPRETATION] A naming caution for the Substrate. "Meta-game" now carries at least three distinct senses in this graph: Howard's technical conditional-strategy construction (this file), the informal use in [[prisoners-dilemma-psychology-coordination]] for altruistic punishment, and the ordinary-language sense of the social maneuvering around a stated game. When synthesis uses the phrase it should say which sense it means, because only the first is Howard's and only the first yields the intention-based dissolution of the one-shot dilemma.

## 5. Reception and the honest limits

- [INTERPRETATION] Metagame theory was largely ignored by mainstream economics and game theory. The evidence is internal to the record: Shubik, an insider, surveys it in 1969 and declines to endorse it ("although I fail to see why" it should be called a resolution), calls it "mathematically interesting" but says it "by no means offers a general resolution to the problem of threats or to the many problems concerning strategies, signalling, communication and coding"; and thereafter it does not appear in the core journals' solution-concept literature. The equilibrium concept that the field canonized was Nash's, refined by Selten and Harsanyi, not Howard's larger game.

- [INTERPRETATION] Its afterlife ran through operational analysis, political science and policing rather than through economics. Brams carried the metagame into a political-science textbook (the metagame payoff matrix for the prisoner's dilemma survives there), Zagare records Howard as part of the Cuban missile crisis modeling history, Bennett carries it into international-relations modeling, and the line ends in Howard and Bennett's confrontation analysis, explicitly written for winning "operations other than war," that is for peacekeeping, policing and low-intensity conflict. The metagame is a real lineage, and its home was the applied and security-studies world, not the theory journals.

- [INTERPRETATION] Why economics did not adopt it, more precisely. Three reasons, each visible in the verified record. First, its equilibria are not selected uniquely, so it does not answer the question the field treats as central, "which outcome is the relevant one." Second, its primitive is an intention, not a payoff, which sits uneasily inside the revealed-preference program the field consolidated in the following decades. Third, its load-bearing assumption is socio-psychological, about framing, which Shubik already named as the extra assumption it buys. It is answerable to "how should a player think about the opponent," a question mainstream game theory treats as peripheral, and not to the equilibrium-selection question it treats as central.

- [INTERPRETATION] Its survival in security and policing studies is a survival of the vocabulary rather than of the theorem. Confrontation analysis imports the metagame's grammar, intents, responses, positions, and uses it to structure a negotiation or an insurgency; it drops the equilibrium apparatus. That is a real and useful afterlife, and it is not a survival in the discipline that owns the prisoner's dilemma's mathematics.

- [BACKGROUND] The bibliographic trace of its currency is thin and applied. It is cited in reference-work summaries (the Springer "From Game Theory to Drama Theory" chapter), in political-science game-theory texts, and in a scattering of modeling papers; it is not cited as a live solution concept in mainstream microeconomics. [INTERPRETATION] Report this plainly rather than inflating it: Howard built a genuine and original solution concept, it was taken up where formal conflict analysis was being applied to real political confrontations, and it was not adopted by the discipline that owns the prisoner's dilemma's formalism.

- [INTERPRETATION] What was left in the language. The modern, informal sense of a "metagame" as the layer of play about the play, in games, in markets, in social strategy, descends from competitive-gaming usage more than from Howard, and it should not be confused with his technical object. Howard's metagame is a solution concept; a "metagame" in current slang is a strategy layer.

- [INTERPRETATION] The one place Howard's precise sense survives intact, usually without the citation, is wherever a choice is made over conditional policies rather than over actions: mechanism design with menus of contingent contracts, and modern agent programs that commit to a policy rather than a single call, are doing Howard's move in other words. Program equilibrium is the named descendant; the unnamed descendants are everywhere a protocol makes an agent declare a response rule up front.

## 6. Where the mapping breaks

- [INTERPRETATION] Multiplicity: the metagame does not select a unique solution. Shubik's own count is "three equilibrium points," two jointly optimal and one the old (0, 0). Adding equilibria is not the same as selecting one; the metagame widens the solution set to include cooperation and thereby blunts the original dilemma's bite, but it does not tell a player which equilibrium to play. This is the standard complaint against equilibrium-widening "solutions," and Howard's construction does not escape it.

- [INTERPRETATION] The ladder problem: "conditional on the other's strategy" is well defined only when the other's strategy is a well-defined object the player can condition on, which requires a game of complete information and a defined choice order. In a genuinely simultaneous one-shot game with no moves to observe, "what I will do given what you choose" is a plan, not an observation, and the metagame depends on the players being able to reason about each other's plans as objects. Howard's move presupposes a common-knowledge rationality that the dilemma's own statement is often used to question.

- [INTERPRETATION] The socio-psychological assumption is real, not rhetorical. Shubik's ranking already names it: the metagame needs "an additional socio-psychological assumption." The requirement is that players actually treat intentions, and not just payoffs, as the things to respond to, and that they trust the opponent to do the same. That is a claim about how people frame a game, and as a claim about framing it belongs beside the experimental psychology in [[prisoners-dilemma-psychology-coordination]], not inside the mathematics.

- [INTERPRETATION] It is not the same move as its heirs, and collapsing them loses the distinctions. Superrationality needs identical reasoners (a symmetry claim). Program equilibrium needs transparent source code (a computability and observability claim). Team reasoning needs a shared framing (a preference or agency claim). The metagame needs only that both players choose contingency plans (a representation claim). It is the weakest of the four in assumptions and, correspondingly, the least determinate in result, which is a large part of why the stronger, more formal heirs, not Howard, carried the idea forward.

- [INTERPRETATION] The application to real conflict is analogy, not derivation. When confrontation analysis is used to advise on a policing or peacekeeping operation, it is using the metagame's vocabulary of intents and responses to structure a messy situation, not applying a theorem. The strength of the lineage in the operational world is precisely the softness the theory journals distrusted.

- [INTERPRETATION] Against the Substrate's own material, the honest limit is this: the metagame is a reframing device, not a mechanism. It dissolves the one-shot dilemma by asking the players to choose jointly optimal intentions, which is close to assuming the cooperation it claims to derive. The repetitions route in [[iterated-prisoners-dilemma-axelrod-tournaments]] earns cooperation from self-interest over time; the metagame earns it from a change in what is chosen. Which of those two is a "solution" is a philosophical question, and Howard answered it one way and was overruled.

### 6.1 Substrate relevance, stated briefly

- [INTERPRETATION] The usable residue for this Substrate is the primitive, not the puzzle solution: choose an intention over an action, and make the intention of the coordinate-versus-avoid kind explicit before play. That is a coordination device, and it recurs wherever an agent commits to a policy rather than to a single call.
- [INTERPRETATION] It pairs with [[multi-agent-coordination-patterns]] because a conditional strategy is exactly a coordination pattern made first-class, and with [[principal-agent-theory]] because reshaping what an agent chooses among is the same instrument as incentive alignment, applied to the choice object rather than to the payoff. It links to [[protocols-coordination-institutional-design]] because the coordinate-versus-avoid distinction is one of the distinctions a protocol must make explicit.
- [INTERPRETATION] It is a caution for the crypto and AI layer in [[faustian-bargain-prisoners-dilemma-crypto-ai]]: intention-based cooperation needs the intention to be observable and credible, the same precondition that program equilibrium makes explicit by publishing source code, and the same precondition that untrusted multi-agent settings fail to provide. Howard's dissolution assumes a transparency that a trustless setting cannot grant.

## 7. Compression points for synthesis

1. A metagame augments a game by making each player choose a conditional strategy, a policy that says what they will do in response to every strategy the opponent might pick, so the unit of choice becomes an intention rather than an action. The Nash equilibria of this enlarged game include outcomes that maximize joint payoff, which is how the metagame is said to dissolve the prisoner's dilemma rather than restate it. [FACT]
2. The construction, in Shubik's 1969 Cowles paper: the second player gets four conditional strategies (play 1 regardless, coordinate-contingent, anti-coordinate-contingent, play 2 regardless); the first player gets sixteen; a 16 x 4 matrix results with three equilibria, two jointly optimal and the old (0, 0); higher regressions add nothing. [FACT, https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1506&context=cowles-discussion-paper-series]
3. Shubik's reading: the four conditional strategies split into "inner-directed" (1 and 4) and "other-directed" (2 and 3), strategy 2 stating an intent to coordinate and strategy 3 an intent to avoid coordination; the metagame is "an attempt to build complete plausibility for a restricted set of intents." [FACT, same URL]
4. Howard's central work is Paradoxes of Rationality: Theory of Metagames and Political Behavior, MIT Press, 1971, with an earlier statement in "The Theory of Meta-Games," General Systems, vol. 11, pp. 167 to 186, 1966; Wikipedia renders the 1971 subtitle as "Games, Metagames, and Political Behavior," a citation variant of one work. [FACT for the citations; the book itself was not read]
5. Rapoport called the metagame "the resolution" of the prisoner's dilemma (Scientific American, July 1967); Shubik recorded his own dissent in the same breath, calling it "mathematically interesting" but no general resolution, and ranking it as needing "an additional socio-psychological assumption." [FACT]
6. Poundstone introduces the metagame as the intention-based answer to a maximizing world: strategies are "intentions about how one would play the game" (p. 226), defection is rational because it raises payoff (p. 227), and "society is founded on cooperation" (p. 227); he reports the promise without the cost. [FACT, page-anchored to the operator's transcription]
7. The lineage: Howard's intention over strategies, Hofstadter's superrationality (Metamagical Themas, 1985), Tennenholtz's program equilibrium (Games and Economic Behavior 49(2), 2004, where the one-shot dilemma's program equilibrium is mutual cooperation), Bacharach's team reasoning (Beyond Individual Choice, 2006, edited by Gold and Sugden), and Sugden's convention-based and team-agency work. Four levers to the same cooperation-without-enforcer end. [FACT for Tennenholtz and Bacharach; BACKGROUND for Hofstadter and Sugden]
8. Reception: largely ignored by mainstream economics and game theory; its afterlife ran through political science, operational analysis and policing, ending in Howard and Bennett's confrontation analysis for "operations other than war." Report this plainly; the metagame is a real but marginal lineage. [INTERPRETATION grounded in the citation record]
9. Where it breaks: it does not select a unique solution (multiple equilibria); it needs complete information and a defined conditioning object; it leans on a socio-psychological assumption about players treating intentions as the thing to respond to; and it is the weakest in assumptions and least determinate of the four lineage moves. [INTERPRETATION]
10. For the Substrate's coordination threads, the usable extract is the primitive, not the puzzle solution: choosing an intention over an action, and making intentions of the coordinate-versus-avoid kind explicit, is a coordination device that recurs in protocol design and in multi-agent agents that commit to policies rather than single actions (see [[multi-agent-coordination-patterns]], [[protocols-coordination-institutional-design]], [[principal-agent-theory]]). [INTERPRETATION]
11. Naming caution: "meta-game" carries three senses in this graph, Howard's conditional-strategy construct (this file), the informal use for altruistic punishment in [[prisoners-dilemma-psychology-coordination]], and the slang sense of a strategy layer; synthesis should say which it means, since only the first is Howard's. [INTERPRETATION]
