## 1. The Logical Form

Popper's canonical structure has three components: universal statement, singular existential basic statement, prohibition structure.

**Universal statement form:** "All As are X," logically equivalent to "No A is not-X." The universal statement prohibits the existence of any A that is not-X. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).

**Basic statement form:** "There is an X at Y" (singular existential). A basic statement is singular (about a specific spacetime region) and existential (asserts something exists there). It must be intersubjectively testable. FACT (SEP Popper, section 4, verified this session).

**Prohibition structure:** The universal statement "All As are X" is falsified by the basic statement "Some A is not-X," or equivalently "There is an A at Y that is not-X." The theory says: such a thing cannot exist. If it is found, the theory is false. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).

## 2. Verified Examples

Three concrete examples of Popper's hypothesis structure, verified this session:

**Conservation of energy.** Popper expresses it as "There is no perpetual motion machine." The universal law prohibits the existence of a perpetual motion machine. The putative basic statement "In space-time region k there is an apparatus which is a perpetual motion machine" would, if true, falsify the law. FACT (SEP Popper, sections 3 and 4, citing LSD 2002:48, verified this session).

**Einstein's light deflection.** Einstein's theory predicted that light is deflected towards solid bodies. This was highly improbable under Newtonian physics. Eddington's 1919 experiments confirmed it. If the deflection had not been observed, the theory would have been falsified. FACT (SEP Popper, section 2, verified this session).

**All swans are white.** Popper uses this as the paradigmatic example of a universal statement that is falsifiable by a single counter-instance. FACT (verified via *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963), essay "Science: Conjectures and Refutations," this session).

## 3. Empirical Content and Falsifiability

The empirical content of a theory is the class of its potential falsifiers. The more a theory prohibits, the more it says. A theory that says more has greater empirical content and is more falsifiable. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).

Popper's rule: maximize content by making the theory more prohibitive, not less. "All As are X" says more than "Some As are X" because it prohibits more. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).

Corroboration only counts when it is the positive result of a genuinely risky prediction, one that might conceivably have been false. Confirming evidence is cheap. What matters is whether the theory sticks its neck out. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).

## 4. Scientific vs. Non-Scientific Structure

**Scientific:** "All swans are white" is scientific because it prohibits something specific (non-white swans) and can be falsified by a single basic statement ("There is a black swan at location Y"). It is risky.

**Non-scientific:** "All human behavior is explained by unconscious drives" is not scientific because nothing could falsify it. Any observed behavior can be reinterpreted as confirming the theory. It does not prohibit any observable state of affairs. Popper saw this in Freud and Adler: their theories were amenable only to confirmation, never falsification. FACT (SEP Popper, section 2, verified this session).

The structural difference: scientific hypotheses have a non-empty class of potential falsifiers. Non-scientific ones have an empty class. They explain everything by explaining away every possible counter-instance.

## 5. Severity

Severity is a property of tests, not just theories. A test is severe when the prediction is unlikely given background knowledge, especially if the theory being tested is false. BACKGROUND.

To build severity into the hypothesis structure itself:

- Make the prediction highly specific and quantitative where possible. Einstein predicted a specific angle of deflection, not just "light bends."
- Make the prediction improbable under the rival or status quo theory. If Newton is true, light should not bend (or should bend by half the GR amount). The more improbable the prediction given the alternative, the more severe the test.
- Make the prediction novel: predict something not yet observed, rather than retrofitting known data.
- Ensure the prediction is derived from the theory's core, not from an auxiliary added to save it.

## 6. Hypothesis vs. Basic Statement

**Hypothesis:** Universal, conjectural, goes beyond experience. Cannot be verified. Can only be falsified or corroborated. It is a guess, a conjecture. FACT (SEP Popper, sections 3 and 5, verified this session).

**Basic statement:** Singular, existential, observational (though theory-laden), intersubjectively testable. It can contradict a universal hypothesis. It serves as a potential falsifier. FACT (SEP Popper, section 4, verified this session).

The testing relation: A hypothesis is tested by deriving basic statements from it. If the derived basic statement is accepted as true, and it contradicts the hypothesis, the hypothesis is falsified. The logic is modus tollens: if H implies B, and not-B, then not-H. FACT (SEP Problem of Induction, Popper section, verified this session).

Basic statements themselves are not incorrigible. They are accepted by convention, by decision of the research community, and remain open to further testing. Popper compares this to a jury verdict. FACT (SEP Popper, section 4, verified this session).

## 7. The Template

A well-formed Popperian hypothesis has this skeleton:

- **Universal claim:** For all X, if condition C holds, then Y holds. (Or: No X under C is not-Y.)
- **Prohibition:** There cannot exist an X under C that is not-Y.
- **Potential falsifier (basic statement):** There exists an X at spacetime region R under condition C that is not-Y.
- **Risky prediction:** A specific, novel, quantitatively precise consequence that is improbable if the hypothesis is false.
- **No escape clause:** The hypothesis does not contain or imply an auxiliary that would explain away the falsifier if observed.

## 8. Popper's Prescriptions for Writing

Six rules drawn from the SEP entries and the structure of Popper's own examples:

1. **Be bold.** The hypothesis must go beyond existing knowledge. Science starts with problems, not observations. FACT (SEP Popper, section 5, verified this session).
2. **Be specific.** Use the universal form "All As are X" or its equivalent. Do not use vague or open-ended formulations that can absorb any counter-example. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).
3. **Be prohibitive.** State what cannot happen. The more you prohibit, the more you say. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3, verified this session).
4. **Make risky predictions.** Deduce consequences that are improbable under current theory. Einstein's light deflection was the model. FACT (SEP Popper, section 2, verified this session).
5. **Avoid vagueness.** A theory that can explain anything explains nothing. The class of potential falsifiers must be non-empty and well-defined. FACT (SEP Popper, section 3; SEP Scientific Method, section 3.3, verified this session).
6. **Avoid ad hoc rescue clauses.** Do not add auxiliary hypotheses solely to save the theory from falsification. Modifications must increase, not decrease, falsifiability. FACT (SEP Scientific Method, section 3.3, verified this session).
