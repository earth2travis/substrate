## 1. Schumpeter's formulation

The concept appears in *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (Harper & Brothers, 1942), Part II ("Can Capitalism Survive?"), Chapter VII, titled "The Process of Creative Destruction." FACT (full scan and standalone chapter text verified this session).

Schumpeter argued that the fundamental fact about capitalism is not equilibrium or price competition but an evolutionary process of industrial mutation that "incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, incessantly destroying the old one, incessantly creating a new one." FACT (chapter text). Key claims:

- The process is "the essential fact about capitalism," not a side effect or pathology. FACT.
- Competition that matters is "competition from the new commodity, the new technology, the new source of supply, the new type of organization," which "commands a decisive cost or quality advantage and which strikes not at the margins of the profits and outputs of the existing firms but at their foundations and their very lives." FACT.
- The "perennial gale of creative destruction" is his framing; price competition within a given structure is secondary. FACT.
- The unit of concern should be long-run performance under structural change, not allocative efficiency at a point in time; a monopoly position that is constantly threatened may perform better than textbook competition. FACT (elaborated in Chapter VIII, "Monopolistic Practices").

**What he got wrong:**

- Schumpeter predicted that capitalism would not survive: its very success would erode the institutional and social strata (the entrepreneur, the bourgeois family, the protective political layer) that sustain it, and bureaucratized "trustified" capitalism would give way to socialism. This prediction, the animating question of Part II, has not been borne out in the form he gave it. BACKGROUND.
- His late theory of the entrepreneur shifted between editions and works: the heroic individual innovator of *The Theory of Economic Development* (1911/1934) is largely replaced in 1942 by the corporate R&D department, with innovation routinized. Critics note this leaves his mature work without a coherent micro-agent of the process he described. BACKGROUND. INTERPRETATION: the tension between individualist and corporatist accounts of innovation is never resolved in his corpus.

## 2. Precursors: Marx, Sombart, the Austrians

**Marx.** The *Communist Manifesto* (1848) states: "The bourgeoisie cannot exist without constantly revolutionizing the instruments of production, and thereby the relations of production, and with them the whole relations of society... Constant revolutionising of production, uninterrupted disturbance of all social conditions, everlasting uncertainty and agitation distinguish the bourgeois epoch from all earlier ones." FACT (quote verified this session against multiple scholarly reproductions). Schumpeter openly credited Marx as the first to see capitalism as an evolutionary process; his 1942 chapters on Marx precede the creative destruction chapter in Part I. BACKGROUND. INTERPRETATION: Schumpeter's process is Marx's dialectic of the forces of production stripped of class struggle and immiseration, with the entrepreneur standing in for the bourgeoisie as collective agent.

**Sombart.** Werner Sombart is the standard cited German precursor. The received lineage runs Nietzsche to Sombart to Schumpeter, documented in Reinert and Reinert, "Creative Destruction in Economics: Nietzsche, Sombart, Schumpeter." FACT (chapter verified to exist; the lineage claim is BACKGROUND). Important correction found this session: a page-by-page re-reading of Sombart's *Krieg und Kapitalismus* (1913) concludes Sombart never actually used the phrase "schöpferische Zerstörung" in that work; the attribution is largely retrospective. FACT (a 2026 Strasbourg working paper and a 2026 *Cliometrica* article both verified to exist and state this). INTERPRETATION: cite Sombart as an influence on the milieu and the motif of destruction-as-renewal, not as the verified coiner of the exact German phrase, unless a primary page citation can be produced.

**Austrian context.** Schumpeter was Austrian-trained (Böhm-Bawerk's seminar) but stood apart from the Mises-Hayek school. Mises's *Human Action* (1949) treats the entrepreneur as the agent of market adjustment; Hayek's "Competition as a Discovery Procedure" (1968 lecture, published 1978) frames competition as a process for discovering facts no one possesses in advance. BACKGROUND. INTERPRETATION: Schumpeterian creative destruction and Hayekian discovery are cousins, not twins. Hayek's competition is equilibrating and epistemic; Schumpeter's is disequilibrating and structural. Modern Schumpeterian growth theory owes more to the disequilibrium side.

## 3. Post-Schumpeter development: Aghion-Howitt, Christensen

**Aghion and Howitt (1992).** "A Model of Growth Through Creative Destruction," *Econometrica*, Vol. 60, No. 2 (March 1992), pp. 323-351. FACT (publisher citation page verified this session). Preceded by NBER Working Paper No. 3223 (January 1990). FACT. The paper endogenizes growth: growth results from quality-improving innovations that render previous vintages obsolete, with the research arbitrage equation determining the rate. BACKGROUND. Aghion and Howitt received the 2025 Nobel Prize in Economics for this program ("sustained growth through creative destruction"). FACT (CEPR/VoxEU column verified this session). INTERPRETATION: the formalization bought rigor at a price: creative destruction enters the model as a Poisson arrival of quality ladders, which tames the structural and sociological depth of Schumpeter's original.

**Christensen (1997).** Clayton M. Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail*, Harvard Business School Press, 1997. FACT (HBS faculty page verified this session). The book develops "disruptive technologies/innovation." FACT. INTERPRETATION: Christensen's concept is a partial popularization. Disruptive innovation describes one mechanism (low-end or new-market footholds moving upmarket), whereas Schumpeter's process is economy-wide and includes competence-destroying change from any direction. Christensen also lacked the macro growth framing; his is a firm-strategy theory.

**Schumpeterian vs. price competition.** Following Chapter VII directly: competition from new commodities, technologies, sources of supply, and organization types is the decisive kind; price competition within an existing structure is secondary. FACT. BACKGROUND: this distinction anchors the modern empirical IO and growth literatures, and Aghion's later work on the inverted-U between competition and innovation is a direct descendant.

## 4. The empirical evidence: job flows and productivity

### Job creation and destruction rates

- FACT: In U.S. manufacturing, roughly 1 in 10 jobs is created and another 1 in 10 is destroyed each year. Davis and Haltiwanger (1999): "Using annual figures, roughly 1 in 10 jobs are created and another 1 in 10 are destroyed each year." This is the canonical summary of gross job flows in the U.S. economy.
- FACT: The 1980-2011 private-sector averages from the Longitudinal Business Database are higher: gross job creation averaged 16.7 percent and gross job destruction averaged 15.2 percent per year. Net employment growth is the difference between creation and destruction. (Decker, Haltiwanger, Jarmin, Miranda 2013, JEP.)
- FACT: Job flows are highly concentrated and lumpy. The average one-year persistence rate for annual job creation is 68 percent and for annual job destruction is 81 percent. (Davis and Haltiwanger 1999.)

### The decline of dynamism

- FACT: The pace of business dynamism in the U.S. has declined over recent decades. Between 1985 and 2005, the rate of gross job creation fell by 2.9 percentage points and gross job destruction fell by 1.5 percentage points. (Decker, Haltiwanger, Jarmin, Miranda 2013.)
- FACT: The firm startup rate declined from an average of 12.0 percent in the late 1980s to an average of 10.6 percent just before the Great Recession, a 12 percent decline over 17 years. (Decker et al. 2013.)
- FACT: The share of employment accounted for by young firms (age 5 or less) declined by almost 30 percent over the last 30 years. (Decker et al. 2013.)
- FACT: The post-2000 decline in job reallocation reflects weaker marginal responsiveness of business-level employment to productivity, not lower dispersion of idiosyncratic shocks. Within-industry dispersion of TFP has risen, while responsiveness has weakened. (Decker, Haltiwanger, Jarmin, Miranda 2020, AER.)
- INTERPRETATION: The dynamism decline is not a simple story of "fewer shocks." The economy is generating at least as much underlying turbulence (TFP dispersion up), but firms are responding less to it. This points to rising adjustment frictions (zoning, licensing, non-competes, declining labor market fluidity, increased market power) rather than a calmer technological environment.

### Productivity evidence

- FACT: Foster, Haltiwanger, and Krizan (2001) find that the entry and exit of plants account for approximately 25 percent of U.S. manufacturing productivity growth over 5-year windows. (Cited in Asturias, Hur, Kehoe, Ruhl 2021.)
- FACT: The FHK decomposition splits productivity growth into within-plant (continuing plants), between-plant (reallocation of shares), cross-product (interaction), and net entry terms. The within-plant component accounts for nearly half of overall growth. Net entry and cross-product effects together account for 60 percent of the 10-year increase in multifactor productivity for the average manufacturing industry. (Davis and Haltiwanger 1999, citing FHK.)
- FACT: The contribution of entry and exit depends heavily on the growth rate. In Chile and Korea, entry and exit account for less than 25 percent during slow-growth periods but 37 to 58 percent during fast-growth periods. China shows a 72 percent contribution. (Asturias, Hur, Kehoe, Ruhl 2021.)
- INTERPRETATION: Creative destruction is not a uniform accounting identity. It matters most when the economy is actually growing fast. In slow-growth regimes, incumbents dominate productivity gains. In fast-growth regimes, selection and entry/exit do the heavy lifting.

## 5. Historical cases

### The power loom and the textile industry, 1785-1830

- FACT: Handloom weavers in the English cotton industry averaged 240 pence per week in 1806 but earned under 100 pence per week by 1820. Factory wages stayed roughly flat near 120 pence/week over the same period. Family earnings for handloom weavers in two Lancashire towns fell by half over the five years starting in 1814. (Acemoglu and Johnson, *Annual Review of Economics* 2024; Allen, NYU Abu Dhabi WP 2017.)
- FACT: Real earnings of handloom weavers collapsed after about 1815. Power-loom weaving only became cost-competitive because of the high handloom wages of 1793-1817, an induced-innovation pattern: high labor costs pulled the new technology into use, then the technology drove the incumbent craft's earnings to subsistence. (Allen 2017.)
- BACKGROUND: The productivity gain was enormous. A power loom weaver could produce many times the output of a hand-loom weaver. The price of cotton cloth fell dramatically, driving mass adoption and the expansion of the textile industry into the first leading sector of the Industrial Revolution.
- BACKGROUND: The destruction was concentrated in specific regions (Lancashire, Yorkshire) and occupations. The creation side (factory jobs, machine tenders, mechanics, engineers) emerged in the same regions but required different skills and often different workers.
- INTERPRETATION: The textile case is the canonical historical example because it shows both sides clearly: the old occupation was destroyed within a generation, while the new industry created more total employment and vastly higher productivity. But the destruction was borne by specific individuals who could not easily transition.

### The automobile replacing the horse economy, 1900-1930

- FACT: In 1910, the U.S. had approximately 24 to 26 million horses and mules. By 1930, the U.S. had registered 23 million automobiles. The horse and mule population peaked around 1910-1920 and then entered a steep decline. (USDA Statistical Bulletin No. 5, 1925; Yale Energy History.)
- FACT: In New York City in the 1880s, 12,000 horses and mules transported over 160 million passengers annually. By 1910, there were 130,000 horses in Manhattan alone. The automobile displaced this entire urban ecosystem within two decades. (Revs Institute.)
- FACT: In 1840, there were 4.3 million horses and mules in the U.S. By 1900, this had grown to 24 million, a six-fold increase. The transition to automobiles and tractors reversed this trend dramatically. (Yale Energy History.)
- BACKGROUND: In 1910, roughly 130 million acres of U.S. farmland were devoted to growing fodder for horses. This land was gradually released for other crops or reforestation as the horse population declined.
- INTERPRETATION: The automobile transition is the best-quantified historical case. The destruction side (horses, blacksmiths, stables) was complete within 20 years. The creation side (auto industry, oil, roads, suburbs) ultimately employed far more people. But the transition was regionally and occupationally asymmetric: rural areas that bred horses lost, while Detroit and Akron gained.

### The internet disrupting retail and media, 1995-2010

- BACKGROUND: E-commerce grew from negligible levels in 1995 to approximately 4-5 percent of total U.S. retail sales by 2010, with much higher shares in specific categories (books, electronics, music).
- BACKGROUND: The destruction side was highly visible: Borders Books (bankrupt 2011), Blockbuster (bankrupt 2010), Circuit City (bankrupt 2008). Newspaper classified advertising revenue collapsed from roughly $20 billion in 2000 to under $6 billion by 2010, destroying the business model of local journalism.
- BACKGROUND: The creation side included Amazon, eBay, Google, Netflix, and millions of online-only small businesses. The productivity gain was substantial but difficult to measure in standard statistics.
- INTERPRETATION: The internet case shows that creative destruction in the digital era operates faster than in previous waves (roughly 10-15 years for visible disruption) but with more ambiguous productivity measurement. The destruction side is easy to count (bankruptcies, layoffs). The creation side often takes the form of consumer surplus (free search, free news, lower prices) that does not appear in GDP.

## 6. The destruction side: who pays

### The China shock literature

- BACKGROUND: Autor, Dorn, and Hanson (2013, 2016, 2021) document that rising import competition from China after 1990 caused persistent, localized labor market devastation in U.S. manufacturing regions. The affected regions did not recover within a decade or more.
- BACKGROUND: The key finding is that adjustment to trade shocks is slow, incomplete, and geographically concentrated. Workers in exposed industries experienced lower wages, lower employment, higher disability claims, and higher mortality.
- BACKGROUND: The standard trade model predicted that displaced workers would move to new sectors or regions. The empirical evidence shows they did not. Mobility was low, and the new jobs were in different places and required different skills.
- INTERPRETATION: The China shock literature is the strongest empirical challenge to the optimistic view of creative destruction. It shows that destruction can be permanent for specific people and places, even when the aggregate economy gains. The asymmetry is geographic: costs are concentrated, benefits are diffuse.

### Displaced workers and non-recovery

- BACKGROUND: The displaced worker literature (Jacobson, LaLonde, Sullivan 1993; Couch and Placzek 2010; von Wachter 2010) shows that workers who lose jobs in mass layoffs experience large, persistent earnings losses. On average, earnings are 20-30 percent lower even 5-10 years after displacement.
- BACKGROUND: Recovery is especially poor for older workers, workers with firm-specific skills, and workers in declining industries or regions. The losses are not just transitional; they are often permanent.
- INTERPRETATION: The destruction side of creative destruction is not a rounding error. For the individuals and communities experiencing it, it is a life-altering event with long-term consequences. The aggregate productivity gain does not compensate the losers.

### Regional asymmetry

- FACT: The decline in job reallocation is pervasive across U.S. states, but the magnitude varies. Between 1987/89 and 2004/06, the change in reallocation rates ranged from -18.5 percentage points in Alaska to -2.8 in Michigan. (Decker et al. 2013, Figure 13.)
- INTERPRETATION: Creative destruction is not a national average phenomenon. It is a local phenomenon with national aggregates. The asymmetry means that political and social responses will be geographically concentrated, even when the economic benefits are diffuse.

## 7. The AI wave: what's being destroyed, what's being created

The destruction side of the current wave is concentrated in routine cognitive work. Unlike previous automation waves that hit manufacturing and manual clerical work, generative AI targets language, pattern recognition, and structured decision-making.

**Customer support.** US customer service employment declined by approximately 80,000 positions between 2022 and 2024. FACT (Site Selection Group data). Goldman Sachs Research lists customer service representatives among occupations with highest displacement risk. FACT. Klarna publicly stated its AI assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, equivalent to 700 full-time agents. BACKGROUND (company announcement 2024).

**Translation and interpretation.** Research tracking Google Translate adoption found that each percentage-point increase in adoption correlated with a 0.7 percentage-point decline in translator employment growth. FACT ("Lost in Translation" study). Prediction markets assign an 85% probability to a large decrease in translator employment by end of 2027. FACT (Manifold Market). The destruction is not total elimination but demand compression: machine translation handles routine volume, leaving human translators to complex or high-stakes work.

**Content writing and copywriting.** Upwork data shows writing gigs dropped by roughly 30% in the year following ChatGPT's launch. BACKGROUND (platform data 2023-2024). The destruction is asymmetric: low-end SEO content and product descriptions have collapsed, while high-end editorial and strategic writing remains intact.

**Coding assistance and junior developer roles.** Unemployment among 20- to 30-year-olds in tech-exposed occupations rose by almost 3 percentage points since the start of 2025. FACT (Goldman Sachs Research). Evidence suggests hiring in remote-friendly occupations has skewed toward more experienced workers since 2024. FACT (Stanford SIEPR). The destruction is not of "programmers" as a class but of the entry-level pipeline: AI coding assistants handle routine implementation tasks that previously justified junior hires.

**Radiology and diagnostic reads.** Despite early predictions, radiologist displacement has not materialized at scale. AI diagnostic tools function as decision support rather than replacement. Radiologist employment remains stable. BACKGROUND (BLS data through 2025). This is a case where destruction was predicted but creation (AI-assisted radiology) arrived first.

**What is being created:**

- **Prompt engineering.** A snapshot of 20,662 LinkedIn job postings found only 72 prompt engineer positions, less than 0.5% of AI-related roles. FACT (arXiv 2506.00058). The title is already fading as the skill diffuses. What remains is a skill premium: AI engineers with prompt expertise earn 15-25% more. FACT (PE Collective 2026).
- **AI safety and alignment research.** A genuine new category. Job postings grew from near zero in 2020 to hundreds of openings by 2024. BACKGROUND. The field has institutionalized with journals, conferences, and dedicated funding.
- **Agent orchestration and AI workflow design.** Roles like "AI orchestration engineer," "LLM operations," and "agent architect" are appearing. Not yet standardized titles but appearing at scale. BACKGROUND (LinkedIn/Indeed aggregation 2024-2025).
- **Model evaluation and AI governance.** The EU AI Act and US executive orders created demand for compliance roles. BACKGROUND (WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025).
- **The creation lag.** The WEF estimates a net employment increase of 7%, or 78 million jobs globally by 2030, with displacement of 92 million roles and creation of 170 million. FACT (WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025). The new roles demand higher education and different geographic concentration than the destroyed ones.

## 8. The speed question

The speed comparison is the strongest argument that this wave is different.

**Historical baselines.** Electrification took roughly four decades to reach 70% of US households. FACT (Epoch AI analysis). Telephones took six decades. The internet took approximately 10 years to reach 50% of US households. BACKGROUND.

**AI adoption speed.** ChatGPT reached 100 million monthly active users in two months. FACT (UBS via Reuters, February 2023). This is the fastest-growing consumer application in history, faster than TikTok (9 months) and Instagram (2.5 years). Epoch AI's analysis concludes that AI diffusion has been faster than almost all technologies in history. FACT.

**Enterprise adoption.** As of a recent US survey, only 9.3% of companies reported using generative AI in production during the last two weeks. FACT (Goldman Sachs Research). The consumer adoption curve has outpaced enterprise integration, but the gap is narrowing.

**The asymmetry argument.** The destruction side operates at the speed of software deployment. A company can replace a customer support team with an AI agent in a quarter. The creation side operates at the speed of institutional formation: new job titles, training pipelines, and professional norms take years to stabilize. INTERPRETATION: This asymmetry means the transition period may be more painful than previous waves, even if the long-run equilibrium is positive. The speed of capability improvement (measured in months per model generation) exceeds the speed of labor market adjustment (measured in years per occupation).

**Historical counterpoint.** Previous general-purpose technologies also destroyed faster than they created during their early deployment. The power loom destroyed hand-weaving jobs in decades; the new textile industry took generations to absorb displaced workers. INTERPRETATION: The difference now is that AI targets cognitive work, and cognitive workers have higher expectations of stability and stronger political voice than manual workers did in previous waves.

## 9. The productivity J-curve

**The Solow paradox applied to AI.** Robert Solow's 1987 observation that "you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics" has been updated by Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson as the "modern productivity paradox." FACT (NBER Working Paper 24001). AI systems match or surpass human performance on many tasks, yet measured productivity growth remained tepid through the 2010s.

**The Productivity J-Curve.** Brynjolfsson, Rock, and Syverson formalized this as the Productivity J-Curve. FACT (NBER Working Paper 25148, published in AEJ: Macroeconomics 2021). Their model shows that general-purpose technologies require complementary intangible investments (organizational change, training, new business processes) that are not captured in national accounts. Early in a GPT's deployment, measured productivity understates true productivity because resources are diverted to unmeasured capital formation. Later, as these intangibles yield measurable output, productivity appears to surge. They estimate that adjusted TFP was 15.9% higher than official measures at the end of 2017. FACT.

**Recent productivity data.** US nonfarm business labor productivity increased 2.3% in 2024, following 1.6% in 2023 and a decline of 1.5% in 2022. FACT (BLS). Preliminary data for 2025 shows productivity growth of 2.8% in Q4 2025. FACT (BLS preliminary release March 2026).

**Is the J-Curve turning?** INTERPRETATION: The 2024-2025 productivity acceleration is consistent with the J-Curve's upward leg, but it is too early to attribute causally to AI. The productivity surge could reflect post-pandemic normalization, labor market tightening, or other factors. Apollo Academy notes that "AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data." FACT. If the J-Curve is turning, the inflection is recent and fragile.

**The measurement problem.** The J-Curve argument implies that current productivity statistics may still understate AI's contribution because firms are investing in unmeasured intangibles (prompt libraries, fine-tuned models, workflow redesign). INTERPRETATION: If Brynjolfsson's model is correct, the true productivity effect of AI is larger than the official statistics show.

## 10. Where the mapping breaks

- **The dynamism decline undermines the theory's empirical foundation.** Creative destruction is supposed to be the engine of capitalism, but Decker et al. show the engine has been slowing since the 1980s, and the slowdown accelerated after 2000. If reallocation drives growth, why is there less of it? The theory does not answer this. The decline may be a measurement artifact, a structural shift toward services, or a genuine reduction in the economy's capacity for self-renewal. All three readings threaten the theory's applicability to the present moment.
- **The WEF projections are optimistic by construction.** The estimate of 170 million jobs created vs 92 million displaced by 2030 assumes the creation side materializes on schedule. Previous technology waves did not deliver net job creation on the timeline that advocates predicted. The projection is a model output, not an observation.
- **The speed comparison may be misleading.** ChatGPT's 100M users in two months measures consumer curiosity, not enterprise transformation. The 9.3% enterprise adoption rate is the more relevant number, and it suggests the diffusion is much slower than the headlines imply.
- **The J-Curve is unfalsifiable in the short run.** If productivity doesn't accelerate, the model says "the intangibles haven't matured yet." If it does, the model says "told you so." The theory explains any outcome, which weakens its predictive power.
- **The destruction evidence is occupation-level, not task-level.** Most of the displacement data measures job categories, not the actual tasks within those jobs. A customer service representative who uses AI to handle routine queries while escalating complex ones hasn't been destroyed; they've been augmented. The net effect on employment is more ambiguous than the headline numbers suggest.
- **The entry-level coding pipeline contraction may be cyclical, not structural.** The 3-percentage-point rise in unemployment among 20-30 year olds in tech-exposed occupations coincides with a broader tech hiring downturn post-2022. Disentangling AI displacement from interest-rate-driven hiring contraction is genuinely difficult.
- **The China shock is not a clean creative destruction case.** The Autor-Dorn-Hanson literature measures trade-driven displacement, not technology-driven displacement. The two interact, but the policy implications differ. Using the China shock as evidence about AI-driven creative destruction requires assuming that the adjustment dynamics are the same, which is unproven.
- **The historical cases are not controlled experiments.** The power loom, the automobile, and the internet each coincided with massive confounding changes (urbanization, two world wars, the rise of consumer credit). Attributing all the destruction and creation to the technology alone overstates the case.

## 11. Compression points for synthesis

1. Creative destruction is Schumpeter's 1942 term for the essential fact about capitalism: not price competition but structural replacement, "competition from the new commodity, the new technology, the new source of supply, the new type of organization" that strikes "at the foundations and the very lives" of existing firms.
2. The concept descends from Marx's observation that the bourgeoisie "cannot exist without constantly revolutionizing the instruments of production." Schumpeter stripped Marx's dialectic of class struggle and kept the evolutionary engine. The Sombart attribution (that he coined the German phrase) is contested by 2026 scholarship.
3. Aghion and Howitt (1992, Econometrica, verified) formalized creative destruction as endogenous growth theory, earning the 2025 Nobel. The formalization bought rigor at the price of taming Schumpeter's sociological depth into Poisson arrivals of quality ladders.
4. The U.S. economy historically exhibited high rates of job creation and destruction (roughly 10-16 percent per year), but these rates have declined significantly since the 1980s, and the decline accelerated after 2000. The engine of creative destruction is slowing even as the AI wave accelerates.
5. Reallocation across plants and firms accounts for roughly 25 percent of U.S. manufacturing productivity growth, with entry and exit alone contributing that share. But the contribution depends on the growth rate: in fast-growth regimes, selection does the heavy lifting; in slow-growth regimes, incumbents dominate.
6. The historical cases (textile, automobile, internet) show both sides clearly: old occupations destroyed within a generation, new industries creating more total employment. But the destruction was borne by specific individuals who could not transition, and the creation went to different people in different places.
7. The AI wave is the fastest-adopted technology in history (100M users in 2 months vs. 40 years for electrification), but enterprise adoption is only 9.3%, suggesting the consumer curve has outpaced the structural transformation.
8. The destruction side operates at software speed (replace a team in a quarter); the creation side operates at institutional speed (new job titles, training pipelines, professional norms take years). This asymmetry is the central risk: the labor market has no precedent for absorbing displaced cognitive workers at the pace AI can displace them.
9. The productivity J-Curve (Brynjolfsson, NBER WP 25148, verified) says current statistics understate AI's true effect because complementary intangible investments are unmeasured. The 2024-2025 productivity acceleration (2.3%, 2.8% Q4 2025) is consistent with the upward leg but too recent to attribute causally.
10. The destruction side has real costs: displaced workers experience 20-30 percent earnings losses even 5-10 years after displacement, and affected regions can experience persistent decline. The aggregate productivity gain does not compensate the losers.
