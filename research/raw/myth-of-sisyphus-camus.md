# The Myth of Sisyphus by Albert Camus (1942)

## Who and What

Albert Camus (1913–1960), French-Algerian writer, journalist, and Nobel laureate in Literature (1957). Not a trained academic philosopher — his university advisor noted he was "more a writer than a philosopher" — but a *philosophe* in the Enlightenment tradition: a public intellectual who used novels, plays, and essays to wage arguments by image rather than by syllogism.

*The Myth of Sisyphus* is a 185-page philosophical essay published in 1942 by Éditions Gallimard. It is Camus's foundational statement of his philosophy of the absurd, written during the Fall of France (1940) as millions fled advancing German armies. Robert Zaretsky argues that the chaos of war — the sudden, violent collapse of ordinary meaning — directly fed the essay's central question: if nothing makes sense, why keep going?

The essay belongs to Camus's "Cycle of the Absurd" alongside the novel *The Stranger* (1942) and the plays *The Misunderstanding* (1942) and *Caligula* (1944). A second cycle, the "Cycle of Revolt," would follow with *The Plague* (1947) and *The Rebel* (1951).

## The Central Question

"There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide."

Camus opens with a provocation. He is not advocating suicide. He is establishing a criterion: judge a philosophical problem by the consequences it entails. If someone concludes life is meaningless, they may kill themselves. If someone concludes their meaning is the one true meaning, they may kill others to defend it. No other philosophical question — free will, the existence of God, the nature of reality — has stakes this high.

The essay is an extended attempt to answer: does the recognition that life has no given meaning *necessarily* imply that life is not worth living?

## The Absurd Defined

The absurd is not a property of the universe. It is not a property of the human mind. It is the *collision* between two things:

1. **The human demand for meaning.** We have a "wild longing for clarity." We want the world to make sense, to have purpose, to answer us. We are nostalgic for a unity we have never actually experienced.

2. **The unreasonable silence of the universe.** The world is irrational, indifferent, opaque. Science describes phenomena but never explains *why*. "Of whom and of what indeed can I say: 'I know that!' This heart within me I can feel, and I judge that it exists. This world I can touch, and I likewise judge that it exists. There ends all my knowledge, and the rest is construction."

The absurd is born in the gap. "The absurd is essentially a divorce. It lies in neither of the elements compared; it is born of their confrontation." It is the tension of a question that gets no answer, asked by a creature that cannot stop asking.

### The Feeling of Absurdity

Before the absurd becomes a philosophical concept, it is a *feeling*. Camus describes it as a sudden estrangement — the world ceases to feel like home. You wake up, go to work, eat, sleep, repeat. One day you notice yourself doing it. You see the mechanical nature of your own life from the outside, and the question rises unbidden: "Why?"

This is the feeling of exile. The world was never truly a home; you only believed it was. "In a universe suddenly divested of illusions and lights, man feels an alien, a stranger."

## The Three Responses to the Absurd

Once the absurd is recognized, three responses are possible:

### 1. Physical Suicide

Eliminate the problem by eliminating one term of the equation. If the collision between human consciousness and the silent universe is unbearable, end the consciousness. Camus rejects this as an escape, not a solution. It does not resolve the absurd; it flees from it.

### 2. Philosophical Suicide

This is the subtle one, and the one Camus spends the most energy attacking. Philosophical suicide means resolving the tension by *eliminating one of the terms through intellectual dishonesty*. Two variants:

**The Leap of Faith (Kierkegaard, Chestov).** Acknowledge that reason cannot provide meaning, then *abandon reason* and believe in something transcendent anyway. Kierkegaard sees the absurd — the paradox of the infinite God becoming finite man in Christ — and makes it the foundation of faith. For Camus, this is a betrayal: you do not solve the absurd; you kill reason to preserve hope. You amputate the very faculty that revealed the absurd in the first place.

**The Deification of Reason (Husserl, the phenomenologists).** The other direction: elevate reason until it becomes a substitute for the transcendent. Husserl's phenomenology claims to grasp essences directly, to make the world fully intelligible through a purified consciousness. For Camus, this is equally dishonest — it denies the irrationality of the world by pretending reason can swallow it whole.

In both cases, something is killed to relieve the tension. Either reason is sacrificed to faith, or the world's opacity is sacrificed to reason. Camus calls both "philosophical suicide."

### 3. Revolt

Camus's answer. Stare at the absurd without flinching. Accept that the universe will never answer, but *refuse to stop asking*. Hold the tension indefinitely. "Living is keeping the absurd alive. Keeping it alive is, above all, contemplating it."

Revolt is not anger. It is not rebellion against a specific injustice (that comes later, in *The Rebel*). It is a metaphysical posture: the sustained, clear-eyed refusal of despair. You know the rock will roll back down. You push it anyway.

## The Three Consequences: Revolt, Freedom, Passion

If you accept the absurd without escape, three consequences follow. Camus calls them his "trinity."

### Revolt

The permanent state of tension. Every day you wake up and choose to live without appeal, without hope of a grand answer, that is an act of rebellion. You give the absurd its only value: "the magnitude of the confrontation."

### Freedom

Before the absurd, you live as if you are free — making plans, pursuing goals, believing your choices matter because they serve some larger purpose. The absurd destroys all that. There is no cosmic script, no afterlife, no divine scoreboard. "Death is there as the only reality."

But this destruction is liberating. If there is no eternal tomorrow, you are radically free *now*. Your life is yours alone. Every choice is real precisely because it is final. This is not the freedom *to* do anything in particular; it is freedom *from* the illusion that your actions serve a meaning outside themselves.

### Passion

If quantity and quality of experience cannot be measured against an eternal standard, then the only ethic is one of quantity. "What matters is not to live the best life but to live the most life." Pursue experience intensely. The present moment is everything, because there is nothing else.

Camus is careful: this is not hedonism. It is not mindless consumption. It is the lucid commitment to *feeling* your life while you have it, knowing it is all you have.

## The Absurd Man: Three Sketches

Camus provides three "sketches" of lives lived in full consciousness of the absurd. He explicitly says these are not models or ideals — they are illustrations.

### Don Juan

The legendary seducer. Don Juan does not seek total, eternal love. He loves each woman "with the same passion and each with his whole self," then moves on. He knows love is fleeting. He does not hope for more than the moment gives him. When others accuse him of being melancholy, Camus answers: people are melancholy for two reasons — "they do not know, or they hope." Don Juan "knows and does not hope." He lives an "ethic of quantity" in love.

### The Actor

The actor embodies ephemeral identities. In three hours on stage, he lives and dies as a character it took a lifetime of suffering to produce. Then the curtain falls and the character is gone. The actor does this every night, knowing each performance is final in its own way. "Appearing creates being." The actor's glory is to illustrate that identity itself is constructed, finite, and that this is enough.

### The Conqueror

The man of action. He chooses deeds over eternity, knowing no victory is permanent. The conqueror knows that "one cannot destroy everything without destroying oneself." He acts within history but without the illusion that history has a direction. He fights for the sake of fighting, builds for the sake of building. The struggle is the point.

## Absurd Creation

Camus devotes a section to art — specifically the novel — as the absurd creative act. An absurd work must:

- **Describe**, not explain. It presents the world as it is, without imposing meaning.
- **Not judge.** It offers no moral.
- **Not hope.** It does not console. It does not promise resolution.

The artist who succeeds at this creates a self-contained world that mirrors the absurd condition: fully realized, internally consistent, but offering no transcendent meaning. "To create is to live twice."

### The Case of Dostoevsky

Camus examines Dostoevsky's *The Possessed* and *The Brothers Karamazov* as test cases. Dostoevsky starts from the absurd — Kirilov's logic that "if God does not exist, I am God" is, Camus says, perfectly absurd — but Dostoevsky ultimately falls back. He gives Ivan Karamazov's rebellion a Christian answer. He ties Kirilov's suicide to a messianic hope. For Camus, this is a betrayal. Dostoevsky's novels are "absurd" in their material but not in their conclusion. They begin in revolt and end in consolation.

## The Myth of Sisyphus

The final chapter, and the one that gives the book its title.

### The Myth

Sisyphus, in Greek mythology, was condemned by the gods to roll a boulder to the top of a mountain. Each time he nears the summit, the rock rolls back down. He must descend, find it again, and begin anew. Forever.

Camus focuses on the *descent*. As Sisyphus walks back down the mountain, he is conscious. He knows the rock will roll again. He knows the labor is pointless. "It is during that return, that pause, that Sisyphus interests me."

In that moment of lucid awareness, tragedy becomes victory. Sisyphus "knows the whole extent of his wretched condition: it is what he thinks of during his descent." He is stronger than his rock. His scorn of the gods, his refusal to hope for reprieve, his ownership of the absurd fate — this is revolt.

### Why Sisyphus Is Happy

"The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

This is the most famous line in the book, and the most misunderstood. It is not optimism. It is not a silver lining. It is the assertion that if you accept your fate fully — without appeal, without resentment, without asking the gods for mercy — the struggle itself becomes sufficient. The rock is yours. The mountain is yours. You do not need external meaning. You make it by pushing.

### The Workman of Today

Camus explicitly connects Sisyphus to modern industrial labor: "The workman of today works every day in his life at the same tasks, and this fate is no less absurd. But it is tragic only at the rare moments when it becomes conscious."

The difference between tragedy and drudgery is awareness. The worker who performs repetitive labor without reflection is merely unhappy. The worker who *sees* the absurdity of their condition and continues anyway — that is the absurd hero.

## Appendix: Kafka

The English edition includes a separately written appendix on Franz Kafka. Camus finds Kafka's work — *The Trial*, *The Castle* — to be a nearly perfect depiction of the absurd universe: bureaucratic, opaque, filled with yearning that is never satisfied.

But Kafka, like Dostoevsky, ultimately introduces hope. In *The Castle*, the protagonist K. dies, and the villagers are told the Castle has granted him permission to stay. The absurd tension is resolved. Kafka's work is "universal" in its depiction of the absurd condition but not "absurd" in its conclusion.

## Philosophical Context and Influences

Camus wrote *The Myth of Sisyphus* in dialogue with — and against — several traditions:

### Existentialism

Camus is routinely labeled an existentialist, but he rejected the label, and the essay makes clear why. Existentialists (Sartre, Heidegger) begin from the premise that "existence precedes essence" — that humans create their own meaning through choice and action. Camus does not believe meaning is *created*. He believes the demand for meaning is permanent and permanently unsatisfied. The absurd is not a starting point from which meaning is constructed; it is the permanent condition.

### Kierkegaard

The most significant interlocutor. Camus admired Kierkegaard's honesty about the limits of reason and the depth of despair. But Kierkegaard's "leap of faith" — embracing the paradox of the Incarnation *because* it is absurd — is, for Camus, the textbook example of philosophical suicide. Kierkegaard solves the problem by killing one of its terms. Camus insists on keeping both alive.

### Nietzsche

Camus acknowledges Nietzsche's influence — the death of God, the rejection of transcendent values — but distances himself from Nietzsche's metaphysical claims (the will to power, the eternal return). Camus's absurd is lived, not systematized.

### Schopenhauer

The pessimism of Schopenhauer — the claim that life is suffering, that the will is a curse — is a backdrop. Camus inverts it: the will, lucidly accepted, is not a curse. It is the only thing worth having.

## Critical Reception and Debates

### Thomas Nagel's Response (1971)

In his essay "The Absurd," Thomas Nagel argues that Camus gets the absurd wrong. For Nagel, the absurd is not a collision between human meaning-seeking and a silent universe. It is an internal conflict within human consciousness itself: we take our lives seriously, but we are also capable of stepping back and viewing our actions as arbitrary and contingent. The absurd is the gap between the serious view and the detached view. Nagel's response is not revolt but *irony* — an amused recognition of our own ridiculousness.

### Criticisms of the Absurd Man Sketches

Camus's "absurd man" examples have faced sustained criticism. Don Juan treats women as interchangeable conquests. The conqueror is a warrior-archetype that sits uncomfortably with the realities of colonial violence (relevant given Camus's own context as a French-Algerian). The ethic of "quantity over quality" has been challenged as incoherent: if values are not real, on what basis is quantity preferred?

### The Gap Between Absurd and Revolt

A recurring scholarly debate: how does Camus get from "life is absurd" to "we must revolt"? The move from description to prescription has no obvious logical bridge. If nothing matters, why does revolt matter? Camus never fully resolves this tension in the essay; *The Rebel* (1951) is his attempt to ground the ethics of revolt more rigorously, and it creates its own set of problems.

## Relation to Camus's Other Works

### The Stranger (1942)

Published the same year. Meursault is the absurd man *before* he becomes conscious of the absurd. He drifts through life without reflection, kills a man almost absent-mindedly, and only in his prison cell, facing execution, does he achieve lucidity: "I opened myself to the gentle indifference of the world." Meursault embodies the absurd condition; Sisyphus embodies the absurd response.

### The Plague (1947)

Moves from metaphysical revolt to ethical revolt. Dr. Rieux fights the plague not because he believes he will win but because fighting is what one does. The plague is the absurd made concrete: senseless, indifferent, and requiring a response that expects no reward.

### The Rebel (1951)

The sequel. Where *Sisyphus* asks "should I kill myself?", *The Rebel* asks "should I kill others?" It examines how metaphysical revolt, when politicized, becomes revolutionary violence. Camus's rejection of Soviet communism and his insistence on limits to revolt led to his famous break with Sartre.

## Why This Matters

*The Myth of Sisyphus* is not a book that answers questions. It is a book that refuses to let you stop asking them. Its core move — turning a pointless punishment into a model of human dignity — is one of the most influential gestures in 20th-century philosophy.
