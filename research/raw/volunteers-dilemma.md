## 1. Notation, and the structure of the game

- [BACKGROUND] Notation used throughout. n players. A discrete collective good is produced if and only if at least one player volunteers. Let V be the value of the good to each player, and C the private cost to the volunteer.
- [BACKGROUND] Payoffs. If at least one player volunteers, every player receives V, and each volunteer pays C, so a volunteer nets V minus C while every non-volunteer nets V. If nobody volunteers, every player receives 0. A second volunteer adds nothing to the good and simply pays a second C.
- [INTERPRETATION] Requirements for the game to be a dilemma: V greater than C greater than 0. If V is not greater than C, volunteering is never worth it and nobody volunteers, so the good never appears and there is no dilemma to analyse, only a failure.
- [FACT] Diekmann's own illustration, read in the paper's full text this session: "Imagine a somewhat different kind of a 'prisoner's dilemma': N prisoners are sentenced for ten years if there is not at least one volunteer who confesses. In this case the volunteer(s) can expect one-year imprisonment, whereas the other prisoners will be released." (https://doi.org/10.1177/0022002785029004003)
- [INTERPRETATION] In the prisoners example the volunteer trades ten years for one, so the volunteer's gain is nine years, the released prisoners gain ten, and if nobody confesses all bear ten. That is the V greater than C greater than 0 setup, and it is why the volunteer is a self-interested player rather than a martyr.
- [INTERPRETATION] The ordinal payoff ranking that defines the game, for each player: free-ride on someone else's volunteering (V) is strictly best; volunteering (V minus C) is second; nobody volunteering (0) is worst. The ranking is V, then V minus C, then 0.
- [INTERPRETATION] The defining tension: a player's best outcome requires another player to act, and the universally worst outcome is what universal non-action delivers. Each player wants the good and wants not to be the one who pays for it.
- [BACKGROUND] Small comparison of payoff orderings, one preference line each, written best first:
  - Volunteer's dilemma: free-ride if another volunteers, then volunteer, then all abstain.
  - Prisoner's dilemma: defect on a cooperator (temptation), then mutual cooperation (reward), then mutual defection (punishment), then cooperate on a defector (sucker).
  - Chicken: win the dare, then swerve while the other drives straight, then both swerve (no crash), then both drive straight (crash). The crash is worst in chicken; the all-abstain outcome is worst in the volunteer's dilemma.
  - Stag hunt: mutual stag, then both hunt hare, then a lone stag-hunter (two equilibria, assurance problem).
- [INTERPRETATION] The single structural fact to hold on to: the VOD's efficient outcome is strictly asymmetric (exactly one volunteer), and none of the other three games has that property written into its successful outcome.
- [BACKGROUND] Production-function taxonomy, to locate the VOD among collective goods. A best-shot good is provided to the level of the single largest contribution, and the VOD is its one-contributor case. A weakest-link good is set by the smallest contribution, as in a levee. An additive (summation) good accumulates contributions. The clean VOD payoff ordering arises only in the best-shot case, which is why the production function recurs as the driver of the group-size effect in section 5.
- [INTERPRETATION] A terminological caution. "Volunteer" here names a formal role, the player who supplies the discrete good at private cost, and is not a claim about altruism or good intentions. A profit-maximising firm, a state, or a software process can equally be the volunteer. Diekmann's word has stuck, but the concept is about who bears the cost, not about virtue.

## 2. Why it is not a prisoner's dilemma

- [INTERPRETATION] A two-player prisoner's dilemma has a strictly dominant strategy (defect) for each player, so (defect, defect) is the unique equilibrium and it is Pareto-inferior to (cooperate, cooperate). The VOD has no dominant strategy at all.
- [INTERPRETATION] The absence of dominance is the whole point. Volunteering is the better move when every other player is going to defect, because it avoids the 0. Free-riding is the better move when at least one other player will volunteer, because it yields V instead of V minus C. The best move depends on what a player expects the others to do. That is a coordination or anti-coordination problem, not a dominance problem.
- [FACT] Diekmann states it directly: "In contrast to other social traps, a dominant strategy does not exist." (same DOI). His own framing is that this is only "a somewhat different kind of" prisoner's dilemma, not one of them.
- [FACT] Diekmann's account of the equilibrium set, verified from the paper's abstract and full text: the pure-strategy equilibria are the n asymmetric profiles in which exactly one player volunteers and the remaining n minus one players free-ride, and these require coordination. The only symmetric equilibrium is in mixed strategies, and it "yields very low payoffs": "the mixed-equilibrium strategy yields very low payoffs. The expected gain is not greater than the payoff achieved by the nonequilibrium maximin strategy." (same DOI)
- [INTERPRETATION] So the VOD's equilibria are a mix of one volunteer and n minus one free riders, and selecting the volunteer is the coordination problem the theory leaves open. This is the structural reason the VOD, not the prisoner's dilemma, models any situation where the good is indivisible and the cost falls on whoever moves.
- [INTERPRETATION] The standard social-dilemma definition Diekmann works against, and rejects for this game, is: a dominant strategy exists, and the intersection of dominant strategies is a Pareto-deficient equilibrium. The VOD satisfies only the second clause, and only partially (the all-abstain outcome is inefficient, but no dominant strategies point to it).
- [BACKGROUND] Where this sits beside chicken and the stag hunt, deferring the formal family to [[prisoners-dilemma-anatomy]], which already carries chicken as the two-player escalation game and the payoff-order family:
  - Chicken: the two-player anti-coordination game in which each wants the other to be the one who backs down, and mutual defection (the crash) is worst for both. The VOD is chicken's n-player extension in this respect: in both, a player's preferred move is conditional on the other's.
  - Dissimilarity from chicken: in chicken the worst cell is mutual defection, whereas in the VOD the worst cell is mutual abstention, and the costly volunteer still does better than the loser of chicken.
  - Stag hunt: a different animal, an assurance problem with two symmetric equilibria (all-stag and all-hare), where the failure mode is mutual risk-aversion rather than mutual abstention.
- [INTERPRETATION] The summary placement: the VOD is the multiperson anti-coordination extension of chicken, with a different catastrophic cell, and it is a coordination-failure problem distinct from the assurance problem of the stag hunt. This file only adds the multiperson branch; the two-player family stays with [[prisoners-dilemma-anatomy]].

## 3. The founding paper and its own experimental results

- [FACT] Primary source: Andreas Diekmann, "Volunteer's Dilemma", Journal of Conflict Resolution 29(4), December 1985, pages 605 to 610, SAGE, DOI 10.1177/0022002785029004003. Title, volume, issue, page range, and date confirmed at the publisher DOI, at JSTOR (https://jstor.org/stable/174243), and at RePEc (https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/jocore/v29y1985i4p605-610.html).
- [FACT] The 1985 article is theoretical and reports no experiment. The verified abstract describes a game "in which a collective good can be provided by a volunteer", states that "some solution concepts are analyzed", and closes with a research proposal rather than a result: "the conflicting predictions of the different solution concepts can be regarded as rival hypotheses that should be investigated in cross-experiments" (same DOI).
- [FACT] Diekmann credits the psychology directly. The paper states that the all-defect outcome "is frequently produced by 'diffusion of responsibility' as described by Darley and Latane (1968)" (same DOI). The bridge from the game to the bystander literature is Diekmann's own, not a later graft.
- [FACT] Diekmann flags Hofstadter's superrationality as a possible route out of the trap while doubting it: "Superrationality might be a way out of the trap. However, this concept lacks the desirable equilibrium property. Only in the free communication version of the game can a definitive answer to the question of a rational strategy be given." (same DOI). This is the lineage the Poundstone notebook also records under Nigel Howard's metagame, where the unit of strategy is an intention rather than an action.
- [FACT] Diekmann's own experimental results are in a companion chapter, not in the 1985 article: Diekmann, A. (1986), "Volunteer's Dilemma. A Social Trap without a Dominant Strategy and some Empirical Results", in A. Diekmann and P. Mitter (eds.), Paradoxical Effects of Social Behavior, Physica-Verlag (https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/978-3-642-95874-8_13). The chapter title itself asserts empirical results.
- [UNVERIFIED] What the 1986 chapter's experiments actually found: design, sample, and measured group-size effect. Could not be retrieved within the six-call budget, because the Springer page returns only the citation and its reference list, not the abstract text or body. This is the single most important gap in this file, because the question "what did Diekmann's own experiments find" cannot be answered from the 1985 paper, which reports none.
- [BACKGROUND] The "social trap" vocabulary the paper uses traces to Platt, "Social Traps" (1973, American Psychologist) and to Dawes (1975) on social dilemmas, which Diekmann cites for the standard definition. [UNVERIFIED] Exact volume and page for Platt 1973 and Dawes 1975 were not re-verified.

## 4. The group-size prediction: theory

- [FACT] The theoretical prediction, as the later literature restates it: the symmetric mixed-strategy equilibrium propensity to volunteer is a strictly decreasing function of group size. A peer-reviewed restatement: "The equilibrium tendency to volunteer is a decreasing function of group size, which is known as the 'bystander effect' or 'diffusion of responsibility' from the social psychology literature (Diekmann 1985; Goeree, Holt, and Smith 2017)." (https://academic.oup.com/jpart/article/32/1/217/6290901)
- [BACKGROUND] The intra-model mechanism: with more players, each can raise its subjective probability that someone else will act, so each can lower its own volunteering probability while still expecting the good to be produced. More players means more chances to free-ride successfully.
- [BACKGROUND] On the standard binary parameterization, the equilibrium individual volunteer probability is p = 1 minus (C/V) raised to the power 1/(n minus 1), which is decreasing in n, and the probability that nobody volunteers is (1 minus p) raised to n, which is increasing in n. So provision probability falls with group size and approaches a floor, 1 minus C/V. This arithmetic is stated as standard theory, not re-derived from Diekmann's text.
- [INTERPRETATION] The precise group-level prediction is parameterization-dependent, which is one root of the empirical disagreement below. The robust, parameter-free theoretical claim is only about the individual propensity. That is exactly the point at which the textbook one-liner ("more people, less likely anyone helps") starts to outrun the model, because the slogan asserts the individual claim about a group outcome.
- [INTERPRETATION] A well-posed follow-up note: a 2025 working paper observes that the group's probability of success "may be strictly decreasing as the size of the group increases" (Battaglini, "Welfare in the Volunteer's Dilemma", http://www.mbattaglini.com/s/VolunteerDilemma_25Mar2025.pdf). The hedge "may" is the honest word: the sign of the group-level effect is not parameter-free.

## 5. The group-size prediction: empirical status

- [FACT] The empirical record is genuinely mixed, and its pieces cut in different directions. Four verified results, ordered by how far each departs from the simple prediction:
- [FACT] Goeree, Holt, and Smith (2017), Games and Economic Behavior 102, pages 303 to 315: "Observed volunteer rates are lower with larger groups, as predicted, but the incidence of no-volunteer outcomes declines with group size, in contrast to theory." (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/gamebe/v102y2017icp303-315.html). Individual propensity falls as the theory says, yet the group actually succeeds more often in larger groups, which is the opposite of the naive diffusion-of-responsibility story.
- [FACT] Kopanyi-Peuker (2019), Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics 80, pages 211 to 218: a one-shot VOD with group sizes from 3 to about 100 found "non-monotonic group size effect on the individual volunteering decisions", no monotonic decline, systematic over-volunteering relative to symmetric mixed Nash, and a cost effect only in the smallest groups of 3 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214804318305147).
- [FACT] A study of remote workers in Experimental Economics: volunteering rates "do not vary in the group size", statistically indistinguishable across team sizes (all p greater than 0.1), even though participants' beliefs about others' volunteering were sensitive to group size and 93 percent correctly recalled their group size; the authors call the insensitivity to team size their main and surprising result. (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/willingness-to-volunteer-among-remote-workers-is-insensitive-to-the-team-size/D0656828614BDE0EC7FC83748FCC3A60)
- [FACT] Przepiorka and Diekmann (2018), PLOS ONE 13(11): e0208129, modelling norm enforcement as a VOD: in the symmetric VOD "subjects' propensities to sanction the norm breaker decrease with group size", consistent with diffusion of responsibility, but in an asymmetric VOD where one strong actor has lower costs "groups tacitly coordinate on the strong subject to sanction the norm breaker alone", and that actor's rate converges upward across group sizes (https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0208129).
- [INTERPRETATION] How to state the empirical status carefully. Three separate claims must be kept apart. (1) The individual propensity to volunteer falls with group size: supported by Goeree, Holt, and Smith and by Przepiorka and Diekmann's symmetric condition, but not by Kopanyi-Peuker (non-monotonic) or by the remote-worker study (flat). (2) The group's probability of provision falls with group size: contradicted where it is measured, since Goeree, Holt, and Smith find no-volunteer outcomes declining with n. (3) Real-world aggregate helping falls as crowds grow: contradicted by the CCTV evidence in section 6.
- [INTERPRETATION] The claim that survives across the whole record is the weakest one: in some settings and some group sizes, individuals volunteer less when the group is larger. The strong textbook claim, that diffusion of responsibility means bigger groups help less, is a simplification the experiments do not cleanly support. It should never be stated more strongly than the individual-propensity claim, and even that has counterexamples.
- [INTERPRETATION] Why the disagreement is not simply error. The VOD has at least four degrees of freedom the experiments vary independently: whether play is one-shot or repeated; whether costs are symmetric or heterogeneous; how the collective-good production function is specified (best-shot versus weakest-link versus threshold); and whether players hold correct beliefs about n. Diekmann's own 1985 paper anticipated exactly this by calling the rival solution concepts "rival hypotheses" for cross-experiments.
- [INTERPRETATION] Heterogeneous costs in particular (the asymmetric VOD) dissolve the symmetric prediction, because groups coordinate on the naturally cheapest volunteer rather than diffusing responsibility. That is the mechanism behind the Przepiorka and Diekmann result.
- [INTERPRETATION] The production function is the through-line. A PLOS ONE discussion of the field notes that "the literature is less equivocal in its concluding that the production function of a collective good is a major factor driving the group size effect" (same Przepiorka and Diekmann URL). The VOD is one specific production function, the one-volunteer best-shot case; where the good is additive instead, the group-size prediction can reverse. That reversal is the bridge to Pamela Oliver's critical-mass program in section 6.
- [FACT] The experiments above vary group size over very different ranges, which may itself explain part of the disagreement. Goeree, Holt, and Smith ran "groups of 2, 3, 6, 9, 12" (https://papers.tinbergen.nl/18072.pdf), while Kopanyi-Peuker went "from groups of 3 to about 100" (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214804318305147). [INTERPRETATION] So "large group" means 12 in one study and 100 in another, and a single monotone function of n is unlikely to fit both regimes.
- [INTERPRETATION] A model-side nuance worth flagging for synthesis: as n grows large under symmetric costs, the equilibrium per-player volunteer probability falls toward zero while the expected number of volunteers tends toward a constant, so a very large group can behave like a lottery in which almost no one expects to be the volunteer and almost everyone expects someone else to be. This is the mathematical face of the bystander story, and it is also why the remote-worker and Kopanyi-Peuker subjects, who keep volunteering at roughly constant rates, are so far from the symmetric prediction.

## 6. The neighbouring literature

- [BACKGROUND] The bystander effect, the empirical seed Diekmann cites. Darley and Latane (1968) reported that the presence of other witnesses reduces any given individual's likelihood of intervening in an emergency, and framed it as diffusion of responsibility. The research was prompted by the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in Kew Gardens, Queens. The 1968 result is recorded as Darley, J. M., and Latane, B., "Bystander intervention in emergencies: Diffusion of responsibility", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 8(4, Pt. 1), 377 to 383.
- [BACKGROUND] The originating anecdote is itself noisy, and the task's warning about that record is well taken. The "38 witnesses" figure, from a 1964 New York Times report by Martin Gansberg and repeated for decades, has been substantially disputed by later reconstruction (Rasenberger, Kitty Genovese, 2014), and the number should not be quoted as established fact.
- [FACT] The bystander effect's own replication status is contested and must be stated precisely rather than roundly. The largest meta-analysis pooled "over 7,700 participants and 105 independent effect sizes" and found "an overall effect size of g = -0.35", with the effect attenuated when the situation was perceived as dangerous, when a perpetrator was present, and when intervention costs were physical: Fischer, P., Krueger, J. I., Greitemeyer, T., Vogrincic, C., Kastenmuller, A., Frey, D., and others (2011), "The bystander-effect: A meta-analytic review on bystander intervention in dangerous and non-dangerous emergencies", Psychological Bulletin 137, pages 517 to 537, DOI 10.1037/a0023304 (https://www.ovid.com/journals/plbul/fulltext/10.1037/a0023304~the-bystander-effect-a-meta-analytic-review-on-bystander).
- [INTERPRETATION] So the individual-level effect is real on average but conditional, not the uniform suppression the popular telling implies. The effect shrinks precisely in the dangerous, perpetrator-present cases that dominate the popular image of a bystander emergency, which is a further reason not to cite the effect as a general law.
- [FACT] The strongest direct challenge comes from real-world CCTV rather than the lab: Philpot, R., Liebst, L. S., Levine, M., Bernasco, W., and Lindegaard, M. R. (2020), "Would I be helped? Cross-national CCTV footage shows that intervention is the norm in public conflicts", American Psychologist 75(1), pages 66 to 75, DOI 10.1037/amp0000469 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31157529).
- [FACT] Across 219 arguments and assaults in Amsterdam, Lancaster, and Cape Town, at least one bystander intervened in roughly nine of ten conflicts, and "with increasing numbers of bystanders there is a greater likelihood that at least someone will intervene to help" (https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/sci-tech/about-us/news/bystanders-will-intervene-to-help-victims-of-aggressive-public-disputes). This is the aggregate question the lab literature neglected, and on it the group-size effect reverses sign: the more bystanders, the more likely someone acts.
- [INTERPRETATION] Read together, the individual-level and aggregate-level findings are not in contradiction. A falling individual propensity and a more numerous crowd can coexist, and the aggregate can still rise: n players each helping with a smaller probability can yield a larger probability that at least one helps. The synthesis is that the popular "diffusion of responsibility means crowds fail" claim conflates the individual propensity with the aggregate outcome, and the interval over which the individual propensity actually falls is narrower than the slogan. The Philpot et al. result is the cleanest single correction and should be carried into any Substrate use of the bystander phenomenon.
- [BACKGROUND] Pamela Oliver and colleagues on critical mass. Where the VOD assumes homogeneous players and a one-volunteer production function, the critical-mass program (Marwell and Oliver and colleagues, mid-to-late 1980s, the core papers running in the American Journal of Sociology and the American Sociological Review; Oliver's 1993 "Formal Models of Collective Action", Annual Review of Sociology, is the survey) argues that real collective action is produced by a small critical mass of highly interested or low-cost contributors.
- [BACKGROUND] Two critical-mass claims matter here. First, heterogeneity in interests and resources is the normal case, not a deviation. Second, larger groups can raise rather than lower the expected level of collective action, because they contain more of the highly motivated, so the naive monotone group-size prediction can reverse.
- [UNVERIFIED] The exact volume and page numbers of the individual critical-mass papers were not re-verified this session and should not be cited until checked. The substance above is established record; the citations are provisional.
- [INTERPRETATION] Where the critical-mass view and the VOD meet: they are the two ends of the heterogeneity axis. With homogeneous players and a best-shot good, the VOD's group-size effect is negative. Introduce heterogeneous costs or interests and the logic flips toward critical mass, which is exactly what Przepiorka and Diekmann's asymmetric condition demonstrates experimentally, and what the PLOS ONE remark about the production function as the driver of the group-size effect is gesturing at.
- [FACT] Threshold public goods, the n-player generalization the VOD specialises. Palfrey, T. R., and Rosenthal, H. (1984), "Participation and the provision of discrete public goods: a strategic analysis", Journal of Public Economics 24(2), pages 171 to 193, DOI 10.1016/0047-2727(84)90023-9 (https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2727(84)90023-9).
- [FACT] The abstract: "a discrete public good is to be provided. Each individual may participate by making a fixed contribution. If a sufficient number of contributions are made, the good is provided. Otherwise, the good is not provided. One variant of the rules allows for contributions to be refunded when the good is not provided." (https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/2vnat-k7q66). The VOD is the threshold case with the threshold equal to one and the contribution equal to the whole private cost.

## 8. Real-world instances, with named sources

- [BACKGROUND] Whistle-blowing. An employee who observes illegal conduct provides a public good (the misconduct is stopped, the organisation is corrected) at a private cost (retaliation, dismissal, legal exposure), and each insider prefers that someone else be the one to report. The structure is a VOD whenever the good (the report) is indivisible and one reporter suffices.
- [BACKGROUND] Two institutional responses convert the anonymous volunteer into a named one: bounty and protection statutes, and antitrust leniency programs that give the first cooperating cartel member amnesty. The U.S. Department of Justice Antitrust Division Leniency Program is the canonical example (the first firm to self-report and cooperate escapes criminal penalties), and the Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) and Dodd-Frank (2010) whistleblower provisions are the securities-law analogues. [UNVERIFIED] Exact dates and program mechanics are established record but were not re-verified this session.
- [FACT] Who calls for help, in public emergencies. The CCTV evidence in section 6 is the strongest named source: Philpot et al. 2020 is the only large-scale measurement of who actually intervenes in real conflicts, and it shows intervention is the norm rather than the exception (nine in ten conflicts). The historical anchor is the Genovese case (section 6), whose witness count is disputed.
- [INTERPRETATION] Which firm moves first on a public good. Standard-setting, voluntary environmental abatement, and open-source release each have the VOD shape: the first mover bears the transition cost (building to a non-incumbent standard, cleaning up before regulation, publishing a codebase) and every rival prefers to free-ride on the resulting public good. The mover's private cost is the volunteer's cost, and the coordination problem is which firm is the cheapest volunteer, an asymmetric VOD in which the leader with scale or reputation shoulders it.
- [BACKGROUND] Who maintains an unpaid open-source dependency. A critical library maintained by one unpaid volunteer, used by thousands of firms, is a textbook VOD: the maintenance is a public good, the maintainer bears the private cost, and every downstream user free-rides in the hope that someone else, or the maintainer's goodwill, covers it.
- [INTERPRETATION] Who takes the night's on-call, and who escalates a stalled pipeline. The workplace version of the VOD is now studied explicitly: the remote-worker experiment is set precisely here, where employers allocate a discretionary extra task by asking for volunteers rather than by assignment, and its finding of flat volunteering across team sizes is the field-relevant one.
- [BACKGROUND] The named artifacts: the xkcd strip on dependency (xkcd 2347) and Nadia Eghbal's Working in Public (2020), which describes exactly this structure. The named incidents where the unpaid maintainer was the single point of failure are the OpenSSL Heartbleed episode (2014, CVE-2014-0160) and the Log4Shell vulnerability in log4j (2021, CVE-2021-44228). [UNVERIFIED] The book and the strip are established record; the CVE identifiers are established record and were not re-verified this session.
- [INTERPRETATION] The cartel member who must be first to defect openly. Two readings. First, to break a tacit price agreement someone must be the first to cut price openly, which invites retaliation and detection; each member prefers a rival move first so it can follow without paying the leader's cost. Second, to have the cartel prosecuted one member must be the first to cooperate with the authority, which is the whistle-blowing VOD above with antitrust leniency as the institutional fix.
- [BACKGROUND] Both cartel readings are analysis here rather than a single cited study; the leniency-program background is the same DOJ material cited for whistle-blowing.

## 9. Where the mapping breaks

- [INTERPRETATION] The step function is load-bearing. The VOD needs an indivisible good that materialises at one volunteer. If the good is additive in contributions, the game is a standard public-goods game, defection is closer to dominant, and the group-size prediction can reverse into the critical-mass regime. Treating every collective-action problem as a VOD is the same over-application Poundstone warns against when he writes that "most conflicts are not prisoner's dilemmas" (p. 129).
- [INTERPRETATION] Symmetric costs are the assumption that does the most silent work. The neat mixed equilibrium, and therefore the whole group-size prediction, assumes identical players and common knowledge of costs. The asymmetric VOD dissolves it, and Przepiorka and Diekmann's 2018 result (groups coordinate on the cheapest volunteer) is the demonstration. Any real instance with visibly heterogeneous players is closer to an asymmetric VOD than to the textbook symmetric one.
- [INTERPRETATION] Bystander emergencies are not clean one-shot static games. Real emergencies involve ambiguity (is this an emergency?), sequential and dynamic moves, and social signals, and the Philpot et al. aggregate result shows the static model mispredicts the collective outcome. Mapping the bystander phenomenon onto the VOD as a static n-player matrix loses the interpretation stage that Darley and Latane's own model had.
- [INTERPRETATION] Repetition and reputation are missing from the base model. Most real instances (the maintainer, the firm, the insider) are repeated, and repetition creates the shadow-of-the-future incentives the iterated literature documents, which a one-shot VOD ignores. The one-shot VOD is the boundary case, not the generic one.
- [INTERPRETATION] The no-dominant-strategy claim depends on the volunteer receiving V too. If the volunteer gains nothing personally, the game collapses toward a threshold public good with a strictly worse volunteer payoff, and the volunteer becomes an altruist rather than a self-interested player. Diekmann's prisoners example is carefully built so the volunteer profits (nine years less), which is what keeps the game a dilemma for a self-interested player rather than a pure sacrifice.
- [INTERPRETATION] The named instances often have a designated volunteer that the abstract game lacks. A fire service, a police force, an on-call rota, a maintenance team: these are institutions that resolve the coordination problem by assignment rather than by equilibrium. That is why [[protocols-coordination-institutional-design]] and [[principal-agent-theory]] are the right neighbours: the VOD describes the problem an institution exists to solve.
- [INTERPRETATION] Where the VOD adds value beyond the prisoner's dilemma for the Substrate's own work: any agent fleet in which one member must spend resources to unblock the rest (take a lock, run the recovery, escalate to the operator, pay the one-off cost) faces the VOD, not the prisoner's dilemma, and the failure mode is that every member waits. The institutional fix is a designated volunteer or a cost-sharing rule, which is why the assignment literature is the right neighbour. [INTERPRETATION]
- [UNVERIFIED] The 1986 experimental results, again, are the one place where this file cannot check its own central empirical question. Everything in section 5 about Diekmann's own findings is inference from the 1985 paper's theory plus the later literature; it is not read from the 1986 chapter.

## 10. Compression points for synthesis

1. The VOD is an n-player discrete-public-good game in which one volunteer produces the good for all and pays a private cost, with the ordinal ranking free-ride (V), then volunteer (V minus C), then nobody-volunteers (0) for every player, so there is no dominant strategy and no symmetric pure-strategy equilibrium. [BACKGROUND plus INTERPRETATION]
2. It is not a prisoner's dilemma because the prisoner's dilemma turns on a strictly dominant strategy (defect) and a unique deficient equilibrium, whereas the VOD turns on conditional best responses and a coordination requirement; Diekmann states outright that no dominant strategy exists. [FACT for the statement, INTERPRETATION for the contrast]
3. The equilibria are the n asymmetric pure profiles of one volunteer and n minus one free riders, plus one mixed symmetric equilibrium that Diekmann found yields payoffs no better than maximin; selecting the volunteer is the coordination problem the theory leaves open. [FACT]
4. Diekmann credits Darley and Latane (1968) for the diffusion-of-responsibility link; the bridge from game theory to the bystander literature is his own. [FACT]
5. Diekmann's 1985 Journal of Conflict Resolution article is theoretical and reports no experiment; his experiments are in the 1986 chapter in Paradoxical Effects of Social Behavior, whose results could not be verified here and remain the file's key gap. [FACT for the split, UNVERIFIED for the chapter's content]
6. The theoretical prediction is that the individual propensity to volunteer falls with group size; the group-level and real-world versions of the claim are both parameterization-dependent and empirically contested, so the slogan should never be stated more strongly than the individual-propensity claim. [FACT for the prediction, INTERPRETATION for the caution]
7. The empirical record is mixed and cuts both ways: Goeree, Holt, and Smith (2017) find individual rates falling but no-volunteer outcomes also falling, contrary to theory; Kopanyi-Peuker (2019) finds no monotonic group-size effect and over-volunteering; a remote-worker study finds volunteering flat in team size; Przepiorka and Diekmann (2018) find the symmetric effect but its dissolution under heterogeneous costs. [FACT]
8. The bystander effect itself is real but conditional: Fischer et al. (2011) give g = -0.35 over 105 effect sizes, attenuated for danger, perpetrator presence, and physical cost; the effect is not the uniform suppression of the popular telling. [FACT]
9. The strongest correction is aggregate: Philpot et al. (2020), on 219 real CCTV conflicts, find intervention in about nine of ten, rising with the number of bystanders, which reverses the sign of the naive group-size story at the level victims care about. [FACT]
10. Palfrey and Rosenthal (1984) supplied the general discrete-threshold public-good game; the VOD is its threshold-equals-one and full-cost-contribution special case. [FACT]
11. Pamela Oliver's critical-mass program is the heterogeneity counterpart: with unequal interests and resources, larger groups can raise collective action rather than lower it, which is the regime the symmetric VOD misses. [BACKGROUND, citations provisional]
12. Beside chicken and the stag hunt: the VOD is the n-player anti-coordination extension of chicken, differs from it in which cell is catastrophic, and differs from the stag hunt because its efficient outcome is strictly asymmetric (one volunteer), a feature neither of the others has. Defer the two-player family to [[prisoners-dilemma-anatomy]]. [INTERPRETATION]
13. Named instances: whistle-blowing and antitrust leniency (the DOJ Leniency Program as the institutional fix for the anonymous-volunteer problem), who calls for help (Philpot et al. 2020 as the measured case), which firm moves first on a public good, the unpaid open-source maintainer (xkcd 2347; Eghbal, Working in Public; Heartbleed 2014; Log4Shell 2021), and the cartel member who must be first to defect openly. [BACKGROUND and INTERPRETATION]
14. Where the mapping breaks: indivisibility is load-bearing; symmetric costs are the silent assumption; real emergencies are dynamic and ambiguous; repetition is missing from the base model; the no-dominant-strategy property requires the volunteer to profit; and real instances usually have a designated volunteer that the abstract game lacks. [INTERPRETATION]
15. Poundstone supplies the naming and the network position ("the volunteer's dilemma", p. 201; chicken's anti-coordination property, p. 199; the mirror relation to the largest-number game, p. 275), and his one VOD-adjacent line on group size (p. 220) is a general dilemma remark, not a VOD-specific claim. [FACT]

## Summary

This file gives the Substrate its first node on the volunteer's dilemma, distinct from the prisoner's dilemma series: a game with no dominant strategy whose equilibria are one volunteer plus n minus one free riders. It verifies the Diekmann 1985 primary source structurally and transcribes its key claims, and it states the group-size prediction three ways, careful to separate the individual-propensity claim from the group-level and real-world claims the experiments contest. It reports the mixed empirical record (Goeree, Holt, and Smith; Kopanyi-Peuker; Przepiorka and Diekmann; the remote-worker study) and the corrected bystander record (Fischer et al. 2011; Philpot et al. 2020) without overclaiming. It plants the neighbour set (bystander effect, critical mass, threshold public goods) and the real instances with named sources. It could not verify Diekmann's own 1986 experimental results, the Oliver critical-mass citations, or the remote-worker study's bibliographic details.
