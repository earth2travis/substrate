# Grove's OKRs: Origins at Intel and Documentation in High Output Management (1983)

## Origins

Andrew Grove introduced the approach that became known as Objectives and Key Results (OKR) at Intel in the 1970s. He documented it in his 1983 book *High Output Management*. At Intel, the system was originally called "iMBOs" — Intel Management by Objectives. John Doerr, then a salesperson at Intel, attended a course taught by Grove in 1975 where he was introduced to the theory.

Grove on management: "The key result has to be measurable. But at the end you can look, and without any arguments: Did I do that or did I not do it? Yes? No? Simple. No judgments in it."

## The Mechanics

An OKR comprises:
- **Objective**: A significant, concrete, clearly defined, inspirational goal.
- **Key Results**: 3–5 measurable success criteria (0–100% scale or numerical value: count, dollar amount, percentage) used to track achievement of the goal. No grey area.
- **Initiatives**: Plans and activities that help move forward the key results and achieve the objective.

Target success rate for key results: approximately 70%. A 70% success rate encourages competitive goal-making meant to stretch workers at low risk. If 100% of key results are consistently being met, the key results should be reevaluated. OKRs are scored 0.0 to 1.0, with 0.7 as the normal target for "aspirational" KRs and 1.0 for "committed" KRs (binary done/not done, deadline delivery).

## Key Differences from Traditional MBO

- Set at various levels (company, team, individual) rather than just manager-employee.
- Objectives are usually ambitious and may not always be fully achieved (~70% expected).
- Typically public and transparent within the organization.
- Not directly tied to compensation or performance review.

## The Spread: Intel → Google → Industry

Doerr, by 1999 at Kleiner Perkins, introduced OKRs to Google in 1999. Larry Page credited OKRs with leading Google to 10x growth. The system became central to Google's culture as a "management methodology that helps to ensure that the company focuses efforts on the same important issues throughout the organization."

Subsequent adopters: LinkedIn, Twitter, Uber, Microsoft, GitLab, and many others.

## High Output Management as Cult Classic

*High Output Management* was not a bestseller in the 1980s or 1990s. It became a cult classic in Silicon Valley decades later. Ben Horowitz described it as having "almost legendary status," with top venture capitalists giving copies to their entrepreneurs. It is frequently praised by Mark Zuckerberg, Evan Williams, Brian Chesky, and Drew Houston.

The book introduced Grove's "management by objective" approach, discusses the importance of measurable processes, performance reviews, and trainings, and has been described as a "crash course for middle managers."

## Examples from Industry

- **Snap, Inc. 2019**: O = "Invest in the root if you want the fruit." KRs = "Make Snapchat the fastest way to communicate," "Find best friends for all Snapchatters," "Achieve full year profitability," "Lead the way in augmented reality," "Spread positivity."
- **Allbirds**: O = "Create the lowest carbon footprint in our industry." KRs = zero-waste shipping, 100% carbon offset, 25% compostable material, 75% biodegradable material.
- **Beam Dental**: O = "Scale Services to Improve Margins." KRs = delight broker partners, increase broker acquisition efficiency, launch new dental benefits product.

## Failure Modes and Criticism

- **Individual OKRs as task lists**: Most organizations no longer define OKRs for individual contributors as these tend to look like a task list and lead to conflating OKRs with performance reviews.
- **Waterfall cascade**: Creating OKRs at multiple levels may cause too much of a waterfall approach, something that OKRs in many ways intend to avoid.
- **Metric gaming / Goodhart's Law**: Teams optimize for the measurable KR at the expense of the meaningful O.
- **Business as usual**: OKRs should not represent business as usual; they are meant to be action-oriented and inspirational.
- **Vague verbs**: Words like "help" and "consult" should be avoided as they describe vague activities rather than concrete outcomes.
- **Lagging vs. leading indicators**: Measuring lagging indicators prevents course-correction. Leading indicators are preferred.
