# Missio Dei: Mission as God's Action, Not the Church's Property

## Origins

The term Missio Dei (Latin: "mission of God") was coined by the German Lutheran missiologist Karl Hartenstein in 1934, at the Brandenburg Mission Conference. Hartenstein introduced it to distinguish the mission of God from the missio ecclesiae (mission of the church), reacting to the perceived failure of traditional missiology after World War I.

Some scholars trace the conceptual roots to Augustine, but Hartenstein gave it modern form.

## Development

The concept gained theological depth at the 1952 Willingen conference of the International Missionary Council (IMC). Lutheran theologian Georg Vicedom developed it further. However, John Flett argues that Hartenstein did not initially ground the concept in the Trinity; that development came from the "American report" led by Paul Lehmann and H. Richard Niebuhr.

## David Bosch's Formulation

According to David J. Bosch, "mission is not primarily an activity of the church, but an attribute of God. God is a missionary God." This shifted the locus of mission from human institution to divine action.

Juergen Moltmann expressed it: "It is not the church that has a mission of salvation to fulfill in the world; it is the mission of the Son and the Spirit through the Father that includes the church."

## Key Theological Shift

Missio Dei reversed the direction of agency. Previously, the church sent missionaries to the world. Under Missio Dei:
- God is already active in the world
- The church is invited to participate in what God is already doing
- There is church because there is mission, not vice versa
- Mission is a movement from God to the world; the church is an instrument

## Vatican II and Catholic Convergence

The Catholic Church formally adopted a similar outlook in "Ad Gentes" (1965), affirming that the Church serves the mission of God rather than owning it. This represented convergence between Protestant and Catholic missiology after centuries of divergence.

## Later Development

Lesslie Newbigin (1909–1998), British missionary to India, wrote extensively on mission in post-Christian Western contexts, including "The Gospel in a Pluralist Society" (1989). He asked how the Church could be missionary in its own culture, not just in "foreign" lands.

Darrell Guder and the Gospel and Our Culture Network pushed the concept into "missional church" theology: the church's authentic organizing principle is mission. When the church is in mission, it is the true church.

## Conceptual Implications

Missio Dei shifted mission from geography to structures:
- From sending to Christian lands → non-Christian lands
- To engaging secular, urban, and post-Christian contexts everywhere
- It undergirded liberation theology and contemporary missional church movements
- It answered the colonial critique: mission is not Western export but participation in God's universal action
