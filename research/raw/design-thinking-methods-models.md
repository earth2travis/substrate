## 1. The claim under examination

INTERPRETATION: each process model is a codification of the same underlying rhythm (divergent exploration, convergent selection, iterative prototyping) packaged for a different institution. The differences between them are mostly packaging and governance, not mechanics. This file tests that by laying the models side by side.

2. IDEO's human-centered design framing

## 3. Stanford d.school Five-Stage Model

[BACKGROUND] The Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford, known as the d.school, was founded in 2005 with David Kelley as its central figure and funding from SAP cofounder Hasso Plattner.

[FACT] The d.school's canonical process model names five stages: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test.

[FACT] The model is distributed in the d.school's public teaching document "Bootcamp Bootleg," a working deck of methods and mindsets used in the d.school bootcamp course and freely circulated as a PDF.

[FACT] The d.school visualizes the five stages as a row of interlocking hexagons labeled "the design thinking process," with each stage occupying one hexagon.

[FACT] The d.school's own materials state explicitly that the stages are not sequential. The Bootcamp Bootleg frames the stages as modes a team moves between iteratively, not a linear pipeline.

[INTERPRETATION] Despite the stated non-sequential caveat, the hexagon visual is almost universally reproduced as a left-to-right sequence, which is how it is taught in corporate workshops. The caveat and the visual are in tension, and the visual wins. This tension is the seed of the academic critique in section 7.

## 4. IDEO Human-Centered Design

[BACKGROUND] IDEO is the Palo Alto design consultancy founded in 1991 from the merger of David Kelley Design, ID Two, and Matrix Product Design. It is the single most influential popularizer of design thinking as a business practice.

[FACT] IDEO frames innovation through three lenses: Desirability (what people want), Feasibility (what is technically and organizationally possible), and Viability (what a sustainable business can support). Innovation sits at the overlap of the three circles.

[FACT] Tim Brown, IDEO's longtime CEO, described the process with the triad "inspiration, ideation, implementation" in his 2008 Harvard Business Review article "Design Thinking" and his 2009 book "Change by Design."

[FACT] IDEO published the "Human-Centered Design Field Guide" and operates the Design Kit platform (designkit.org), which packages its methods for social sector and general use. The Field Guide was released with support from the Rockefeller Foundation and later reissued through Design Kit.

[FACT] In 1999, ABC's Nightline aired the segment "The Deep Dive," in which Ted Koppel's team challenged IDEO to redesign the shopping cart in five days. The segment, reported by Jack Smith, showed IDEO's multidisciplinary team brainstorming, prototyping, and testing under deadline. Verified live this session via IDEO's own retrospective and the archived transcript. (One transcript archive records February 9, 1999; IDEO's own journal and TV listings give July 13, 1999. The year 1999 is certain; the exact air date has conflicting listings.)

[INTERPRETATION] The Nightline segment did more to make design process visible to executives than any white paper. It compressed the IDEO method into a watchable five-day drama: messy collaboration, rapid prototyping, "fail often to succeed sooner." Every later codification, from the d.school bootcamp to the GV sprint, inherits this televised template.

## 5. UK Design Council Double Diamond

[FACT] The Double Diamond was created in 2003 by the Design Council's in-house Design and Innovation team under Richard Eisermann, then Director of Design and Innovation, in response to the question "How do we describe design process?" It was first shared publicly at conferences in 2004. Verified live this session against the Design Council's own history page.

[FACT] The model has four phases named as a memorable device: Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver. Discover and Develop are divergent; Define and Deliver are convergent. Two diamonds: one on the problem, one on the solution.

[FACT] The frequently repeated company list (Alessi, BSkyB, BT, LEGO, Microsoft, Sony, Starbucks, Virgin Atlantic, Whirlpool, Xerox) comes from the Design Council study "Eleven Lessons: Managing Design in Eleven Global Brands." Verified live this session: the eleven-brand study exists and LEGO, Microsoft, Sony, Starbucks, and Virgin Atlantic are confirmed by name in sources citing it; the Design Council structured its 2007 report around the Double Diamond.

[INTERPRETATION] Popular accounts fuse two documents into one origin story: the 2003 to 2004 model and the eleven-brand study (published as "11 Lessons" in 2007). The model did not originate from the company study; it appeared in that study as a framing device. The underlying divergence-convergence rhythm predates both, traceable to Bela Banathy's 1996 "Designing Social Systems in a Changing World." Treat "the Double Diamond came from studying eleven companies in 2004" as a marketed simplification, not the documented record.

[FACT] In 2019 the Design Council published the Systemic Design Framework, an update that embeds the Double Diamond in a larger system with six principles and adds considerations of sustainability, circularity, and place. Cited from the established record; not re-verified this session.

[INTERPRETATION] The the Double Diamond is structurally a process map, which makes it adjacent to [[value-stream-mapping]]: both externalize an invisible workflow so a group can reason about it. It is also a diverge-converge cousin of the [[pdca-cycle]]: Plan maps loosely to Discover and Define, Do and Check to Develop and Deliver.

## 6. IBM Enterprise Design Thinking

[FACT] IBM announced the formation of IBM Design in 2012, with Phil Gilbert as General Manager of Design. Verified live this session via IBM's own jobs blog and a 2021 tribute by IBM design executive Karel Vredenburg.

[FACT] Gilbert came to IBM through the 2010 acquisition of Lombardi Software, which he led. He introduced a version of design thinking inside his business unit, then was asked by CEO Ginni Rometty to scale it across IBM. Verified live this session via Vredenburg's account.

[FACT] A new release of IBM Design Thinking shipped in January 2016. In March 2018, Gilbert announced at the Brainstorm Design conference in Singapore that IBM was making Enterprise Design Thinking available to clients, including the digital badge accreditation program. Verified live this session via Fortune, March 7, 2018. The "2016 to 2018 rollout" framing is accurate for the formal framework's externalization; the internal program dates to late 2012.

[FACT] The framework's engine is the Loop: Observe, Reflect, Make, a continuous iteration cycle. Verified live this session via Fortune's description of the "three-part feedback loop."

[FACT] The framework's alignment mechanisms are the Keys: Hills (user-focused mission statements for a release), Playbacks (structured review moments that bring stakeholders into the work), and Sponsor Users (real users embedded with the team). Cited from the established record; not re-verified this session.

[FACT] The program grew IBM's designer and researcher headcount from roughly 230 to over 3,000 and built close to a hundred studios; a badge program accredited tens of thousands of IBMers (figures of 100,000 or more appear in IBM communications). The headcount growth was verified live this session; the exact badged total is cited from the established record and was not re-verified.

[FACT] Gilbert stepped down as GM of Design in 2021; Katrina Alcorn succeeded him. Verified live this session.

[INTERPRETATION] IBM's version is design thinking adapted for an enterprise of 388,000 employees. The Loop replaces the five-stage sequence with a cycle, making it structurally closer to [[pdca-cycle]] than to the d.school hexagons. The Keys are governance machinery bolted onto the method so it survives contact with quarterly planning, sales, and legal.

## 7. LUMA Institute

[FACT] LUMA Institute, founded in Pittsburgh in 2010, organized design methods into a taxonomy built on three categories: Looking, Understanding, Making. The name LUMA is formed from those three words. Verified live this session.

[FACT] The LUMA System comprises 36 methods, twelve per category, marketed as a modular, adaptive collection for practitioners. Harvard Business Review called the LUMA System a "taxonomy of innovation." Verified live this session via Mural's acquisition announcement.

[FACT] Mural announced its acquisition of LUMA Institute on March 28, 2022. Verified live this session via Mural's press release and Computerworld. Note: the research prompt stated 2021; the documented announcement date is March 28, 2022. The 2021 figure is incorrect per the live record.

[INTERPRETATION] LUMA's contribution is taxonomy, not sequence. Where the d.school sells a process shape and IBM sells an operating system, LUMA sells a periodic table: any method can be selected and combined. This makes it the most library-like of the codifications and the easiest to embed in software, which is presumably why a digital whiteboard company bought it.

## 8. Google Ventures Design Sprint

[FACT] Jake Knapp created the design sprint at Google in 2010, then refined it at Google Ventures from 2012 onward with John Zeratsky and Braden Kowitz. Verified live this session.

[FACT] The book "Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days" by Knapp, Zeratsky, and Kowitz was published in 2016 (ISBN 9781501121746). Verified live this session.

[FACT] The sprint is a five-day structured process: map and pick a target (Monday), sketch competing solutions (Tuesday), decide and storyboard (Wednesday), prototype (Thursday), test with five customers (Friday). Cited from the established record; day-by-day structure not re-verified this session.

[FACT] Knapp has described the sprint's lineage as drawing on IDEO's process and the Stanford d.school's teaching. The sprint is a derivative that compresses empathize through test into a fixed week with a decision-maker in the room. Cited from the established record; lineage claims per Knapp's own accounts, not re-verified this session.

[INTERPRETATION] The sprint is the most disciplined and most constrained codification: it trades open-ended iteration for calendar certainty. It is "The Deep Dive" formalized into an agenda, the televised five-day drama turned into a book-length recipe.

## 9. Critique pointer

[BACKGROUND] A substantial academic and critical literature argues that the codified models above are a marketed myth: that real design practice is situated, political, and iterative in ways no stage model captures, and that selling design thinking as a recipe converts it into a management fashion.

[FACT] Natasha Iskander's "Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative and Preserves the Status Quo" (Harvard Business Review, September 2018) argues that design thinking's emphasis on empathy and iteration leaves power structures unexamined and privileges the designer's interpretive authority.

[INTERPRETATION] The Iskander piece belongs to the critique file, not this one. This brief only notes its existence as a pointer. A future Substrate node on design thinking critique should cover Iskander alongside design studies scholarship (Cross, Kimbell, Vinsel) on the gap between process models and observed practice.



## 10. Where the mapping breaks

* The d.school's own materials state the five stages are not sequential, but the hexagon visual is almost universally reproduced and taught as a left-to-right sequence; the caveat and the visual are in tension, and the visual wins.
* The popular Double Diamond origin story fuses two documents: the 2003 to 2004 model (created by the Design Council's in-house team under Richard Eisermann) and the later eleven-brand study (published as "11 Lessons" in 2007). The model did not originate from the company study; it appeared there as a framing device. The divergence-convergence rhythm itself predates both, traceable to Bela Banathy's 1996 work. Treat "the Double Diamond came from studying eleven companies in 2004" as a marketed simplification.
* The LUMA acquisition date: an earlier draft of this research stated 2021; the documented announcement is 28 March 2022 by Mural. The 2021 figure is incorrect per the live record.
* The Nightline Deep Dive air date has conflicting listings (9 February 1999 versus 13 July 1999); the year 1999 is certain, with July 13 the more widely cited listing.

## 11. Compression points for synthesis

1. d.school five stages (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test) ship in the Bootcamp Bootleg with an explicit non-sequential caveat that practice ignores.
2. IDEO's contribution is framing, not sequence: Desirability-Feasibility-Viability lenses and Brown's inspiration-ideation-implementation triad.
3. Double Diamond: created 2003 by the Design Council's in-house team, public from 2004; Discover-Define-Develop-Deliver, divergent-convergent twice; 2019 Systemic Design Framework update.
4. IBM is design thinking adapted for a 388,000-person enterprise: the Loop (Observe, Reflect, Make) plus governance Keys (Hills, Playbacks, Sponsor Users); designer headcount grew roughly 230 to over 3,000 (verified); badged totals of 100,000-plus are IBM self-reports.
5. LUMA sells taxonomy (36 methods under Looking, Understanding, Making), not sequence; acquired by Mural, announced 28 March 2022 (verified).
6. The GV Design Sprint (Knapp 2010 at Google, refined at GV from 2012, "Sprint" book 2016, verified) is the Nightline Deep Dive formalized into a five-day agenda.
7. Structurally: IBM's Loop is closest to [[pdca-cycle]]; the Double Diamond is a process map adjacent to [[value-stream-mapping]]; the d.school model is PDCA with empathy prepended.
