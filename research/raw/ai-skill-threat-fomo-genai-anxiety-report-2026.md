AI Skill Threat, FoMO-AI, and the Human Cost of Generative AI Adoption: A 2026 Research Synthesis

This report compiles and synthesizes findings from multiple 2023-2026 studies on developer and professional anxiety surrounding generative AI (GenAI) tools. It draws from Pluralsight's Developer Success Lab research, peer-reviewed papers on FoMO-AI and job insecurity, arXiv preprints on cognitive load and burnout, student interviews, and a Nature poll of scientists. The central thread is the emergence of "AI Skill Threat" and "FoMO-AI": pervasive fear, anxiety, and worry that current competencies will become obsolete, coupled with social and professional pressure to adopt AI or risk being left behind. These dynamics drive upskilling compulsion, cognitive overload, work-family conflict, knowledge hiding, and equity gaps, often at the expense of well-being and sustainable productivity.

Executive Summary

45% of software developers report experiencing AI Skill Threat, defined as fear, anxiety, and worry that their current competencies will become obsolete as they adapt to AI-assisted coding (Pluralsight, 2023). 74% plan to upskill in AI-assisted coding, but significant equity gaps persist, with lower rates among female and LGBTQ+ developers. Parallel research identifies FoMO-AI as a distinct construct: individuals' fear of missing out on the benefits of AI technology and of being rendered obsolete by others or by society. This fear manifests in workplaces through loss of autonomy, skill devaluation, and "robo-boss" oversight concerns. GenAI adoption heightens burnout by increasing job demands, amplifying cognitive load through oversight labor (reviewing, evaluating, and correcting AI outputs), and escalating expectations around output and pace. Studies show AI-induced job insecurity positively relates to knowledge-hiding behavior via reduced psychological safety. Students in computer science programs report strong job replacement anxiety, leading to compelled upskilling or reskilling. A Nature poll reveals scientists feel broadly negative toward AI yet pressured to adopt it due to FOMO. Across populations, these pressures produce complex behaviors including obsessive skill acquisition and overzealous AI use, while deepening inequalities in access and support.

The GenAI movement is transforming professional norms, altering career entry points, demanding continuous adaptation, and exacting a measurable human cost in stress, diminished work passion, and work-life imbalance.

1. AI Skill Threat: The Pluralsight Developer Research (2023, with 2024-2025 updates)

Pluralsight's Developer Success Lab surveyed over 3,000 software engineers and developers across 12+ industries. Key findings:

- 45% of developers experience AI Skill Threat: worry, anxiety, and fear about whether they can succeed in an era of rapid AI-assisted coding evolution.
- 74% plan to upskill in AI-assisted coding in the near future, indicating broad industry momentum toward an AI-enabled developer landscape.
- Usage patterns: 85% of AI tool users primarily employ them to write code; 77% to learn or research; 54% to test or debug.
- Team culture matters: 44% reported that their teammates do not use AI tools, correlating with higher threat perception.
- Equity gaps: Upskilling intent is significantly lower for female and LGBTQ+ developers, signaling opportunity and access disparities.

Later Pluralsight materials (2024 AI Skills Report and 2025 updates) reinforce that IT skills now last only about 2.5 years, with 74% of IT professionals worried that advancements will render their day-to-day skills obsolete. The report frames AI Skill Threat not merely as individual anxiety but as a consequence of team culture and organizational learning environments. Managers can mitigate it through deliberate culture-building around learning and psychological safety.

2. FoMO-AI: Fear of Missing Out on Artificial Intelligence

Two 2025-2026 papers formalize FoMO-AI:

- Méndez-Suárez et al. (using OECD workplace AI data and fsQCA) focus on employees' fear of being left behind. Multiple causal pathways lead to FoMO, with combinations of perceived skill devaluation, lost decision-making autonomy, mental health impacts, and concerns over AI oversight ("robo-boss") as key drivers. Employees who perceive AI reducing their autonomy are significantly more likely to experience FoMO.
- Fengyi et al. introduce and validate the FoMO-AI scale, defining it as individuals' fear of missing out on AI benefits and of being rendered obsolete by others or society. FoMO-AI may drive complex behaviors such as obsessive focus on acquiring AI skills or overzealous use of AI technologies.

Workplace surveys (LiveCareer, Freshworks 2024-2025) show the shift from job-replacement fear to "AI FOMO": nearly 4 in 10 employees wish employers made AI tools more available; nearly half believe companies not using AI are already behind and likely to fail; 7 in 10 want to grow individual AI skills. Millennials and Gen Z lead this charge. This reframing moves anxiety from existential threat to competitive disadvantage, yet still produces pressure and potential maladaptive adoption.

3. Cognitive Load, Oversight Labor, and Burnout from GenAI

arXiv preprints document the hidden costs of productivity-focused GenAI adoption:

- Guizani et al. (2026, "At What Cost?") argue that GenAI tools amplify cognitive load, introduce new forms of oversight labor (reviewing, evaluating, and correcting AI outputs), and escalate expectations around output and pace. This contributes to stress, burnout, and diminished work-life balance. The "Do More With Less" trap creates invisible oversight fatigue; the developer's role shifts from active creator to reviewer, reducing autonomy and satisfaction. The GenAI movement transforms professional norms, alters career entry points, demands continuous adaptation, and deepens inequalities.
- Brandebusemeyer et al. (2026 field study at SAP with 22 developers, multimodal data including screen recordings, questionnaires, and physiological measures) found that during development-heavy tasks, perceived cognitive load arises from AI interaction. Tasks with AI showed higher perceived cognitive load and comparable perceived productivity to tasks without AI. Interaction type preferences differ by task: code suggestions suit monotonous/repetitive/structured tasks; chat-based prompting improves efficiency on complex tasks. Combining interaction types within one task diminishes benefits. Perceived productivity depends on AI output quality. The study proposes a rule-of-thumb for selecting interaction type based on task characteristics.
- Additional findings link GenAI to work intensification: effort shifts from content creation to verification, unrecognized hidden workload. One cited study found 19% productivity decline with AI tools in certain contexts.

These results challenge the narrative of unambiguous productivity gains and highlight that "effort saved" in one area is often offset by new cognitive and oversight demands.

4. Job Insecurity, Knowledge Hiding, Work-Family Conflict, and Diminished Passion

Multiple studies trace downstream effects of AI awareness and insecurity:

- Kim (2024): AI-induced job insecurity positively relates to knowledge-hiding behavior, directly and indirectly via reduced psychological safety. This erodes collaboration and organizational learning.
- Yi et al. (2026): AI awareness increases work-family conflict (WFC) by impairing employees' ability to detach from work. High trait resilience buffers this effect. The spillover pathway from workplace AI awareness to daily recovery failure produces strain at home.
- Additional papers link AI awareness to negative emotions, counterproductive work behavior toward customers, and emotional distress.
- A 2025 study found anxiety about job replacement and anxiety about learning both diminish employees' work passion.

These mechanisms create vicious cycles: insecurity reduces safety and sharing, which heightens isolation and further anxiety, while constant connectivity and upskilling pressure erode boundaries and recovery.

5. Student and Early-Career Anxiety: The Next Generation

Farooqi et al. (2026) conducted semi-structured interviews with 25 computer science undergraduate and graduate students at the University of Toronto:

- Students experience significant job replacement anxiety, especially regarding entry-level positions.
- Subfields perceived as vulnerable: software engineering, web development, game development (LLMs seen as comparable to junior developers).
- More secure: specialized fields such as quantum computing and AI research (often requiring graduate school).
- Responses: Many feel compelled to upskill by using more AI technologies, taking AI courses, and specializing in AI through graduate school. Others pursue reskilling via second majors or unrelated fields.
- International students face amplified anxiety due to pressure to secure permanent residence.
- Implications: Low security in CS careers, potential oversaturation in AI subfields, and possible dissuasion of future students from CS altogether.

A 4.82/7.00 average score was recorded for students reporting they had upskilled or felt motivated to do so because of AI. Broader surveys show 61% of workers considering upskilling/reskilling in response to AI anxiety, yet only 4% currently pursuing AI-related education.

6. Scientific Community Pressures: The Nature Poll (2026)

A Nature poll of more than 1,900 researchers found:

- Almost half feel broadly negative toward artificial intelligence.
- Yet many feel pressured to adopt the technology owing to concerns about being left behind (AI FOMO).
- Troubles include AI tools confidently outputting misinformation, especially when user demand favors speed over accuracy.
- Scientists recognize differential tool quality but experience the same adoption pressure documented in developer and general workplace studies.

This demonstrates that AI-related anxiety and FOMO are not limited to software developers or knowledge workers in industry; they permeate research and academic environments.

7. Behavioral Responses and Maladaptive Patterns

Across studies, anxiety and FoMO-AI produce:

- Compulsive upskilling and continuous adaptation demands.
- Obsessive focus on acquiring AI skills or overzealous AI use.
- Knowledge hiding (reducing collaboration).
- Difficulty detaching from work, elevating WFC.
- Diminished work passion.
- Potential for rushed, unstrategic adoption that wastes resources and increases frustration.

Positive adaptations exist (intentional use, selective interaction patterns, resilience buffering), but the dominant reported experience is pressure rather than empowerment.

8. Equity, Access, and Structural Implications

Recurring themes:

- Lower upskilling intent among female and LGBTQ+ developers (Pluralsight).
- Deepening inequalities in access and support for GenAI tools and training.
- International students facing compounded pressures.
- Career entry points altered: junior roles more vulnerable; specialized or AI-fluent paths privileged.
- Risk of oversaturation in "AI-proof" or AI-centric subfields.

The "Centaur Principle" (weak human + machine + better process) is undermined when process improvements are absent and anxiety drives reactive rather than deliberate adoption.

Conclusion and Research Gaps

The 2023-2026 literature paints a consistent picture: GenAI tools, while promising productivity gains, are generating measurable psychological, cognitive, and social costs through AI Skill Threat, FoMO-AI, cognitive overload from oversight labor, job insecurity, and boundary erosion. 45% of developers feel their skills threatened; 74% feel compelled to upskill. Students, scientists, and knowledge workers across domains report parallel pressures. These dynamics risk creating burnout, reduced collaboration, work-family strain, and inequitable outcomes rather than sustainable augmentation.

Critical gaps remain:

- Longitudinal studies tracking well-being, skill evolution, and career trajectories beyond short-term adoption.
- Intervention research on organizational and team-level mitigations (culture, psychological safety, deliberate interaction guidelines).
- Measurement of actual (vs. perceived) cognitive load and oversight labor across task types and experience levels.
- Equity-focused studies on differential access, support, and outcomes by gender, identity, geography, and career stage.
- Design research on tools and workflows that minimize oversight fatigue and maximize human agency.
