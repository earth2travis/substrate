# Jesuit Missions: Professionalizing the Mission

## Founding of the Society of Jesus (1540)

The Society of Jesus was founded by Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556) and companions, approved by Pope Paul III in 1540. Their Constitutions emphasized mobility and obedience to the Pope regarding "the propagation of the faith" and "the progress of souls in Christian life." This was the first truly global, professional missionary organization.

## Francis Xavier (1506–1552)

Francis Xavier, a Navarrese cleric, co-founded the Society of Jesus and became its most celebrated missionary. He led extensive missionary work across Asia within the Portuguese Empire:
- Activities in Portuguese India (Goa)
- First Christian mission to Japan (1549)
- Died en route to China (1552), trying to enter the mainland

In 1546, Xavier wrote to King John III of Portugal proposing measures to strengthen Christianity in Goa, demonstrating how Jesuit missions were intertwined with colonial state power.

## Matteo Ricci (1552–1610): The China Mission

Matteo Ricci arrived in Macau in 1582 and became the first European to enter the Forbidden City in Beijing in 1601, invited by the Wanli Emperor. His approach:
- Mastered Chinese language and writing
- Adopted Confucian scholar dress and emphasized parallels between Catholicism and Confucianism
- Worked with Chinese elites like Xu Guangqi to translate Western texts (Euclid's Elements) into Chinese
- Opposed Buddhism but respected Confucianism

Ricci represents the Jesuit strategy of cultural accommodation (adaptatio): entering a culture on its own terms to find points of contact.

## Roberto de Nobili (1577–1656): The India Mission

In India, de Nobili adopted Brahmin customs to reach high-caste Hindus. Like Ricci, he practiced accommodation but pushed it further, wearing the saffron robe and adopting vegetarianism.

## The Jesuit Reductions in Paraguay

The Jesuit missions among the Guarani (also called the Rio de la Plata missions) were established early in the 17th century and ended in the late 18th century after the expulsion of the Jesuits from the Americas. These settlements have been called:
- An experiment in "socialist theocracy"
- A rare example of "benign colonialism"
- Alternatively, a system that took away Indigenous freedom and forced radical lifestyle changes

The Spanish and Portuguese Empires gathered native populations into "reductions" to impart Christianity and European culture. The Jesuit Antonio Ruiz de Montoya (1585–1652) organized the reductions and defended Indigenous rights.

## The Chinese Rites Controversy

The Jesuit accommodation strategies triggered the "Chinese Rites Controversy" in the early 18th century. The question: could Chinese converts continue ancestor veneration and Confucian rituals? The Vatican eventually ruled against the Jesuits, demonstrating the tension between cultural adaptation and doctrinal purity in mission work.

## Conceptual Evolution

The Jesuits professionalized mission in unprecedented ways:
- Systematic cultural adaptation (adaptatio)
- Establishment of schools, astronomical observatories, and printing presses
- Tight integration with colonial state power
- Highly educated missionaries (unlike earlier monastic or Pietist missions)
- Global network: a single order operating across continents with centralized coordination
- The "mission" became an institutional, bureaucratic enterprise
