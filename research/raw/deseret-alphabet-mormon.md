# The Deseret Alphabet: Mormon Linguistic Utopianism

## The Context

In the 1850s, Brigham Young and the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints proposed a new phonemic alphabet called Deseret (from the Book of Mormon, meaning "honeybee"). It had 38 letters, each representing a distinct English phoneme.

## The Purpose

The Deseret Alphabet was not created for missionary work per se, but it embodies a missionary logic: if the message is to be pure, the medium must be reformed. English spelling was irregular; a phonemic alphabet would make literacy easier for immigrants and children, and perhaps for future converts.

## The Implementation

The Deseret News published in Deseret. Books were printed, including the Book of Mormon and elementary readers. Congress was lobbied to make Deseret the official alphabet of Utah Territory.

## The Failure

The Deseret Alphabet was abandoned within 20 years. It was costly to typeset, looked alien to readers, and solved a problem most people did not perceive. It is a case study in top-down linguistic reform: technically elegant, socially rejected.

## The Structural Point

The Deseret Alphabet represents the missionary impulse to redesign the medium itself. Cyril and Sequoyah created scripts for languages that lacked them. The Mormons tried to replace an existing script with a "better" one. The difference: the former addressed a genuine gap; the latter imposed a solution on a functioning system.

This distinction matters for any system that encodes knowledge: new tools succeed when they solve real problems, not when they optimize theoretical ones.
