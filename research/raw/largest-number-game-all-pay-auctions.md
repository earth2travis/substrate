# The Largest-Number Game and the All-Pay Auction Family

Raw research file, single-concept deep dive. Produced 2026-11-23. Scope: the largest-number game as William Poundstone presents it in Prisoner's Dilemma (1992), and the wider family it belongs to, meaning the all-pay auction, the war of attrition, rent dissipation and overdissipation, Tullock contests, and the places the family turns up in the wild. The dollar auction itself, its history and its psychology, are left to the sibling file [[dollar-auction-escalation]]. This is source material for synthesis, not a finished essay. No YAML frontmatter.

Sourcing status, stated plainly up front. The largest-number game has essentially one independent source outside Poundstone: Douglas Hofstadter devised it and ran it as the "luring lottery" in Scientific American in June 1983, and re-presented it in his 1985 collection Metamagical Themas. Beyond that single popular-mathematics lineage I could locate no academic literature on the largest-number game under that name, and no encyclopedia entry: Wikipedia carries articles on the all-pay auction and the war of attrition but none on the luring lottery or the largest-number game. So the game reaches the record almost entirely through Poundstone's two pages, and through Hofstadter's column and book before them. That is thin, and this file says so rather than padding it. The real substance here is the all-pay and contest family, which is large, independent and heavily cited, and into which the largest-number game fits only partially. I state the fit and the misfit rather than forcing them together, because a fabricated lineage is the one outcome worse than a short honest file.

## 1. The largest-number game as Poundstone frames it

Quote anchors below are from the operator's hand transcription of the print copy, First Anchor Books edition, February 1993, and are text-verified for wording and page in [[poundstone-prisoners-dilemma-quotations]], section 13. Where Poundstone's own full text of the game could be checked against a reproduction, that is marked.

Rules, reconstructed from Poundstone's text and marked for inference:

- The contest is free to enter and allows an unlimited number of entries per person. [FACT, Poundstone text reproduced at https://www.heretical.com/pound/lnumg.html]
- Each entry has an equal chance of winning; the sponsor draws one entry at random. [FACT, same]
- Each contestant submits a single card stating how many entries they wish to submit, so an astronomically large integer can be entered with no literal effort. [FACT, same]
- The prize is one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received; if no entries arrive, nothing is awarded. [FACT, same]
- Team play, pools, deals and any communication among contestants are forbidden by the rules. [FACT, same]
- The intended good outcome is a single winning entry, which pays the full million to one person; the bad outcome is that everyone over-enters and the prize rounds to zero. [FACT per Poundstone; page anchors 272 to 276 in the operator's transcription]

Now the passages the operator verified in his own copy:

- [FACT] "The insidious thing about the largest-number game is the slippery way it pits individual and group interests against one another." (p. 274)
- [FACT] "Greed is part and parcel of the largest-numbers game, but not the incentive to be thought clever by naming the biggest number. In a true largest-number game, the players are motivated only by maximizing personal gain." (p. 275; the operator's transcription slip "largest-numbers" is corrected to "largest-number")
- [FACT] "The largest-number game is almost a mirror image of the volunteer's dilemma." (p. 275)
- [FACT] "In the largest-number game, even one defector can ruin it for everyone." (p. 276)
- [FACT] "The only reasonable conclusion is that the largest-number game is a hopeless situation." (p. 276)
- [FACT] "The dollar auction and largest-number game share important features. Shortsighted rationality forces players to subvert the common good." (p. 277)

Structure, worked out, with the inference boundary marked. The rules are Poundstone's and are not inferred. What follows is my reading of their logic:

- Each player's privately rational move is to submit more entries, because more entries raise one's own win probability. [INTERPRETATION, though Poundstone states the reasoning]
- But entries are a pure negative externality on the prize: the denominator of the prize pool is the sum of all entries, so every entry by anyone lowers the prize for everyone. [INTERPRETATION; the arithmetic is Poundstone's]
- The collectively optimal outcome is exactly one entry in total. Achieving it needs coordination, to designate the single entrant, and the rules forbid coordination. [FACT per Poundstone]
- The prescribed cooperative play is a mixed strategy, each player entering with probability roughly 1/n (Poundstone's footnote refines this to about 1.5/n to reduce the chance that nobody enters). [FACT per Poundstone]
- The mixed strategy is not stable in practice because entry is private and unverifiable: any player can secretly enter and no one can detect it, so the temptation to defect is superhuman. [FACT per Poundstone; INTERPRETATION for the stability gloss]
- Hence the game is, in Poundstone's own words, hopeless: there is no cooperative equilibrium reachable without communication, and defection by even one player collapses the pool. [FACT for the conclusion; INTERPRETATION for the framing]

Two boundary notes the operator's notebook does not contain and that this file adds, both verified against the reproduced text at https://www.heretical.com/pound/lnumg.html:

- [FACT] Poundstone names the game's author: "Another caricature of escalation is a game devised by Douglas Hofstadter. It is known as the 'luring lottery' or 'largest-number game.'" The operator's transcription samples the phrase "largest-number game" at p. 272 but not the Hofstadter attribution, so the independent author is a genuine addition to the Substrate, not something the notebook already held.
- [FACT] Poundstone records the origin run: Hofstadter announced the contest in Scientific American, June 1983, with the one-million-divided-by-total-entries prize, and Scientific American agreed to supply any needed prize money. The outcome was the over-entry the game predicts: nine players submitted a googol entries, fourteen submitted a googolplex, some filled cards with microscopic nines, and Hofstadter could not decide which of the exotic-notation entries was largest, so no one was named winner.

The "true largest-number game" distinction matters and Poundstone draws it himself. He separates the pure rent-dissipation game, where players only maximize personal gain, from the actual Scientific American run, which was contaminated by a status motive, the wish to be thought clever by naming the biggest number. [FACT for the distinction; INTERPRETATION that the contamination changes the game from a pure rent-dissipation problem into a status contest]

## 2. The all-pay auction family, and the theory

The generalisation the task points at: a mechanism in which every player pays what they bid and only the largest bid wins. That is the first-price all-pay auction, and it has a real, independent and heavily cited literature.

- [FACT] Definition: in a first-price all-pay auction, bidders simultaneously submit sealed bids, all players forfeit their bids, and the high bidder receives the item, ties broken randomly. Source: Baye, Kovenock and de Vries, "The all-pay auction with complete information", Economic Theory 8(2):291 to 305, 1996, DOI 10.1007/BF01211819. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01211819 and https://casperdevries.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/the_all_pay_auction_with_complete_information.pdf
- [FACT] The same paper states the family's reach in one sentence: the all-pay auction is "widely used in economics to model rent seeking, R&D races, political contests, and job promotion tournaments." https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01211819
- [FACT] Equilibrium structure with complete information: there is no Nash equilibrium in pure strategies, only in mixed strategies; with more than two players and symmetric valuations there is a unique symmetric equilibrium and a continuum of asymmetric equilibria; the equilibria are payoff equivalent, and for some heterogeneous-valuation configurations they are not revenue equivalent, with asymmetric equilibria yielding higher expected revenue. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01211819 and https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1130&context=economics_articles
- [FACT] Baseline for the private-value case: bidding in an all-pay auction is revenue equivalent to bidding in a sealed high-bid or open ascending-price auction, after Riley and Samuelson (1981), "Optimal Auctions", American Economic Review 71(3):381 to 392. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-pay_auction (cites the AER paper at https://www.jstor.org/stable/1802786)
- [FACT] Complete-information baseline: the mixed-strategy equilibrium has zero expected payoffs and the seller's expected revenue equals the value of the prize; experiments nonetheless commonly show overbidding, so the seller's revenue frequently exceeds the prize value (Gneezy and Smorodinsky, Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization 61(2):255 to 275, 2006). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-pay_auction
- [FACT] Rent dissipation: when there is one prize, all equilibria dissipate expected rent exactly equal to the prize value, so the expected sum of bids equals the total prize value; Barut and Kovenock (1998) extend this to m prizes, where expected aggregate dissipation equals the total prize value mR. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ecin.12935
- [FACT] Overdissipation: an extensive experimental literature finds aggregate overdissipation, meaning the sum of bids exceeds the total prize value, which is overbidding relative to the Nash prediction. This is documented from Davis and Reilly (1998) and Potters, de Vries and van Winden (1998) onward, and recent work links overdissipation to prize scarcity, with overbidding disappearing when subjects perceive a high enough chance to win. Source: "Prize Scarcity and Overdissipation in All-Pay Auctions", Economic Inquiry, DOI 10.1111/ecin.12935. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ecin.12935
- [FACT] Political contests: Hillman and Riley, "Politically Contestable Rents and Transfers", Economics and Politics 1(1):17 to 39, March 1989, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0343.1989.tb00003.x. A rent or transfer is politically contestable when policy is open to influence by beneficiaries and losers; asymmetric valuations deter low-valuation contenders, which explains small numbers of active participants and low lobbying outlays relative to the value of the prizes. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-0343.1989.tb00003.x
- [FACT] Hillman and Riley (1989) also supply the family's two-branch taxonomy that later work uses: the perfectly discriminating (deterministic) contest, which is the first-price all-pay auction where the prize goes to the highest bidder, and the imperfectly discriminating (probabilistic) contest, where a prize is allocated by a stochastic success function and a higher bid only raises the win probability. https://www.econ.uzh.ch/apps/workingpapers/wp/econwp186.pdf
- [FACT] The probabilistic branch is the Tullock contest. Wikipedia calls the straightforward all-pay form a "Tullock auction" or "Tullock lottery", after Gordon Tullock. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-pay_auction (the underlying Tullock 1980 paper on efficient rent seeking is [BACKGROUND] here, not re-verified)
- [BACKGROUND] The contest literature is largely isomorphic to the all-pay auction: Baye, Kovenock and de Vries note that contests are "an all-pay auction in effort", the greatest effort winning while the effort of others goes unrewarded, and point to Nalebuff and Stiglitz (1983) as an early contest-of-effort model. https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1130&context=economics_articles
- [FACT] Lobbying as the canonical application: Baye, Kovenock and de Vries, "Rigging the Lobbying Process: An Application of the All-Pay Auction", American Economic Review, 1993, pages 289 to 294. Lobbyists ante up with non-refundable payments and the greatest payment wins the favor, a structure isomorphic to the all-pay auction; the paper shows a politician may do better by narrowing the slate of finalists ("rigging the process"). https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1120&context=economics_articles
- [BACKGROUND] Other equilibria-relevant structures that the same sources name as isomorphic: Varian (1980), Narasimhan (1988) and related models of sales and procurement. Cited in the Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1996 footnotes, not re-verified here.

Relationship to the largest-number game. This is where honesty matters most.

- [INTERPRETATION] The largest-number game is best read as a Tullock-family rent-seeking lottery rather than as a pure first-price all-pay auction. The winner is drawn at random with probability proportional to one's entries, which is the imperfectly discriminating branch, not the deterministic branch where the largest bid wins outright. On that reading the game is a lottery contest whose total effort dissipates the prize.
- [INTERPRETATION] The game carries one twist no standard contest has: the prize is endogenous. The prize is one million divided by total entries, so aggregate effort shrinks the prize as well as the win probability. Standard Tullock contests fix the prize and let effort dissipate it from the players' own budgets; here the players destroy the pool itself. The all-pay auction similarly fixes the prize value V.
- [INTERPRETATION] So the task's stated generalisation, that the mechanism in which everyone pays what they bid and the largest bid wins is an all-pay auction, describes the family's deterministic member, not the largest-number game's own mechanism. The all-pay auction is the game's nearest well-studied relative, not its species. I record the mismatch rather than smoothing it away.
- [INTERPRETATION] What does carry across cleanly is the dissipation result and the collective-action shape. Every equilibrium of the one-prize all-pay auction dissipates expected rent equal to the prize value, which is the same "the pool is competed to nothing" outcome Poundstone describes for the largest-number game, and overdissipation beyond the prize value is the experimental mirror of the Scientific American run, where total entries vastly exceeded any sustainable number.

## 3. The war of attrition

The task asks for the war of attrition as part of the family, and it is.

- [FACT] Definition: in game theory the war of attrition is a dynamic timing game in which players choose a time to stop and trade off the strategic gain from outlasting others against the real costs of elapsed time; its opposite is the pre-emption game. The model was originally formulated by John Maynard Smith, and a mixed evolutionarily stable strategy was determined by Bishop and Cannings. An example is a second-price all-pay auction, in which the prize goes to the highest bidder and each player pays the loser's low bid. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_attrition_(game)
- [FACT] The core citations: Maynard Smith, J. (1974), "The theory of games and the evolution of animal conflicts", Journal of Theoretical Biology 47:209 to 221, DOI 10.1016/0022-5193(74)90110-6; and Bishop, D. T., and Cannings, C. (1978), "A generalized war of attrition", Journal of Theoretical Biology 70(1):85 to 124, DOI 10.1016/0022-5193(78)90304-1, PMID 564432. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_attrition_(game)
- [FACT] The unique symmetric Nash equilibrium is given by the survival function S_i(x) = e^(-x/V_j), where V_j is the opponent's valuation; the player with the lower value persists longer and so has the higher probability of winning, both players receive zero expected payoff, and the unique evolutionarily stable strategy coincides with the symmetric Nash equilibrium. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_attrition_(game)
- [FACT] Luce and Raiffa (Games and Decisions, 1957) named the case where both bid above the value a "ruinous situation": both players suffer and there is no winner. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/War_of_attrition_(game)
- [BACKGROUND] There is a companion paper, Bishop, Cannings and Maynard Smith (1978), "The war of attrition with random rewards", Journal of Theoretical Biology 74(3):377 to 388, which the task's "Bishop, Cannings and Maynard Smith, 1978" names. It was not re-verified live this session, so treat the volume and pages as established record to be checked before quotation.
- [INTERPRETATION] The war of attrition is the family's sequential, real-time member, and it is the closest formal cousin of the dollar auction: both reward persistence and punish the party who stops. The largest-number game differs because it is a simultaneous one-shot submission, not a timing game, even though the escalation it produces looks qualitatively similar.

## 4. Where the family appears in the wild

- [FACT] Lobbying and political contests. Rent-seeking and lobbying are the canonical all-pay application (Hillman and Riley 1989; Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1993), where non-refundable influence payments are the forfeited bids. URLs as in section 2.
- [FACT] R&D races, patent races and job promotion tournaments. The Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1996 abstract names R&D races, political contests and job promotion tournaments as the model's uses, and describes the all-pay structure as applying to contests in general, where the greatest effort wins and the effort of others is unrewarded. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01211819
- [FACT] Priority gas auctions and MEV in blockchains. In an open mempool, searchers who spot the same profit opportunity bid against each other by raising the gas fee on otherwise identical transactions; the ordering rule is a priority gas auction, and because losing transactions still pay gas, the competition is an all-pay auction. Sources: "Blockchains, MEV and the knapsack problem: a primer" (arXiv 2403.19077), which states that searchers "engage in an open-bid first-price auction on-chain" and that "since the (failed) transactions by losing searchers were also recorded on-chain, they still incurred a gas cost; this was, therefore, an all-pay auction", https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19077v1; and a CMU computer science blog that calls the priority gas auction "a first-price-all-pay auction", https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~csd-phd-blog/2024/max-extractable-value/
- [FACT] The MEV case is a live escalation, not a static model. Searcher profit is competed away in the open-bid auction so the block producer extracts the value as rent, and the primer itself notes the resemblance to escalation games and names the Shubik dollar auction as the classic demonstration. https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19077v1
- [FACT] The auction format on-chain is between pure forms. A 2026 study of priority gas auctions on Base (Offchain Labs; arXiv 2610.03922) models the winner as paying in full and losers as paying in part, "an auction format between a winner-pays and an all-pay first-price auction", and estimates that splitting a block into ten 200 ms sub-auctions cut the searcher effective priority fee and collapsed top-of-block fee concentration. https://arxiv.org/html/2610.03922v1
- [INTERPRETATION] Queueing and congestion games sit in the same neighbourhood: any setting where arrivals pay a real cost to gain position, and the cost is sunk whether or not position is won, has the all-pay shape. I could not verify a specific queueing-as-all-pay citation within budget, so this is named as a plausible adjacent instance and flagged rather than cited.
- [INTERPRETATION] The war of attrition's wild instances are the traditional biological ones, animal contests resolved by display rather than combat and strikes or standoffs resolved by whichever side can bear the cost longer, which is why Poundstone links labour strikes to the dollar auction. See [[dollar-auction-escalation]] for the strike and dollar-auction material itself.

## 5. Where the mapping breaks

- The winner rule differs. In an all-pay auction the highest bid wins with certainty; in the largest-number game the winner is drawn at random in proportion to entries. That is the probabilistic Tullock branch, not the deterministic all-pay branch, so calling the largest-number game "an all-pay auction" is a category slip, even though both are rent-dissipating contests.
- Who pays, and from where, differs. In the all-pay auction and the dollar auction the loser really loses money from their own pocket. In the largest-number game the nominal entry cost is zero; the only thing burned is the prize pool itself, through the division by total entries. The resource destroyed is the common pool, not the players' budgets, so the "everyone pays what they bid" premise does not hold literally.
- The prize is endogenous only in the largest-number game. Standard contests and all-pay auctions fix the prize value; the largest-number game makes the prize a decreasing function of aggregate effort, which changes the equilibrium arithmetic and is not captured by the off-the-shelf dissipation results.
- Timing differs. The all-pay auction and the largest-number game are simultaneous one-shot submissions; the war of attrition and the dollar auction are sequential. The escalation flavour that Poundstone draws out of the largest-number game is imported from the dollar auction, which is a different structure.
- The communication ban is a rule, not an assumption. All-pay auction models simply assume non-cooperation. The largest-number game forbids coordination by explicit rule, which is what makes its prescribed cooperative solution, drawing lots, unavailable and produces Poundstone's "hopeless situation" verdict. A standard all-pay auction, by contrast, has a well-defined mixed-strategy equilibrium with zero expected payoffs even without communication.
- The empirical run was contaminated. The Scientific American luring lottery was played partly as a status contest to name the biggest number, which Poundstone explicitly excludes from a "true largest-number game". That contamination turns the observed behaviour into evidence about a status all-pay auction, not about the pure rent-dissipation game, so the empirical anecdote and the theoretical game should not be treated as the same object.
- The Substrate caveat. Every Poundstone quotation above is a reader's transcription, not a verified artifact. Before any of it is promoted into a finding, the wording and page should be confirmed against the print copy, per the standing caveat in [[poundstone-prisoners-dilemma-quotations]].

## 6. Compression points for synthesis

1. The largest-number game was devised by Douglas Hofstadter, who ran it as the "luring lottery" in Scientific American, June 1983, with a prize of one million dollars divided by total entries; Poundstone presents it as a caricature of escalation. [FACT via the reproduced Poundstone text and the operator's verified page anchors]
2. The game's rules: free entry, unlimited entries per person, equal chance per entry, private unverifiable submission, no communication, and a prize that shrinks with total entries. The intended good outcome is exactly one entry; over-entry rounds the prize to zero. [FACT]
3. The game is a rent-dissipating lottery contest in the Tullock family (probabilistic winner, proportional allocation) with an endogenous prize, not a first-price all-pay auction, even though the all-pay auction is its nearest well-studied relative. [INTERPRETATION, grounded in the two-branch taxonomy of Hillman and Riley 1989]
4. Poundstone's verdict, text-verified: even one defector can ruin it for everyone (p. 276), the game is a hopeless situation (p. 276), and it shares with the dollar auction that shortsighted rationality forces players to subvert the common good (p. 277). [FACT]
5. Largest-number game against volunteer's dilemma: Poundstone calls it "almost a mirror image" (p. 275). In a volunteer's dilemma you are penalized by the proportion of defectors; in the largest-number game a single defector collapses the pool. [FACT]
6. The all-pay auction with complete information is fully characterized in Baye, Kovenock and de Vries, Economic Theory 8(2):291 to 305 (1996), DOI 10.1007/BF01211819: no pure-strategy equilibrium, a unique symmetric mixed equilibrium plus a continuum of asymmetric equilibria, payoff equivalence but not always revenue equivalence. [FACT]
7. Rent dissipation and overdissipation: all one-prize equilibria dissipate expected rent equal to the prize value, while experiments find aggregate overdissipation, the sum of bids exceeding the prize; overdissipation is linked to prize scarcity. [FACT, Economic Inquiry, DOI 10.1111/ecin.12935]
8. Hillman and Riley (1989), Economics and Politics 1(1):17 to 39, DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0343.1989.tb00003.x, is the political-contest primary source and supplies the deterministic versus probabilistic contest taxonomy. [FACT]
9. The war of attrition: Maynard Smith (1974), Journal of Theoretical Biology 47:209 to 221, DOI 10.1016/0022-5193(74)90110-6; mixed ESS by Bishop and Cannings (1978), Journal of Theoretical Biology 70(1):85 to 124, DOI 10.1016/0022-5193(78)90304-1; unique symmetric equilibrium survival function S_i(x) = e^(-x/V_j) with zero expected payoff. [FACT]
10. In the wild: lobbying (Baye, Kovenock and de Vries 1993, AER 289 to 294), R&D and patent races and job promotion tournaments (named in the 1996 abstract), and priority gas auctions in blockchains (Daian et al. 2019; arXiv 2403.19077 and 2610.03922), where losing searchers still pay gas, making the race an all-pay auction. [FACT]
11. Honest sourcing: outside Poundstone and Hofstadter's column and book, the largest-number game has no independent academic literature and no encyclopedia entry; the all-pay and contest family, by contrast, is well established and independent. [FACT for the negative searches; INTERPRETATION that the game is carried by Poundstone]
