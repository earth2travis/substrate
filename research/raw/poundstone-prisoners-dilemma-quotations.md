# Poundstone, Prisoner's Dilemma (1992): Verbatim Passages from the Operator's Print Copy

Raw research file. Produced 2026-11-23. This is the operator's own hand transcription of passages from his print copy of William Poundstone, Prisoner's Dilemma: John von Neumann, Game Theory, and the Puzzle of the Bomb, taken while reading the book through and organized here by the book's arc, with page anchors. It is the companion to research/raw/poundstone-prisoners-dilemma-book.md, which was built from the book's public record because no text was in hand. These are the Substrate's first text-verified quotations from the book.

Provenance note. The source is a hand transcription made by the operator from the print copy, not a scan and not a machine extraction, so its authority is that of a careful reader, not a verified artifact. Three consequences. First, page numbers are anchored to that specific printing. The companion file's Wikipedia-derived citations for the same passages (pp. 8, 117, 118, and 126 to 127) match the page numbers recorded here, which is consistent with the operator's copy carrying the Anchor 1993 pagination. [INTERPRETATION; the printing itself was not confirmed, so treat the anchor as "operator's copy" rather than "Anchor 1993" until checked.] Second, the transcription contains a handful of apparent copying slips, which are almost certainly artifacts rather than the author's text, given the book was professionally copy-edited. The clearly wrong words are listed in section 0 and the quotations below are rendered in their evident intended wording. Third, minor function words may have been dropped in copying; where a phrasing reads as an elision rather than an error it has been left as transcribed. Nothing has been paraphrased or merged.

Tag key: FACT here means transcribed from the operator's print copy with a page number, the strongest provenance this Substrate has for the book. INTERPRETATION marks analytic connection. Anything still resting on the public record is marked as it is in the companion file.

## 1. The opening frame (pp. 3 to 9)

- [FACT] "The dilemmas of real life are created not by mad scientists but by the sundry ways that our individual interests clash with those of others and of society." (p. 3)
- [FACT] "Real life consists of bluffing, of little tactics of deception, of asking yourself what is the other man going to think I mean to do." (p. 6) [INTERPRETATION: the italicization in the operator's notes suggests the book is quoting von Neumann here rather than speaking in its own voice; the surrounding source is not recorded in the notes.]
- [FACT] "there is always a rational course of action for games of two players, provided their interests are completely opposed. This proof is called the 'minimax theorem.'" (p. 7)
- [FACT] "Theorists now realize that prisoner's dilemmas occur in biology, psychology, sociology, economics, and law. The prisoner's dilemma is apt to turn up anywhere a conflict of interests exists." (p. 9)
- [FACT] "Is there any way to promote the common good in a prisoner's dilemma? The attempt to answer this question is one of the great intellectual adventures of our time." (p. 9)
- [INTERPRETATION] These four pages carry the book's promise and its frame: the dilemma as a general feature of social life rather than a puzzle in a matrix, the minimax theorem as the rationalist baseline it will then complicate, and the four disciplinary applications that the middle of the book will work through.

## 2. Von Neumann's machines (pp. 24, 31)

- [FACT] "One of von Neumann's theoretical interests was the question of whether a machine could be built to reproduce itself." (p. 24)
- [FACT] "he would spend much of the last years of his life considering how a 'mind' of sorts might be embodied in the relays and circuits of a computer." (p. 31)
- [INTERPRETATION] Worth holding against [[turing-1950-imitation-game]] and [[ai-machine-soul]]: the book places self-reproduction and machine mind among von Neumann's serious theoretical interests, which is the same decade and the same intellectual neighbourhood as Turing's imitation game.

## 3. The apparatus as the book presents it (pp. 39 to 63)

- [FACT] "The problem is one common to oracles, namely that game theory's answers can depend on exactly how you phrase the questions." (p. 39)
- [FACT] "Game theory is not about 'playing' as usually understood. It is about conflict among rational but distrusting beings." (p. 39)
- [FACT] "Game theory is about perfectly logical players interested only in winning." (p. 44)
- [FACT] "a strategy is a much more specific plan. It is a complex description of a particular way to play a game, no matter what the other player(s) does and no matter how long the game lasts. A strategy must prescribe actions so thoroughly that you never have to make a decision in following it." (p. 48)
- [FACT] "'Zero-sum game' is one of the few terms from game theory that has entered general parlance. It refers to a game where the total winnings or payoffs are fixed." (p. 51)
- [FACT] "The minimax theorem proves that every finite, two-person, zero-sum game has a rational solution in the form of a pure or mixed strategy." (p. 61)
- [FACT] "the minimax theorem says that there is always a rational solution to a precisely defined conflict between two people whose interests are completely opposite." (p. 62)
- [FACT] "Morgenstern's work on economics never got off the ground. It remains for someone else to extend their foundations." (p. 63)
- [INTERPRETATION] The oracles line at p. 39 is the epistemologically interesting one: it is the book admitting, early, that a formal answer is only as good as the question's framing, which is the theme the psychology chapters later pick up as framing effects. The formal statements here (strategy, zero-sum, minimax) are the textbook material the companion series already carries in full, so only the formulations are recorded.

## 4. Nash's bargaining problem, and the doubt that produced the dilemma (pp. 102 to 107)

- [FACT] "The peculiar thing is that the party who is more unreasonable is apt to get the better of the deal." (p. 102)
- [FACT] "it is 'rational' to cheat." (p. 105)
- [FACT] "Logic (?) blocks a deal beneficial to both parties." (p. 105)
- [FACT] "cheating undermines the common good." (p. 105)
- [FACT] "Flood and Dresher were concerned that Nash's equilibrium point solutions could be unsatisfying." (p. 106)
- [FACT] "In a prisoner's dilemma, the equilibrium point strategy is called defection. A player is always better off defecting, no matter what the other does." (p. 107)
- [FACT] "The other strategy of a prisoner's dilemma, which leads to the best collective outcome, is called cooperation." (p. 107)
- [INTERPRETATION] This is the most important page run in the notebook for provenance, because it settles two things the companion file could only infer. First, the book itself states that Flood and Dresher were motivated by dissatisfaction with Nash's equilibrium as a solution concept, which upgrades the companion's accuracy audit from inference to text-verified fact. Second, it corrects the companion's warning that the book "can invert who was proving what": on this evidence the book does convey the anti-equilibrium motive. The naming of defection and cooperation at p. 107 is the vocabulary the rest of the book and the whole popular literature inherit.

## 5. The naming, the canonical story, and the quadrants (pp. 117 to 129)

- [FACT] "Tucker devised a now-well-known dilemma tale and coined the name, 'prisoner's dilemma.'" (p. 117)
- [FACT] The prison tale in the book's own words: "Two men, charged with a joint violation of the law, are held separately by the police. Each is told that (1) if one confesses and the other one does not, the former will be given a reward ... and the latter will be fined ...; (2) if both confess, each will be fined ...; At the same time, each has good reason to believe that (3) if neither confesses, both will go clear." (pp. 117 to 118)
- [FACT] The four payoff quadrants are named reward payoff, punishment payoff, temptation payoff, and sucker payoff. (p. 120)
- [FACT] "categorical imperative" (p. 123) [INTERPRETATION: noted without context by the operator; apparently a Kantian aside in the same chapter, not developed in the notes.]
- [FACT] "free-rider dilemma" (p. 126)
- [FACT] "Why not just hop over the turnstiles and save yourself the fare?" (p. 126)
- [FACT] "In general, a free rider dilemma occurs any time payment for a product or service is on the honor system or where payment, while technically mandatory, is difficult to enforce." (p. 127)
- [FACT] "a liberal is a 'cooperator'" (p. 128)
- [FACT] "Conservatives are often 'defectors'" (p. 128)
- [FACT] "Most conflicts are not prisoner's dilemmas, though." (p. 129)
- [INTERPRETATION] Three things are now text-verified here. The Tucker attribution at p. 117 is confirmed verbatim, and the pages match the citation the Substrate's anatomy file already carries (Poundstone 1993, pp. 8, 117), which means that citation is substantively sound even though it arrived second-hand. The quadrant names at p. 120 are the book's version of the ordinal skeleton T > R > P > S that [[prisoners-dilemma-anatomy]] states formally. And the passage Wikipedia renders as the "newspaper-box example" at pp. 126 to 127 is, on this evidence, the honour-system free-rider discussion with the turnstile illustration, so the two renderings are the same pages. The p. 129 line is the book's own guard against over-application, and it belongs in the companion file's honesty section.

## 7. Chicken, the volunteer's dilemma, and bully (pp. 197 to 201, 221)

- [FACT] "chicken is an important model for a diverse range of human conflicts." (p. 197)
- [FACT] "Mutual defection (the crash when both players drive straight) is the most feared outcome in chicken. In the prisoner's dilemma, cooperation while the other player defects (being the sucker) is the worst outcome." (p. 199)
- [FACT] "A curious feature of chicken is that both players want to do the opposite of whatever the other is going to do." (p. 199)
- [FACT] "Just as the prisoner's dilemma has a multiperson version, so does chicken: the 'volunteer's dilemma.'" (p. 201)
- [FACT] "The game of bully is a cross between chicken and deadlock." (p. 221)
- [INTERPRETATION] The chicken and prisoner's dilemma contrast at p. 199 is the same distinction the companion file's audit found to be the book's largest game-theoretic simplification of the arms race. The book draws the distinction cleanly for the abstract games, and the audit's complaint is about which one it then applies to the Cold War, not about whether the book understands the difference.

## 8. The four social dilemmas (pp. 215 to 221)

This is the book's most portable analytical contribution, and the notebook records its definitions completely.

- [FACT] "A symmetric game is one where the payoffs are the same for each player under comparable circumstances, where neither player is in a privileged position." (p. 215)
- [FACT] "The disturbing thing about the prisoner's dilemma and chicken is the way that the common good is subverted by individual rationality." (p. 216)
- [FACT] "For a true dilemma to exist, there must be a temptation to defect, otherwise, why defect?" (p. 216)
- [FACT] The four games are named as Deadlock, Prisoner's Dilemma, Chicken, and Stag Hunt, and all four "are common games in real-life interactions. For that reason they are called 'social dilemmas.'" (p. 217)
- [FACT] The transformation rules that generate the family: "Chicken is a prisoner's dilemma with punishment and sucker payoffs reversed. The stag hunt is the prisoner's dilemma with the preferences of the reward and temptation payoffs switched. Deadlock is a prisoner's dilemma with the reward and punishment payoffs switched." (p. 217)
- [FACT] "Deadlock occurs when two parties fail to cooperate because neither really wants to." (p. 218)
- [FACT] "For this reason the dilemma is most acute when one has reason to doubt the rationality of the other player, or in groups large enough that, given the vagaries of human nature, some defections are likely." (p. 220)
- [FACT] "Preferences need not match." (p. 221)
- [INTERPRETATION] This maps directly onto the anatomy file's family-of-2x2-games section, but the book supplies something the series does not: an explicit generative rule. The four games are not four examples, they are one game under three payoff swaps, and the swap names which quadrant moves. That is a compact teaching device worth carrying as the book's own contribution, and it is the first place in this Substrate where the four-way social dilemma family is stated in prose rather than swept up in a table.

## 9. Howard's metagame: intentions as strategies (pp. 225 to 227)

- [FACT] "Neither cooperation nor defection is demonstrably right." (p. 225)
- [FACT] "Fundamentally, game theory is about abstract problems of maximization." (p. 225)
- [FACT] "range of intentions" (p. 226)
- [FACT] "The puzzle of the prisoner's dilemma is how such good intentions pave the road to hell." (p. 226)
- [FACT] "Howard's response was to create a 'meta-game.' The strategies of the meta-game are not cooperation and defection but rather intentions about how one would play the game." (p. 226)
- [FACT] "Defection is rational because it always results in a higher payoff." (p. 227)
- [FACT] "society is founded on cooperation." (p. 227)
- [INTERPRETATION] Nigel Howard's metagame theory is a real lineage that this Substrate does not currently hold: the move of making the unit of strategy an intention rather than an action, so that a player chooses a policy contingent on the other's policy. It is an ancestor of the program-equilibrium and superrationality families that the companion file noted the book does not otherwise engage. This entry names it so the synthesis cron can decide whether it warrants its own node.

## 10. Iteration, evolutionary stability, and Axelrod (pp. 228 to 248)

- [FACT] "You may well decide honesty is the best policy, purely out of self-interest." (p. 228)
- [FACT] "In effect, the last game of an iterated prisoner's dilemma is a one-shot prisoner's dilemma." (p. 228)
- [FACT] The "'backward induction paradox,' lies in the fact that 'rational' parties get stuck with the punishment payoff every time, while less logical parties cooperate and do better." (p. 229)
- [FACT] "One reason that even self-serving people don't always betray others is that it's wise not to 'burn your bridges.'" (p. 230)

- [FACT] "Evolutionarily stable strategies are not necessarily 'rational' or 'fair' or 'morally right.' They are simply stable." (p. 234)
- [FACT] "Natural selection 'chooses' or 'prefers' the behaviors that will maximize survival value." (p. 235)
- [FACT] "Defection is an evolutionarily stable strategy. Cooperation is not." (p. 235)
- [FACT] "Most of the dilemmas in nature are iterated." (p. 236)
- [FACT] The book calls Axelrod's The Evolution of Cooperation (1984) a work that "ranks among the most significant discoveries of game theory" (p. 236; the notebook's transcription runs two clauses together here, so quote the phrase rather than the sentence).
- [FACT] "What makes the iterated prisoner's dilemma different from a one-shot dilemma is the 'shadow of the future.'" (p. 236)
- [FACT] "It makes sense to cooperate now in order to secure cooperation in the future." (p. 236)
- [FACT] "The question is, what is the best way for you to play knowing that it will affect how the other person plays?" (p. 239)
- [FACT] "communication by actions" (p. 239)
- [FACT] "This restricted language permits some types of deals or threats to be communicated more readily than others." (p. 239)
- [FACT] "The highest score went to the simplest strategy. Submitted by Anatol Rapoport, it was called TIT FOR TAT." (p. 239)
- [FACT] "cooperate on the first round, then do whatever the other player did on the previous round." (p. 240)
- [FACT] "a nice strategy is one that is never the first to defect." (p. 240)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT is also provocable. It defects in response to defection by the other strategy." (p. 240)
- [FACT] "forgiveness" (p. 240)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT is 'willing' to start cooperating any time its partner strategy is." (p. 240)
- [FACT] "That the winning strategy was nice and provocable was no surprise to the game theory community." (p. 240)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT threatens, 'Do unto others as you would have them do unto you, or else!'" (p. 240)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT 'punishes' for a defection immediately, on the very next dilemma." (p. 241)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT succeeds because its threat is the simplest possible and thus the easiest to react to." (p. 241)
- [FACT] "Still another important quality of TIT FOR TAT is that it does not have to be kept a secret." (p. 241)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT won without ever exploiting another strategy." (p. 241)
- [FACT] "TIT FOR TAT can't beat anybody, but it still wins the tournament." (p. 241)
- [FACT] "Repetition boosts the incentive to cooperate." (p. 242)
- [FACT] "It is important to realize that no strategy is good or bad out of context. How well a strategy does depends on the strategies with which it interacts." (p. 242)
- [FACT] "with any unresponsive strategy, the best course of action is to defect." (p. 243)
- [FACT] TIT FOR TAT "is subject to 'echo' effects." (p. 243)
- [FACT] "Most successful strategies aren't very exploitable, and the act of provoking them to see how they react usually costs more points than are to be gained." (p. 245)
- [FACT] "One key to TIT FOR TAT's success is that its punishment for defection is commensurate with the crime: a defection for a defection." (p. 245)
- [FACT] "Thus the next tournament would start with a new generation including more copies of the successful strategies and less of the unsuccessful ones." (p. 247)
- [FACT] "'predator' strategies ran into trouble as their 'prey' became extinct." (p. 247)
- [FACT] TIT FOR TAT "exerts pressure on other strategies to cooperate." (p. 247)
- [FACT] "The more TIT FOR TATs in the population, the greater the advantage in playing TIT FOR TAT. In this way, a small colony of TIT FOR TAT players could supplant a population of uncooperative players." (p. 248)
- [INTERPRETATION] The companion file's chapter on Axelrod listed Tit for Tat's four properties from the secondary record. The book's own formulations are stronger than the summary: the commensurate-punishment principle (p. 245), the claim that the strategy cannot beat any single opponent yet wins the field (p. 241), the non-secrecy property (p. 241), the generational tournament with predator and prey extinctions (p. 247), and the contextual claim that no strategy is good or bad out of context (p. 242). The last of these is the one with the most reach beyond game theory, and the adaptive-tournament material is the part the companion file lacked entirely.

## 11. What makes cooperation possible, and what breaks it (pp. 223 to 224, 250 to 254)

- [FACT] "it is easier to evade social dilemmas than to resolve the paradox." (p. 223)
- [FACT] "For a person with a sufficiently strong sense of empathy, there is no such thing as a prisoner's dilemma." (p. 223)
- [FACT] "A world of perfect communication and perfect honesty is a world without prisoner's dilemmas." (p. 224)
- [FACT] "Milinski found that the behavior of the sticklebacks was consistent with TIT FOR TAT. The fish started by cooperating, by darting a few centimeters toward the suspected predator, and then tended to do whatever their mirror image appeared to be doing." (p. 250)
- [FACT] "The willingness to donate depended primarily on how well known the bats were to each other." (p. 251)
- [FACT] "How often mutual cooperation occurs is a measure of how effectively the society is functioning." (p. 251)
- [FACT] "'DISCRIMINATORY TIT FOR TAT' (DTFT). This strategy is just like TIT FOR TAT except when dealing with someone of a different color group. Then you always defect." (p. 252)
- [FACT] "The label that is in the minority is hurt more by DTFT than the majority." (p. 252)
- [FACT] "This provides a game-theoretic rationale towards separatist movements." (p. 252)
- [FACT] "In the real world, it is not always obvious whether someone has acted cooperatively or defected." (p. 253)
- [FACT] "When one cannot tell what the other player has done, it is impossible to use any conditional strategy." (p. 253)
- [FACT] "In an uncertain world, TIT FOR TAT-like strategies may be as much a part of the problem as the solution." (p. 253)
- [FACT] "Bertrand Russell claimed that there was only one war in the history of the world for which people knew the cause: the Trojan War. That was over a beautiful woman." (p. 254)
- [FACT] "Because states seldom believe the same history, however, the utility of Tit-for-Tat strategies is severely limited in international affairs." (p. 254)
- [INTERPRETATION] This section is where the book is most useful to the Substrate's coordination threads and least covered by the companion file. Empathy and perfect communication as dilemma dissolvers (pp. 223 to 224) are the same class of move the psychology file reports under framing and cheap talk. Discriminatory Tit for Tat with its separatism rationale (p. 252) is an in-group mechanic with real policy bite. And the noise-limitation argument (p. 253) plus the Russell point about states not sharing a history (p. 254) is the book pre-empting its own favourite strategy: an observable-action strategy fails where actions are unreadable, which is the coupling between the tournaments file's noise-fragility finding and the crypto layer's trustlessness problem.

## 13. Escalation traps: the dollar auction and the largest-number game (pp. 260 to 277)

- [FACT] "The 'dollar auction' is a game of escalation and outrageous behavior." (p. 260)

- [FACT] "Shubik recognizes the Vietnam war as an 'exquisite example' of a dollar auction but doesn't recall it being an inspiration for the game." (p. 264)
- [FACT] "Many conflicts start this way, with a justifiable action that, in retrospect, becomes the first 'defection' in an escalating dilemma." (p. 265)
- [FACT] "The 'rational' first bid would be 72 cents." (p. 269)
- [FACT] "As the bidding continues, each bidder is increasingly worse off, but each has incentive to continue in the short term." (p. 269)
- [FACT] "The player with more money, even a penny more, can always outbid the other if forced to do so." (p. 270)
- [FACT] "What you want is a strategy that works pretty well in this state of ignorance." (p. 271)
- [FACT] "If the player who ends the escalation is the more 'rational' one, the dollar auction penalizes rationality." (p. 272)
- [FACT] "It's a matter of personalities, group psychology, and luck, not game theory; rationality has nothing to do with it." (p. 272)
- [FACT] "largest-number game" (p. 272)
- [FACT] "The insidious thing about the largest-number game is the slippery way it pits individual and group interests against one another." (p. 274)
- [FACT] "Greed is part and parcel of the largest-numbers game, but not the incentive to be thought clever by naming the biggest number. In a true largest-number game, the players are motivated only by maximizing personal gain." (p. 275)
- [FACT] "The largest-number game is almost a mirror image of the volunteer's dilemma." (p. 275)
- [FACT] "In the largest-number game, even one defector can ruin it for everyone." (p. 276)
- [FACT] "The only reasonable conclusion is that the largest-number game is a hopeless situation." (p. 276)
- [FACT] "The dollar auction and largest-number game share important features. Shortsighted rationality forces players to subvert the common good." (p. 277)
- [FACT] "but the 'explanation' is simply that common sense is wrong." (p. 277)
- [FACT] "The paradox lies in the fact that our concept of rationality is not fixed." (p. 277)
- [INTERPRETATION] This is a whole branch the companion file does not have and no existing Substrate node covers: escalation traps as a distinct family from payoff dilemmas. The dollar auction's defining property is that each marginal bid is locally rational and the aggregate is ruinous, with the twist that the party who stops first is the one penalized for rationality (p. 272). The Shubik attribution and the Vietnam example (p. 264) date the material and locate it in the same RAND-adjacent milieu as the rest of the book. For the Substrate's own operating context this is the cleanest formalization of sunk-cost escalation available, and it is directly applicable to agent fleets that can bid, retry, or reinvest without a stopping rule.

## 14. The closing (p. 278)

- [FACT] "The only satisfying solution to the prisoner's dilemma is to avoid the prisoner's dilemma." (p. 278)
- [FACT] "the long-term survival of the human race depends on our devising better ways to promote cooperation than any now in existence." (p. 278)
- [INTERPRETATION] The companion file carried the first of these lines only as a blog review's rendering. It is now text-verified and word for word as the review gave it, which retires one second-hand item. The second line is the book's actual last claim: not a solution but a programme, and a survival claim rather than a mathematical one, which is consistent with the companion file's reading that the book closes on a moral rather than a theorem.

## 15. What these pages confirm, and one correction to the companion file

Confirmed against the print copy, and therefore upgraded from second-hand to text-verified:

1. The Tucker naming and the prison tale (pp. 117 to 118). The companion file and the anatomy file both cite Poundstone 1993 for this; the substance and the page numbers now stand on the operator's own reading, not on Wikipedia's citation of Poundstone.

## 16. The book's own limits, in its own words

- [FACT] "Most conflicts are not prisoner's dilemmas, though." (p. 129)
- [FACT] "For a true dilemma to exist, there must be a temptation to defect, otherwise, why defect?" (p. 216)
- [FACT] "it is easier to evade social dilemmas than to resolve the paradox." (p. 223)
- [FACT] "Neither cooperation nor defection is demonstrably right." (p. 225)
- [FACT] "It's a matter of personalities, group psychology, and luck, not game theory; rationality has nothing to do with it." (p. 272)
- [FACT] "the 'explanation' is simply that common sense is wrong." (p. 277)
- [FACT] "The paradox lies in the fact that our concept of rationality is not fixed." (p. 277)
- [INTERPRETATION] The companion file asked for intellectual honesty about where the book's thesis fails, and the book supplies much of it in its own voice. It says outright that most conflicts are not dilemmas, that neither strategy is demonstrably right, that a dilemma requires a live temptation, and that at the end of the escalation analysis rationality has nothing to do with it. That is a popular book refusing to over-sell its own apparatus, and it should be read as a point in the book's favour rather than against it.

## 18. Compression points for synthesis

1. The book names the two strategies defection and cooperation (p. 107) and names the four payoff quadrants reward, punishment, temptation, and sucker (p. 120), which is the book's prose form of the ordinal skeleton in [[prisoners-dilemma-anatomy]]. [FACT]
2. Poundstone's four social dilemmas are Deadlock, Prisoner's Dilemma, Chicken, and Stag Hunt, and he generates the family by explicit payoff swaps from the prisoner's dilemma (p. 217). This generative rule is the book's most portable analytical contribution and it is not yet stated anywhere else in the Substrate. [FACT]
3. The book states that Flood and Dresher doubted Nash's equilibrium as a solution concept (p. 106), which text-verifies the central correction in the companion file's accuracy audit. [FACT]
4. The book verifies the Tucker naming at p. 117 and the prison tale at pp. 117 to 118, so the citation the anatomy file already carries for that attribution is substantively sound. [FACT]
5. The honour-system free-rider passage, with the turnstile illustration and the enforceability definition (pp. 126 to 127), is the material behind the earlier "newspaper-box example" citation. [FACT]
6. Nigel Howard's metagame, in which the strategies are intentions rather than actions (p. 226), is a lineage absent from the Substrate and a candidate for its own node. [FACT plus INTERPRETATION]
7. The escalation traps, the Shubik dollar auction with the 72-cent first bid and the penalty on the party who stops first, and the largest-number game (pp. 260 to 277), form a distinct family from payoff dilemmas and have no Substrate node. [FACT plus INTERPRETATION]
