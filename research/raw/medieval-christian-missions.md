# Medieval Christian Missions: From Gregorian Mission to Boniface

## The Gregorian Mission (596 CE)

Pope Gregory the Great (c. 540 – 604) sent a mission to England in 596 to convert the Anglo-Saxons. The mission was headed by Augustine of Canterbury, prior of Gregory's own monastery in Rome. In 597, the forty missionaries arrived in Kent and converted King Æthelberht, whose wife Bertha was already a Christian Frankish princess.

Gregory's approach was pragmatic and accommodationist. He instructed Augustine to reuse pagan temples and adapt local customs where possible. This created a template for cultural adaptation in mission work that would be debated for centuries.

## The Celtic Missions

Parallel to the Roman/Gregorian mission, Irish and Scottish monks conducted extensive missionary work:
- Columba (521–597): Founded Iona monastery and conducted missions to the Picts in Scotland
- These were typically monastic rather than papally organized

## Boniface: Apostle to the Germans (c. 675 – 754)

Boniface, born Wynfreth, was an English Benedictine monk who became the leading figure in the Anglo-Saxon mission to Germanic parts of Francia. He was made Archbishop of Mainz by Pope Gregory III and was martyred in Frisia in 754 along with 52 companions.

Boniface is known as the "Apostle to the Germans." He organized significant church foundations in Germany and became a patron saint of Germania. His work helped create "the first Europe" through the alliance between the papacy and the Carolingian family.

## Ansgar and the Northern Missions (801–865)

Ansgar, known as the "Apostle to the North," conducted missions to Scandinavia. He represents the northern frontier of medieval Christian expansion.

## Conceptual Characteristics

Medieval mission was typically:
- Tied to monastic movements rather than formal missionary societies
- Conducted by monk-bishops who established churches, monasteries, and schools
- Paralleled political and cultural expansion (mission followed or accompanied empire)
- The "missionary" was not a separate professional category but a monastic or episcopal role
- The goal was expanding Christendom: creating a Christian civilization, not merely individual conversions
