Towards legitimacy infrastructure for new organizational forms

Co-authored by @DrNickA, @spengrah, @bbeats1, @joshuaztan, @trent_vanepps

**I.** 

Organizations of all kinds require legitimacy. Legitimacy is how organizations secure resources and social approval, validate operations to stakeholders, and defend against crises. 

Like DAOs, legitimacy is a tool of coordination and control, yet producing a shared sense of legitimacy is one area where DAOs have failed. In this paper, we argue that this is exactly what a new era of DAOs should target: legitimacy not just for DAOs themselves, but for inspiring a plurality of new organizational forms. 

Despite the paper’s provocative title, this is not another contribution to the bloated genre of pronouncing the DAO experiment dead, nor is it another attempt to redefine DAOs. Instead, at this crucial time for DAOs, crypto, and global institutions alike, we proclaim that DAOs as popularly conceived are dead. We rightfully say farewell to DAOs as a single model of token-holder-governed blockchain organizations, and welcome in DAOs as a modular toolset and primitive of organizational design. 

Today, DAOs operate as treasuries controlled by token-holder voting at best. At worst, DAOs are a thin veneer of regulatory protection—an online forum for public deliberation and democracy theater that masks the internal operations, decision making, and control structures of traditional companies. DAO practices and structures are stuck in familiar patterns that function on the surface, but fundamentally fail to deliver on desired outcomes or advance broad organizational experimentation. Instead of improving upon incumbent organizations, DAOs have brought out the worst of them: the inaction, the misalignment, the extraction. 

As a collective space, we need to do better. We can do better. 

Now that the twin tailwinds for v1 DAOs—regulatory opacity and speculative fervor—have abated, it is time to reassess the progress that has been made, take stock in what is worth saving and what is not, and reorient ourselves towards the next phase in DAO evolution. To ensure the next iteration of DAOs continues to advance the desirable characteristics of effective organizations, we present the following reframing: 

**DAOs are legitimacy infrastructure for new organizational forms.** 

We begin with a brief overview of the current state of what we call “v1 DAOs.” We then zoom out to address larger questions around organizational legitimacy: What is it? Why does it matter? How can DAOs uniquely solve this problem? After setting a foundation for our argument, we explain how DAOs will evolve by outcompeting incumbents for the social authority to coordinate human and technological actors at scale. Because of the information systems that emerge from their components—namely, enforceable technical guarantees and legible systems of coordination—v2 DAOs will become foundational models for configuring and governing internet-native organizations and beyond. As the promise of “internet capital markets” is realized and autonomous technological actors take on increasingly larger roles in economic life, we believe DAO-based architectures will become an essential pillar of organizational design. 

By realizing better outcomes and increasing the coordination capacity of organizational alternatives—legitimacy through performance—DAOs will counter and eventually outcompete existing hegemonies [1]. But first, we must rid ourselves of the concept we currently know as DAOs. 

**II.** 

The empirical record of v1 DAOs is quite definitive and sufficiently well-documented so that there is little to relitigate. Therefore, this section presents a brief retrospective to: 1) capture a historical artifact of the successes and recent failures of DAOs, 2) support the claim that v1 DAOs are indeed dead, and 3) reveal the most pressing challenges that DAOs must tackle in order to evolve. 

To effectively plot a path forward, we must first tip our hats to the contributions v1 DAOs have made to the longer arc of organizational evolution: 

- Core mechanics and automated execution primitives, including smart contract-based architectures 
- Credible distributed control of blockchain protocols and billion-dollar treasuries over multi-year timeframes 
- Scale of funding open-source software and digital public infrastructure 
- Cohort of practitioners with real, money-at-stake experience in digital coordination 
- Body of experiments large enough to rule out substantial regions of the design space 

Despite these achievements, between 2025-2026, many DAOs altered their ownership and control structures, opting to decrease DAO oversight of protocols and treasuries, either consolidating power in more centralized offchain entities or dissolving the DAO altogether. Exacerbating the situation, the DAO tooling and service provider space has been gutted. As the founder of one of the most prominent, yet recently-shuttered DAO governance platforms, Tally, put it, “There isn’t a venture-backed business in governance tooling for decentralized protocols, at least not yet.” 

Part of the reason v1 DAOs are dead is the disappearance of the two forces that sustained demand for DAOs and decentralized governance up until this point: regulatory arbitrage and a growing ecosystem of decentralized applications. The US has eased away from the previous SEC regime’s regulation-by-enforcement framework, removing the imperative to sidestep securities laws by “decentralizing” control over blockchain-based protocols in a DAO. Meanwhile, a rich ecosystem of consumer apps, protocol communities, and onchain organizations simply has not developed, moving speculative capital elsewhere. 

The other, more nuanced reason v1 DAOs are dead: they have not achieved legitimacy as a way to coordinate human, technology, and money at scale. 

Broadly speaking, organizations enable the cooperative environments required to enact higher-level, more complex processes that produce better outcomes than can be delivered through market forces alone. It follows that social conceptions of organizational legitimacy are underpinned by guarantees of specific outcomes actualized through performance. 

Despite the technologies that support them, today’s DAOs lack both the guarantees and the performance record to challenge existing organizational forms for legitimacy. It is worth noting the hyper-nascency of DAOs in the grand scheme of organizational design, as well as the unduly high expectations set upon them given how early we’re into this new form of coordination. Nevertheless, they have so far failed to produce both robust accountability frameworks that promote the effective performance of agents with respect to principles, and ownership structures that ensure value is distributed predictably and fairly. Instead, DAOs have relied on broken decision-making mechanisms (e.g. one-token-one-vote, “decentralize everything”) and poorly designed systems to align incentives (e.g. foundation model, token-equity dual structure). DAOs have confused decentralizing ownership with legitimating authority—often blindly distributing control to anyone who happened to claim token airdrops. They have produced radically populist, “decentralized” structures without ensuring these structures actually lead to good outcomes, even failing to communicate any clear vision regarding their animating purpose beyond regulatory avoidance. 

These “Original Sins of DAOs” have saturated the landscape with value extraction and organizational capture as norms, effectively undermining all three elements of the acronym D.A.O. at once and corroding the widespread acceptance and participation that legitimacy requires. To make matters worse, these default heuristics have now been ossified into common, suboptimal practices, illustrating how the drive for legitimacy can cause organizations to become increasingly homogenous as they conform to coercive, mimetic, and normative societal pressures [2]. As a result, we find ourselves stuck with the most legible inherited structure—what “works”—rather than the most appropriate one. 

While the conventional reading of this situation often points to “DAOs are dead” and “governance is a failed experiment,” we offer another option—one that presents a viable way forward for DAOs. 

**III.** 

This inflection point for DAOs is happening amidst a broader legitimation crisis of our dominant institutions, with public opinion turning against them, current systems losing the capacity to maintain social stability, and monopolistic digital spaces anchoring an increasing portion of our economic and social lives. The breakneck pace of AI innovation is also set to disrupt every aspect of our organizational systems, provoking the question: 

What does coordination and governance look like when the geopolitical and economic landscape changes enough such that it doesn't look anything like it does today? 

While the exact answer remains unclear, the outcome of this crisis regarding which organizational forms capture a shared sense of legitimacy will largely be determined by how new technologies affect their information systems. These systems help promote organizational legitimacy by facilitating: 1) the transmission of information within an organization to create a shared knowledge layer, and 2) the communication of information outside an organization to provide the observational boundaries that make participant activities visible, interpretable, and acceptable to society [3]. 

Forming an organization, as opposed to relying on alternative mechanisms like markets, makes sense when actions must be tightly coordinated to achieve shared goals or a particular purpose. Organizations provide order, guiding people’s actions towards a common point of orientation and maximizing the collective knowledge gained from the combination of specialization and the matching of complementary actions. In the spirit of Hayek and Coase, corporations exist as the dominant organizational form of modern economic life because they have proven themselves most effective at handling the substantial costs incurred to create such an order—one solution to the “knowledge problem” that remains an everlasting consideration in comparative institutional analysis [4]. The information systems built into the present-day corporate form thus reinforce its legitimacy by catalyzing complex coordination that is capable of producing beneficial outcomes and adapting to uncertain, ever-changing environments. 

By definition, DAOs undermine the tried-and-true corporate information systems by removing hierarchy, blurring organizational boundaries, and decentralizing control and authority. Predictably, this disruption raises the amount of asymmetric information and coordination costs in DAOs. Internal stakeholders—those participating and working in DAOs—have to operate within opaque environments, while external stakeholders, such as investors, regulators, and other organizations, are forced to interpret unclear ownership and value distribution structures. The resultant lack of alignment and poor performance has undermined the legitimacy of DAOs as an organizational form. 

Reframing v2 DAOs as legitimacy infrastructure is a dual proposition to: 

- decompose DAOs to their fundamental components, principally their technical guarantees and legible systems of coordination; and 
- encourage dynamic experimentation with modular architectures towards building more robust, accessible information systems. 

These new systems should establish design constraints that align participants and prevent harmful participation, and observational boundaries—the lens—through which both human and technological actors will view and validate their actions within an organization. Just as legal and moral frameworks imposed on systems of corporate actors have allowed sophisticated structures of information processing to emerge at scale, so too can DAO frameworks imposed on systems of human and technological actors. This development will attract an increasing number of diverse, mutually beneficial participants to reassemble modular DAO architectures in novel ways, engendering an abundance of new organizations that will ultimately outperform in the age of internet capital markets and autonomous AI agents. 

**IV.** 

The legitimacy of cryptocurrency has always been underpinned by technical guarantees. The proliferation of DAOs, like the adoption of Bitcoin, reflects participant “confidence” in algorithmically defined consensus and coordination mechanisms [5]. Running on “unstoppable” protocols that offer programmatic execution (i.e. smart contracts) and leveraging blockchain technology and cryptography for verifiability and security, DAOs reduce the risks of bad actors—minimizing platform risk and preventing counterparties from betraying their agreements. The design constraints offered by technical guarantees represent an important first step towards offering digital-native organizational legitimacy free from the overhang of legacy institutions. 

But blockchain technology and smart contracts can't produce legitimacy on their own. To fully enter their “v2 Era,” DAOs must create a shared conception of legitimacy through performance. This implies that the cost of coordinating capital and action realized through DAO-based architectures must be lower than the corporate benchmark. The failure of v1 DAOs to do this has negatively impacted their ability to generate any sense of legitimacy, as well as the resources and collective desire to improve the status quo. 

Structurally, we believe that v2 DAOs will outperform as a consequence of the human tendency to experiment with different types of organizations. Following economist Brian Loasby: “If people and organisations are moderately free to choose their own actions, they provide continuing sources of variety, and at the same time contribute to an environment in which this variety is tested, and which simultaneously provides the stimulus for the development and novel application of capabilities” [6]. As an internet-native substrate for organizations, DAOs are positioned to benefit from the increasing amounts of global capital formation occurring onchain and in other digital spaces. People will inevitably experiment with organizational design while seeking new solutions to their local information problems, coordination tasks, and knowledge structures. 

DAOs will also become an early site of AI agent integration, offering critical information systems for organizations that increasingly divide knowledge and autonomy between human and technological actors. As agents become prominent participants in decision-making and production, the advantages of the corporate form—structurally locked into a mechanism mix over-reliant on siloed human hierarchies—weaken. Building on top of open-source, modular architectures, humans and agents will iterate faster in DAOs, while the following components and tooling will help legitimate the outputs: 

- Credible commitments, realized through staking or other cryptoeconomic mechanisms, mitigate the costs of participant underperformance or misalignment 
- Formal verification through onchain proofs, help provide confidence about the correctness and security of transactions and the underlying code 
- Permissioning, including the use of multisignature wallets, allow for the secure information sharing and built-in redundancy that underpin collaboration between autonomous actors 
- Onchain attestations that make the actions of individual actors attributable, the interactions between actors verifiable, and the resultant reputation networks scalable 
- Public transactions and money flows that reinforce the fairness and accountability of these systems 

Through these technical guarantees and legible systems of coordination, DAOs will facilitate the shared knowledge necessary for successful coordination and legitimation of new types of “autonomous organizations.” 

In order for this vision to be achieved, we must promote proven, purpose-built, DAO-based architectures that appropriately address the challenges faced by organizations—a shift that requires moving past the current one-size-fits-all model and establishing testbeds for experimentation. The current iteration of DAOs have consistently applied a single inflexible, market-like aggregation mechanism (i.e. one-token-one-vote) to every decision, mismatching the mechanism to the information structure. In addition to providing a legible ruleset, DAOs need to offer a more adaptable substrate that can reconfigure boundaries task-by-task and dynamically combine markets, hierarchies, protocols, and agents to address information demands. This could mean “automation at the center, humans at the edges” with AI agents automating day-to-day operations, but it may not. Instead of focusing on one combination, DAOs should instead aim to track the strengths of AI by deploying modular toolsets and autonomous systems that can be combined, recombined, and built upon. It’s up to us then to provide observable sandboxes that permit rapid and safe experiments with real stakes in order to build winning combinations. 

By uncovering specialized solutions through experimentation and then packaging them as reusable components, v2 DAOs will be able to capture greater gains from the specialization and shared knowledge of the future organizational forms that are built on top of them. These gains will manifest as a wider solution space for operations, asset management, and governance—the only reason organizations will become DAOs in the first place. In other words, DAOs need to be successful in order to be legitimate, and it is up to us to consciously create the conditions of their success. 

**V.** 

We write this paper at an existential time not only for DAOs, but also for the ideological backbone of cryptocurrency and blockchain technology. Initially formed as a radical push for digital sovereignty and decentralized coordination, the industry now risks recreating the same extractive, monopolistic power dynamics that pervade modern economic systems, while abandoning one of the brightest hopes to see its vision through. And so, despite the title, we write this paper as an ode to DAOs, celebrating how far they’ve come, while also acknowledging their shortcomings and the need for a new start. 

We cannot let the vision of DAOs die because blockchains need DAOs, just like DAOs need blockchains. 

This cycle of death and rebirth is inherent in all forms of evolution. So far, the evolution of DAOs has featured such breakthroughs such as the launch of Ethereum and The DAO, which proved out the concepts of decentralized capital formation and distributed digital governance at scale, and DeFi Summer, which showed that governance tokens could bootstrap initial liquidity for these organizations. The current proliferation of BORGs, DUNAs, and other variants of onchain organizations point to the next evolutionary stage of DAOs as legitimating an abundance of new types of organizations. 

Legitimation is the process of drawing boundaries around what is desirable, proper, or appropriate, and plays a key role in turning certain conventions and patterns of behavior into the basis of human communication and coordination. Reframing DAOs as legitimacy infrastructure is about raising DAO-based architectures to the level of institutions—the things we don’t necessarily think about when coordinating day-to-day, and yet play a huge role in prefiguring our actions towards common goals. The new DAO-enabled organizations could look like digital cooperatives, municipal governments, non-profits, or even for-profit corporations, some mix of these, or something completely new. Regardless of form, these v2 DAOs will serve as mediator between human and technology, making the boundary between the two more legible, scoped, and legitimate in both directions. 

DAOs will enable an expansion of creative alternatives, promoting legitimacy in the era of organizational slop. This burst of heterogeneous organizations will generate more competitive pressures between the new instantiations and old forms, introducing real opposition to the current host of monopolistic, feudalistic, and top-down control structures that we see in both corporate and digital worlds alike. We need DAOs to provide desirable and feasible alternatives to the status quo—the true meaning of decentralization—and thereby, provide opportunities to create and share value with the broadest possible set of beneficiaries.
