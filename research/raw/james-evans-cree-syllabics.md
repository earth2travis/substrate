# James Evans and Cree Syllabics: The Missionary as Amateur Linguist

## The Man

James Evans (1801–1846) was an English-Canadian Wesleyan Methodist missionary and amateur linguist. He was a teacher before becoming a minister.

## The Invention

Evans created the syllabic writing system for Cree and Ojibwe, later adapted to Inuktitut and other languages. He first tried Roman script for Ojibwe, then modified syllabics inspired partly by Pitman Shorthand.

The system was elegant: each symbol represents a consonant-vowel combination. Rotation of the symbol indicates the vowel. It was easy to learn and could be written with a stick in the snow or ink on paper.

## The Impact

Cree and Ojibwe literacy rates likely outpaced those of neighboring French and English trappers. The syllabics spread rapidly through the Canadian North and remain in use today.

## The Double Edge

Evans's work was also used to transmit Christian texts. Indigenous oral traditions were transcribed, but so were sermons and hymns. Literacy became a tool for both cultural preservation and cultural transformation. The Cree syllabics enabled Indigenous self-expression but also facilitated missionary control.

## The Structural Point

Evans represents the missionary-linguist as boundary-crosser: someone who enters a culture, learns its language, and creates tools for that language to be written. The tool is neutral; its use is political.
