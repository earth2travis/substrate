# NASA Discovery Program

## Program Goal

The goal of NASA's Discovery Program is to provide **frequent flight opportunities** for high-quality, high-value, focused, planetary science investigations that can be accomplished under a **not-to-exceed cost cap**. By conducting a series of planetary science investigations, NASA provides a mechanism by which pressing questions in planetary science may be addressed, permitting a steady improvement in our understanding of planetary systems and the processes that affect them.

The frequent, steady nature of the investigations ensures a continuing stream of fresh scientific data to the planetary science community, helping to maintain the excellence of the U.S. planetary science program and to inspire the next generation of investigators.

## Program Strives To

1. Advance scientific knowledge and exploration of the elements of our Solar System.
2. Add scientific data, maps, and other products to the Planetary Data System archive for all scientists to access.
3. Announce scientific progress and results in the peer-reviewed literature, popular media, scholastic curricula, and materials that can be used to inspire and motivate students to pursue careers in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics.
4. Expand the pool of well-qualified Principal Investigators and Project Managers for implementation of future missions in Discovery and other programs, through current involvement as Co-Investigators and other team members.
5. Implement technology advancements proven in related programs.

## Cost Constraints

The Discovery Program operates under a **not-to-exceed cost cap**. Individual missions are selected to fit within this cost-constrained framework, though specific dollar amounts are not stated on the public-facing page. The emphasis is on producing investigations with the highest possible science value for the price.

## Selection Process

Missions are selected through a competitive process. NASA solicits proposals and evaluates them based on scientific merit, technical feasibility, and cost realism within the program's cost cap.

## Principal Investigator (PI) Responsibilities

Discovery missions are PI-led. The Principal Investigator:
- Is typically affiliated with a university or research institution.
- Selects team members from industry, small businesses, government laboratories, and universities.
- Develops the scientific objectives and instrument payload.
- Is responsible for the overall success of the project by assuring that cost, schedule, and performance objectives are met.

## Discovery Program Missions

| Mission | Years | Description |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| NEAR-Shoemaker | 1996-2001 | First spacecraft to orbit and land on an asteroid (Eros). Returned 10x more data than expected about Eros' surface and interior. |
| Mars Pathfinder | 1996-1997 | First successful rover on Mars (Sojourner). Traveled ~100m, sent images, performed chemical analysis, and measured Mars atmosphere and weather. |
| Lunar Prospector | 1998-1999 | Found first direct evidence of water ice in regolith at Moon's poles; discovered iron-rich core; mapped rock type distribution. |
| Stardust, Stardust NExT | 1999-2011 | First U.S. mission to study a comet. Collected dust from comet 81P/Wild and returned sample capsule to Earth. Extended mission examined effects of Deep Impact on Tempel 1. |
| Genesis | 2001-2004 | Collected solar wind samples at Sun-Earth L1 for 2 years. Sample capsule was damaged on landing in Utah desert, but samples still yielded significant discoveries. |
| CONTOUR | 2002 | Comet Nucleus Tour. Designed to study at least two comets, but overheating during engine burn caused spacecraft to break apart over Indian Ocean. |
| MESSENGER | 2004-2015 | Orbited Mercury for 4 years. Mapped entire surface, returned composition and volcanic history data, verified water ice at poles. |
| Deep Impact, EPOXI | 2005-2013 | Impacted comet Tempel 1 with probe. Extended mission renamed EPOXI studied comet Hartley 2. |
| Dawn | 2007-2018 | First to orbit an asteroid (Vesta) in main belt; first to orbit two extraterrestrial objects; first to visit a dwarf planet (Ceres). Advanced ion engine technology. |
| Kepler Space Telescope, K2 | 2009-2018 | Detected thousands of exoplanets; confirmed first multi-planet system outside solar system. K2 extended mission investigated red dwarf stars. |
| GRAIL | 2011-2012 | Twin spacecraft (Ebb and Flow) orbited Moon to produce high-resolution map of lunar gravitational field variations. |
| InSight | 2018-2022 | Delivered first seismometer to Mars; recorded first "marsquakes." |
| Lucy | 2021-Present | En route to Trojan asteroids. Had extraordinary encounter with main belt asteroid Dinkinesh in Nov 2023, discovering first contact-binary satellite. |
| Psyche | 2023-Present | First mission to study metal-rich asteroid Psyche (possible planetesimal core). Testing DSOC (Deep Space Optical Communications). |
| DAVINCI | Tentative June 2029 | Will study Venus' atmosphere and past/present climate from surface to cloud tops with descent probe. |
| VERITAS | No earlier than 2031 | First orbital mission to Venus since 1990s. Will produce high-resolution topography and radar images. |

## International Partner Instrument Participation

The Discovery Program also selects NASA-funded instruments and participation on missions led by international partners, including:

- **STROFIO** — Instrument aboard BepiColombo mission to Mercury (2018-present)
- **Akatsuki** — JAXA mission to Venus (2010-2015)
- **M3** — ISRO Chandrayaan-1 lunar mission (2008-2009)
- **Hayabusa 2** — JAXA mission to asteroid Ryugu, now extended (2014-present)
- **ASPERA-3** — ESA Mars Express mission (2003-present)
- **MEGANE** — JAXA Mars Moons eXploration (MMX) mission (Launching 2026)
- **P-SMP** — JAXA Mars Moons eXploration (MMX) mission (Launching 2026)
