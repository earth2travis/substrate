# Mission in Business Context: From Religious Sending to Corporate Purpose

## The Etymological Bridge

The word "mission" entered English around 1598, originally of Jesuits sending ("missio," Latin for "act of sending") members abroad. For centuries, a "missionary" was assumed to be a person on a specifically religious mission. The word's migration into business discourse is recent and telling: it represents the secularization of a concept that originally described divine sending, and its application to a domain where purpose must be manufactured rather than received.

A mission statement is a short statement of why an organization exists, what its overall goal is, the goal of its operations: what kind of product or service it provides, its primary customers or market, and its geographical region of operation. It may include a short statement of such fundamental matters as the organization's values or philosophies, a business's main competitive advantages, or a desired future state: the "vision."

Historically it is associated with Christian religious groups. The word "mission" dates from 1598, originally of Jesuits sending ("missio," Latin for "act of sending") members abroad. A mission statement is not simply a description of an organization by an external party, but an expression, made by an organization's leaders, of their desires and intent for the organization. A mission statement aims to communicate the organisation's purpose and direction to its employees, customers, vendors, and other stakeholders. A mission statement also creates a sense of identity for employees.

According to Chris Bart, professor of strategy and governance at McMaster University, a commercial mission statement consists of three essential components:
1. Key market: the target audience
2. Contribution: the product or service
3. Distinction: what makes the product unique or why the audience should buy it over another

Organizations normally do not change their mission statements over time, since they define their continuous, ongoing purpose and focus.

## Management by Objectives: Drucker's Foundation (1954)

Management by objectives (MBO), also known as management by planning (MBP), is a management style involving the defining of specific objectives within an organization and subsequent deciding how to achieve each objective in sequence. It was first popularized by Peter Drucker in his 1954 book The Practice of Management.

In this system of management, individual goals are synchronized with the goals of the organization, with employee performance being measured and compared with set standards. According to MBO theory, when employees themselves have been involved with the goal-setting and choosing the course of action to be followed by them, they are more likely to fulfill their responsibilities.

According to George S. Odiorne, the system of management by objectives can be described as a process whereby the superior and subordinate jointly identify common goals, define each individual's major areas of responsibility in terms of expected results, and use these measures as guides for operating the unit and assessing the contribution of each of its members.

Peter Drucker first used the term "management by objectives" in his 1954 book The Practice of Management. While the basic ideas of MBO were not original to Drucker, he pulled from other management practices to create a complete system. MBO draws on Mary Parker Follett's 1926 essay, "The Giving of Orders." Drucker's student, George Odiorne, continued to develop the idea in his book Management Decisions by Objectives, published in the mid-1960s. MBO was popularized by companies like Hewlett-Packard, which claimed it led to their success.

The five steps of MBO:
1. Review organizational goals
2. Set worker objectives
3. Monitor progress
4. Evaluation
5. Give rewards

Many other corporations praised the effectiveness of MBO, including Xerox, DuPont, Intel. In many large Japanese corporations, beginning in the late 1990s, MBO was used as the basis of "the performance-based merit system" (seika-shugi) which used clear numerical targets to measure performance in contrast to the previous system of non-specific contracts in Japanese companies.

W. Edwards Deming argued that a lack of understanding of systems commonly results in the misapplication of objectives. Deming also stated that setting production targets will encourage workers to meet those targets through whatever means necessary, which usually results in poor quality. Point 7 of Deming's key principles encourages managers to abandon objectives in favour of leadership because he felt that a leader with an understanding of systems was more likely to guide workers to an appropriate solution than the incentive of an objective.

In a 1991 comprehensive review of thirty years of research on the impact of MBO, Robert Rodgers and John Hunter concluded that companies whose CEOs demonstrated high commitment to MBO showed, on average, a 56% gain in productivity. Companies with CEOs who showed low commitment saw only a 6% gain in productivity.

The limitations mentioned above, combined with the challenges faced by modern service companies, have led to the development of methods that integrate aspects of MBO but appear to be significantly more effective in application. These include, for example, the objectives and key results (OKR) method.

## Objectives and Key Results: Grove at Intel (1970s/1983)

Objectives and key results (OKR, alternatively OKRs) is a goal-setting framework used by individuals, teams, and organizations to define measurable goals and track their outcomes. The development of OKR is generally attributed to Andrew Grove who introduced the approach to Intel in the 1970s and documented the framework in his 1983 book High Output Management.

OKRs comprise an objective (a significant, concrete, clearly defined goal) and 3-5 key results (measurable success criteria used to track the achievement of that goal). Not only should objectives be significant, concrete, and clearly defined, they should also be inspirational for the individual, team, or organization that is working towards them. Objectives can also be supported by initiatives, which are the plans and activities that help to move forward the key results and achieve the objective.

Key results should be measurable, either on a 0-100% scale or with any numerical value (e.g. count, dollar amount, or percentage) that can be used by planners and decision makers to determine whether those involved in working towards the key result have been successful. There should be no opportunity for "grey area" when defining a key result.

Andrew Grove popularised the concept of OKR during his tenure at Intel in the 1970s. He later documented OKR in his 1983 book High Output Management. In 1975, John Doerr, at the time a salesperson working for Intel, attended a course within Intel taught by Grove where he was introduced to the theory of OKRs, then called "iMBOs" ("Intel Management by Objectives").

Doerr, who by 1999 was working for venture capital firm Kleiner Perkins, introduced the idea of OKRs to Google in 1999. Doerr presented the idea to Google's founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, who adopted it. OKRs are now used in many major tech companies including Google, LinkedIn, Twitter, Uber, Microsoft.

The OKR system differs from traditional MBO in that OKRs are often set at various levels of the organization, objectives are usually ambitious and may not always be fully achieved (expected achievement rate is ~70%), and they are typically public and transparent within the organization.

## Built to Last: Collins and Porras (1994)

Built to Last: Successful Habits of Visionary Companies is a book written by Jim Collins and Jerry I. Porras. It outlines the results of a six-year research project exploring what leads to enduringly great companies. The first edition of the book was published on October 26, 1994 by HarperBusiness.

The authors identified two primary objectives for the research: "to identify underlying characteristics common to highly visionary companies" and "to effectively communicate findings so that they can influence management."

Collins and Porras defined a visionary company as one that:
- is a premier institution in its industry,
- is widely admired by knowledgeable businesspeople,
- made an imprint on the world,
- had multiple generations of chief executive officers (CEOs),
- had multiple product/service life cycles, and
- was founded before 1950.

The book introduced a number of key ideas:

**Clock Building, Not Time Telling** — go beyond a great leader to building a great institution. The visionary company is not about a single charismatic founder; it is about building mechanisms that outlast any individual.

**No Tyranny of the "Or"** — embrace the genius of "and." Visionary companies do not choose between stability and change, between purpose and profit. They find ways to have both.

**More Than Profits** — find your organization's purpose and build the "core ideology." A visionary company has a core ideology that goes beyond making money. This is not soft or vague; it is a strategic choice that shapes every other decision.

**Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress** — change everything readily, except the core beliefs and values. The core ideology is preserved while everything else is open to change. This is the central paradox of visionary companies: they are simultaneously conservative and progressive.

**Big Hairy Audacious Goals (BHAGs)** — think big, aim high. A BHAG is a clear and compelling goal that serves as a unifying focal point of effort, galvanizing people and creating team spirit. It has a clear finish line, is tangible, energizing, and highly focused. Examples: Boeing's commitment to build the jet age, IBM's System/360, Sony's quest to change Japan's image of poor-quality products.

**Cult-Like Cultures** — cult-like adherence to the culture. Visionary companies have cultures that are almost cult-like in their intensity. This is not about manipulation; it is about creating an environment where people who do not share the core values self-select out.

**Try a Lot of Stuff and Keep What Works** — try a lot of experiments and keep what works. Visionary companies make more attempts and learn faster than comparison companies.

**Home-Grown Management** — hire leaders from within. Visionary companies develop and promote from within at dramatically higher rates than comparison companies.

**Good Enough Never Is** — strive to do better tomorrow than you did today. Visionary companies institutionalize the pursuit of self-improvement.

The list of eighteen companies identified as visionary (each had a comparison company): 3M (Norton), American Express (Wells Fargo), Boeing (McDonnell Douglas), Citicorp (Chase), Disney (Columbia), Ford (GM), GE (Westinghouse), HP (Texas Instruments), IBM (Burroughs), Johnson & Johnson (Bristol-Myers), Marriott (Howard Johnson), Merck (Pfizer), Motorola (Zenith), Nordstrom (Melville), Philip Morris (RJR), Procter & Gamble (Colgate), Sony (Kenwood), Wal-Mart (Ames).

From 1926 through 1990 the comparison companies outperformed the general stock market by 2 times whereas the visionary companies outperformed the market by 15 times.

Criticism: Phil Rosenzweig in "The Halo Effect" criticized the genre of business books that Built to Last belongs to, calling it the "Delusion of Rigorous Research." Many of the companies profiled have subsequently faltered (Motorola, Ford, Sony, Disney, Boeing, Nordstrom, Merck). Collins has responded that "The books never promised that these companies would always be great, just that they were once great." Daniel Kahneman in Thinking, Fast and Slow criticized Collins' overstatement of the importance of good practices relative to sheer luck, arguing that the comparison of firms is to a significant extent a comparison between firms that have been more or less lucky.

## Simon Sinek: Start with Why (2009)

Simon Oliver Sinek (born 9 October 1973) is an American author and inspirational speaker on business leadership. His books include Start with Why (2009) and The Infinite Game (2019).

Sinek was born in Wimbledon. As a child, he lived in Johannesburg, London, and Hong Kong before his family settled in the United States. He earned a BA in cultural anthropology from Brandeis University, then studied law at City, University of London.

Sinek's "Start with Why" introduced the "Golden Circle" concept: three concentric circles labeled Why (inner), How (middle), and What (outer). Most organizations communicate from the outside in: What they do, How they do it, and (maybe) Why they do it. Inspired leaders and organizations communicate from the inside out: Why, then How, then What.

The "Why" is not about making money. It is about purpose, cause, or belief. It is the reason the organization exists. The "How" is the process or the values that bring the Why to life. The "What" is the product or service the organization provides.

Sinek argues that people don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it. The goal is not to do business with everybody who needs what you have. The goal is to do business with people who believe what you believe.

This maps directly to the mission concept: the "Why" is the mission, the "How" is the method, and the "What" is the output. The religious/military/NASA concept of mission as "being sent with purpose" is structurally identical: the Why (commander's intent, divine sending, scientific question) constrains the How and What but does not specify them.

## The Structural Parallel: Business Mission as Secularized Mission

The business concept of "mission" completes the arc that runs through religion, military, NASA, and diplomacy. In each domain, the word migrated from a specific institutional context to a general term for purpose-driven action. In business, the migration is complete: "mission" is no longer tied to any particular institution. It is a generic term for organizational purpose.

But the structural elements are the same:
- **The Sender** (principal): shareholders, founders, stakeholders
- **The Agent** (subordinate): employees, teams, divisions
- **The Mandate** (commander's intent): mission statement, core ideology, Why
- **The Method** (disciplined initiative): OKRs, MBO, agile, delegated execution
- **The Reporting** (backbrief): performance reviews, quarterly reports, stand-ups
- **The Accountability** (tolerance for friction): KPIs, stock price, market share

The business context adds two distinctive features:
1. **Profit as a constraint**: unlike religious or military missions, business missions operate within a profit requirement. The mission must be sustainable.
2. **Voluntary participation**: unlike military hierarchy, business participation is theoretically voluntary. The mission must attract talent.

## From Corporate Mission to Agent Mission

The business literature on mission, purpose, and goal-setting provides the most direct bridge to agent-native operations. In religion, the mission is received. In the military, it is assigned. In NASA, it is selected through peer review. In business, it is chosen, communicated, and incentivized.

Agent-native operations combine all four: the agent receives a mission (like religion), operates within constraints (like military), is selected and evaluated (like NASA), and is incentivized (like business). The business literature provides the most mature toolkit for the last element: how to align an agent's actions with a principal's goals through a combination of communication (mission statement), measurement (OKRs), culture (core ideology), and incentives (rewards).

The unresolved question from the mission arc applies here too: can a business maintain the clarity of charismatic purpose (Stage 1) while operating at the scale of bureaucracy (Stage 3) and the adaptability of networks (Stage 4)? The companies studied by Collins and Porras suggest it is possible, at least for a time. The fact that many have faltered suggests that the answer is not permanent. Mission, even in business, is a dynamic equilibrium, not a solved problem.
