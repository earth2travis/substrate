# The Apostolic Mission: Being Sent as the Foundation

The Christian concept of mission begins with the Greek apostolos: "one who is sent." In the New Testament, this is not a metaphor. It is a structural claim about authority and directionality: the sent one carries the authority of the sender.

## The Great Commission as Mission Statement

Matthew 28:16-20 is the foundational text: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations." This creates a universal scope. The mission is not local, ethnic, or tribal. It is planetary.

## Paul as the Paradigm

Paul the Apostle (c. 5 – c. 64/65 AD) becomes the archetype of the missionary:
- Self-understood as "sent to the Gentiles" (Romans 10:15)
- Established communities, then departed, trusting local leadership
- His suffering authenticated his mission (2 Corinthians 11)
- His letters constitute half the New Testament

## The Structural Insight

In the earliest Church, mission was not an activity the Church had. The Church *was* the mission. There was no separate "missionary" role. Apostleship and local leadership were continuous.

## Connection to Later Forms

This concept of being-sent-with-authority directly connects to:
- Medieval monks sent by papal authority
- Jesuits sent by the Pope to specific territories
- Protestant missionaries sent by societies
- The military concept of mission command (Auftragstaktik): the subordinate carries the commander's intent because they are sent with it

The religious concept of mission predates the military by 1,800 years. The military usage borrowed the structure: the agent is sent with purpose, not merely assigned a task.
