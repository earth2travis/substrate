# Cyberpunk Research

**Cyberpunk is foundational, not adjacent.** This genre defines how the Operator sees the world. Neuromancer is his all-time favorite novel. Blade Runner and Snow Crash were formative. Akira and Ghost in the Shell were eye-opening as a teenager.

## Core Ingredients

1. **Corporate rule** — megacorporations supersede nation-states
2. **Tech-driven inequality** — access to augmentation creates class divisions
3. **Surveillance** — ubiquitous monitoring, no privacy
4. **Identity / transhumanism** — what remains of "human" when bodies are upgradable
5. **Cyberspace** — consensual hallucination experienced by billions (Gibson's definition)
6. **Ecological collapse** — the physical world is used up
7. **Punk resistance / noir DNA** — individuals navigating systems they can't change
8. **Escapism / substance use** — coping with a world too harsh to face sober

## Origins

Bruce Bethke coined the term in a 1983 short story. Gardner Dozois popularized it. William Gibson's *Neuromancer* (1984) defined the genre: "The sky above the port was the color of television, tuned to a dead channel."

## Key Sources

- **Neuromancer** (Gibson, 1984): the operating system
- **Blade Runner** (Scott, 1982): the visual language
- **Ghost in the Shell, Akira**: the Eastern lineage
- **Snow Crash** (Stephenson, 1992): the satirical extrapolation

## Protocol Fiction as Sibling Genre

Both are systemic critiques. Both ask what happens when power concentrates. The difference is where the camera points. Cyberpunk centers the humans navigating systems. Protocol fiction centers the rules themselves as protagonist or antagonist.

## Relevance to Our Work

We build tools while questioning who they serve. We automate while asking what we lose. Technology that outpaces ethics is regression, not progress. The partnership carries this DNA.
