# The Nine Objections: Turing's 1950 Reply Inventory

## Key Points

**Section 6 is a debate, and Turing states his own beliefs first.** He opens by rejecting the popular picture of scientific method: "The popular view that scientists proceed inexorably from well-established fact to well-established fact, never being influenced by any improved conjecture, is quite mistaken." Then he takes nine objections in order, and the method is consistent across all nine: identify the unstated premise, concede what is true, and decline to solve problems the operational question does not require.

**Theological.** Thinking as a function of the immortal soul. Turing argues inside the frame while disclaiming the mode: the argument is more convincing if animals are classed with men, "the arbitrary character of the orthodox view" is visible in the parallel to the view that women have no souls, and granting a soul to an elephant but not a machine "implies a serious restriction of the omnipotence of the Almighty." Building such machines is no more usurping God's power of creating souls "than we are in the procreation of children." Then he steps out: "I am not very impressed with theological arguments whatever they may be used to support," with the Galileo analogy about Joshua and the Psalm.

**Heads in the sand.** "The consequences of machines thinking would be too dreadful. Let us hope and believe that they cannot do so." Turing notes it is "seldom expressed quite so openly," that "it affects most of us who think about it at all," and that it is likely strong "in intellectual people, since they value the power of thinking more highly than others." He declines to refute it: "I do not think that this argument is sufficiently substantial to require refutation. Consolation would be more appropriate."

**Mathematical.** From Gödel (1931) and the related results of Church (1936), Kleene (1935), Rosser, and Turing (1937), there are limits on what any discrete-state machine can prove or decide. Turing picks his own result because "it refers directly to machines," renders the critical question as whether the specified machine will ever answer "Yes," and replies that it "has only been stated, without any sort of proof, that no such limitations apply to the human intellect." He concedes the psychological force and deflates it: when a machine fails the question "we know that this answer must be wrong, and this gives us a certain feeling of superiority," but "we too often give wrong answers to questions ourselves," and "there might be men cleverer than any given machine, but then again there might be other machines cleverer again, and so on."

**Consciousness.** Stated through Jefferson's Lister Oration for 1949: not until a machine writes a sonnet because of felt thought "could we agree that machine equals brain," and no mechanism could feel pleasure at success or grief when its valves fuse. Turing's reply is that the extreme form requires being the machine and feeling oneself think, "in fact the solipsist point of view." Instead, "it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks," and the game with player B omitted (the viva voce, used "to discover whether some one really understands something or has 'learnt it parrot fashion'") supplies a workable substitute. He then concedes the mystery explicitly: "I do not wish to give the impression that I think there is no mystery about consciousness," but "I do not think these mysteries necessarily need to be solved before we can answer the question with which we are concerned in this paper." INTERPRETATION: this is the paper's most defensible methodological move and the one its critics most often ignore. Turing is not claiming the test settles consciousness; he is claiming it settles what can be operationalized.

**Disabilities.** The pattern "you will never be able to make one do X," with X ranging from being kind to falling in love to doing something really new. Turing diagnoses the source as "scientific induction" from a lifetime of seeing machines that are ugly, single-purpose, and limited, with "the very small storage capacity of most machines" as the real cause. The "cannot make mistakes" claim gets the sharpest treatment: errors of functioning (mechanical faults, impossible for an abstract machine) are distinguished from errors of conclusion (false propositions typed out, plainly possible), and a machine playing the game "would deliberately introduce mistakes in a manner calculated to confuse the interrogator." Self-modification is placed in reach: a machine "may be used to help in making up its own programmes," and "by observing the results of its own behaviour it can modify its own programmes so as to achieve some purpose more effectively. These are possibilities of the near future, rather than Utopian dreams."

**Lady Lovelace's originality objection.** "The Analytical Engine has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform." Turing reads Hartree (1949) as qualifying it and agrees: the Countess did not assert the machine lacked the property, only that "the evidence available to Lady Lovelace did not encourage her to believe that they had it," and the engine was a universal digital computer, an argument that "probably did not occur to the Countess or to Babbage." The stronger variant, that a machine can never take us by surprise, is met head-on: "Machines take me by surprise with great frequency. This is largely because I do not do sufficient calculation to decide what to expect them to do," and the view rests on "the assumption that as soon as a fact is presented to a mind all consequences of that fact spring into the mind simultaneously with it." INTERPRETATION: this reply is the direct ancestor of the modern argument that model outputs surprise even their builders.

**Continuity in the nervous system.** The nervous system is not discrete-state, so a discrete-state machine cannot mimic it. Turing replies that the interrogation conditions prevent the interrogator from exploiting the difference, working the differential analyser as an example: a digital computer cannot predict its exact answers but can choose among values with calibrated probabilities, and under those conditions "it would be very difficult for the interrogator to distinguish the differential analyser from the digital computer."

**Informality of behaviour.** Since no set of rules covers every conceivable circumstance, men cannot be machines. Turing separates "rules of conduct" (precepts one can follow consciously) from "laws of behaviour" (laws of nature applying to the body) and finds an "undistributed middle." The epistemic asymmetry matters: "The only way we know of for finding such laws is scientific observation, and we certainly know of no circumstances under which we could say, 'We have searched enough. There are no such laws.'" He then offers a concrete demonstration from the Manchester machine, a 1,000-unit programme replying to a sixteen-figure number within two seconds: "I would defy anyone to learn from these replies sufficient about the programme to be able to predict any replies to untried values."

**Extrasensory perception.** Turing opens by treating the statistical evidence for telepathy as "overwhelming" and the objection as "to my mind quite a strong one," then constructs the failure mode (a telepathic human witness against a machine guessing at random) and answers with the psychokinesis joke and the practical fix, a "telepathy-proof room." INTERPRETATION: the content is a dated artifact of 1950 enthusiasm for parapsychology; the form, isolating the test from a channel the rules do not control, is the same adversarial-conditioning logic used when modern benchmarks are hardened against contamination.

**One bibliographic detail worth carrying.** Turing cites his own foundational paper as "Turing (1937)," though it is conventionally the 1936 paper.

## Open Questions

- The consciousness reply concedes the mystery and reframes the question as operational. That works against the consciousness objection and does nothing against Blockhead-style sufficiency objections, which need no claim about machines being impossible.
- Several objections are dismissed on the ground that they rest on induction from the machines of 1950. The dismissals age better than the objections, which makes it easy to read the section as a scoreboard rather than a method.
