# Production Systems Compared: Craft, Mass, and Lean

## Summary

A deep comparative analysis of three production eras based on the MIT IMVP study (Womack, Jones, Roos, 1990). Craft production (pre-1900s) used highly skilled versatile workers with simple flexible tools, achieving perfect customization but unable to scale. Mass production (1908 onward) narrowed worker skill to single repetitive tasks, used dedicated single-purpose machines, and made to forecast: Model T price dropped from $850 to $290, but defects ran 130 per 100 cars and inventory buffered every stage.

Lean production (1950s onward) absorbed mass's volume while recovering craft's flexibility. Multi-skilled teams understand multiple operations. Flexible right-sized machines achieve quick changeovers in minutes. Workers empowered to stop the line (andon cord) when defects appear. Long-term supplier partnerships replace adversarial bidding.

Three tensions define the transitions: (1) knowledge distributed in individuals vs. centralized in manuals vs. embedded in team practice; (2) waste invisible in craft, accepted as structural in mass, targeted for elimination in lean; (3) change organic in craft, catastrophic in mass retooling events, continuous in lean kaizen.

The succession pattern is clear: each system absorbed predecessor strengths while solving critical weaknesses. Mass absorbed craft's product knowledge into interchangeable parts. Lean absorbed mass's volume while recovering craft's flexibility. Industry 4.0 adds digital sensing to lean's foundation. The revolutionary fourth era would solve lean's culture dependency: a system encoding lean principles into operating infrastructure so the right behavior is default, not aspirational.
