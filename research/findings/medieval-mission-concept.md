# Medieval Missions: Monastic Expansion and the Accommodationist Strategy

## The Gregorian Mission (596 CE)

Pope Gregory the Great's dispatch of Augustine of Canterbury to England established the papal mission model: a centralized authority identifying a target, selecting an agent, and sending them with institutional backing. Gregory also introduced the "accommodationist" strategy: reuse pagan temples, adapt customs, baptize what can be baptized.

This created a tension that persists throughout mission history: how much adaptation is fidelity, and how much is compromise?

## Monastic Missions vs. Papal Missions

Two models operated in parallel:
- **Papal missions**: Centralized, strategic, tied to political objectives (Gregorian mission to England)
- **Monastic missions**: Decentralized, often driven by individual monastic vocation (Columba to the Picts, Irish missions to continental Europe)

## Boniface: The Professional Missionary Bishop

Boniface (c. 675–754), the "Apostle to the Germans," represents the fusion of monastic and papal models. He was a monk sent by papal authority, establishing church foundations and becoming Archbishop of Mainz. His martyrdom in Frisia sealed his legend and created a pilgrimage cult.

## The Expansion of Christendom

Medieval mission was not primarily about individual conversion. It was about creating Christian civilization: churches, schools, monasteries, legal codes. The missionary was a builder of institutions, not merely a preacher of sermons.

## Key Difference from Apostolic Era

The apostolic mission was itinerant and charismatic. The medieval mission was territorial and institutional. Mission shifted from being an attribute of the Church to being an instrument of empire.
