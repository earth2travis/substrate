# Philosophy of the Soul: From Homer to Chalmers

## The Pre-Philosophical Soul

Before philosophy formalized the question, the Greeks had *psyche*, originally meaning "breath" or "life-force." In Homer, the soul is mentioned almost exclusively in contexts of death: it is what departs the body. Homer never attributes thought, feeling, or personality to the soul of a living person. The soul's presence marks the difference between living and dead, but it is not yet the seat of identity.

By the fifth century BCE, *psyche* had expanded to encompass moral character, emotion, cognition, and courage. The stage was set for philosophy to ask: what is this thing, exactly?

## Major Positions

**Plato (Immortal, Separable Soul)**: The soul exists before birth and after death. It is separable from the body (the body is a prison; death is liberation). The true self is the soul, not the body. In *Republic* Book IV, Plato divides the soul into three parts: Reason (*logistikon*), Spirit (*thumoeides*), and Appetite (*epithumetikon*). The just person has these parts in harmony.

**Aristotle (Soul as Form)**: The soul is not a separate substance but the *form* or *actuality* of a natural body that has life potentially. This is **hylomorphism**: every substance is a composite of matter (*hyle*) and form (*morphe*). The soul is to the body as sight is to the eye: the eye's function, not a separate thing inside the eye. Aristotle identifies three levels of soul: nutritive (plants), sensitive (animals), and rational (humans). The active intellect (*nous poietikos*) alone is immortal and eternal.

**Descartes (Substance Dualism)**: The mind (*res cogitans*) is unextended, indivisible, and non-spatial. The body (*res extensa*) is spatial, divisible, and mechanical. The soul is the mind. Animals, lacking rational souls, are merely complex automata. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia posed the sharpest objection: how can a non-physical soul determine the spirits of the body?

**Spinoza (Dual-Aspect Monism)**: Mind and body are not two substances but two *attributes* of one substance (God or Nature). Every physical event has a mental aspect and vice versa.

**Leibniz (Monads)**: Reality consists of simple, indivisible, soul-like substances. His mill argument remains powerful: even if you could walk through a brain enlarged to the size of a mill, you would see only mechanical parts pushing each other around, never a thought.

**Hume (Bundle Theory)**: Looking inward, he found no "self," only a bundle of perceptions in constant flux. "I never can catch myself at any time without a perception." The self is a fiction, a habit of association.

**Kant (Transcendental Idealism)**: The soul as metaphysical substance is unknowable. But the *idea* of the soul is a necessary regulatory principle of reason. We must *act as if* we have unified, continuous selves even if we cannot prove it.

## Contemporary Philosophy of Mind

**Physicalism / Materialism**: Mental states just *are* brain states. Key variants include identity theory (each mental state is identical with a brain state), functionalism (mental states are defined by causal roles; the "stuff" does not matter), and eliminative materialism (folk psychology will be replaced by neuroscience).

**Property Dualism (Chalmers)**: The body is the only substance, but it has physical and mental *properties* that are not reducible. Even a complete physical description of the brain would leave out the *subjective quality* of experience: **qualia**.

**The Hard Problem of Consciousness**: Why is there subjective experience at all? Why does information processing not happen "in the dark," without any inner light?

**Panpsychism**: Consciousness is a fundamental feature of reality, present in some form in all matter. Echoes Leibniz's monads and Indigenous animist traditions.

**Enactivism (Thompson, Varela)**: Consciousness is not something the brain *produces* but something the whole organism *does* in its interaction with the environment. Mind is a process of *sense-making*. The soul is not a thing but an activity.

## The Philosophical Landscape

| Position | Soul is... | Key figures |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| Platonic dualism | An immortal, separable substance | Plato, Augustine |
| Hylomorphism | The form/activity of a living body | Aristotle, Aquinas |
| Substance dualism | A non-physical thinking substance | Descartes |
| Dual-aspect monism | An attribute of one substance | Spinoza |
| Bundle theory | A fiction, just perceptions | Hume |
| Transcendental idealism | A necessary idea, not knowable | Kant |
| Physicalism | Brain states (nothing more) | Smart, Churchland |
| Functionalism | A pattern of causal relations | Putnam, Fodor |
| Property dualism | Irreducible mental properties | Chalmers, Jackson |
| Panpsychism | A fundamental feature of reality | Strawson, Goff |
| Enactivism | Sense-making activity | Thompson, Varela |

The most interesting positions refuse the simple binary of "soul exists" versus "soul doesn't exist." The soul-as-form, soul-as-activity, and soul-as-relation suggest that asking "does the soul exist?" may be the wrong question. Better to ask: what is it that we are pointing at when we use the word?
