# Cree Syllabics: Missionary Literacy and the Double Edge

## The Invention

James Evans (1801–1846) created the Cree syllabic writing system, inspired partly by Pitman Shorthand. The system was elegant: each symbol is a consonant-vowel combination, with rotation indicating the vowel. It could be written with a stick in snow or ink on paper.

## The Impact

Cree and Ojibwe literacy rates outpaced neighboring European settlers. The syllabics spread rapidly across the Canadian North and remain in use today.

## The Double Edge

The same tool served two masters: Indigenous self-expression and missionary control. Oral traditions were transcribed, but so were sermons and hymns. Literacy enabled cultural preservation but also facilitated cultural transformation. The Cree syllabics are still used for Cree language revitalization, but they also carry the history of residential schools and forced assimilation.

## The Structural Point

Tools are politically neutral in design but politically loaded in use. The missionary linguist cannot control how their invention is used after it leaves their hands. This is the general problem of tool creation: the creator's intent is not binding on the user.

## Implication for Agent Systems

Any tool an agent creates for a user will be used in ways the agent did not intend. The question is not whether to create tools, but how to build them such that their misuse is bounded and their beneficial use is amplified.
