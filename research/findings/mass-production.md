# Mass Production

## Summary

Mass production is the early 20th century manufacturing paradigm relying on standardized parts, assembly lines, and specialized labor to produce large volumes of identical goods at low cost. Ford Motor Company's moving assembly line for the Model T (1913) epitomized the approach: production time dropped from 12.5 hours to 93 minutes per car by 1914, and the price fell from $850 (1908) to $260 (1925). Key characteristics include standardization of parts, sequential workstations with single-task workers, narrow division of labor, high volume with low variety, dedicated machinery, and economies of scale. While mass production dramatically lowered costs and made goods affordable to masses, it was inflexible, created high inventory costs, led to worker alienation, and produced goods customers may not want (push production).

## Key Claims

- Ford's Model T assembly line reduced production time by 90% and price by 70% over 17 years
- Standardization and division of labor are the foundational mechanisms
- Push production creates inventory waste and misalignment with demand
- Superseded by lean production, which replaces push with pull and adds continuous improvement
