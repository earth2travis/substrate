# Mission Across Traditions: Comparative Patterns in Religious Sending

## Islamic Da'wah: Decentralized Invitation

Da'wah ("invitation" or "call") is both individual obligation (fard) and communal activity. Unlike the Christian mission as an ecclesial institution, da'wah is decentralized. Any Muslim may invite others to proper practice.

Historical agents: primarily Sufi orders (tariqas) and traders, not formal state missionaries. The Islamization of Indonesia and Malaysia came through merchants and mystics, not armies.

Key difference from Christian mission: no centralized sending authority equivalent to the papacy or missionary society. Da'wah is diffuse, embedded in daily life.

## Buddhist Dharma Propagation: Royal Patronage and Monastic Diffusion

The Buddha's first sending: "Go forth for the good of the many, for the happiness of the many, out of compassion for the world."

Ashoka (c. 304–232 BCE) is the decisive figure. After the Kalinga War, he sent emissaries (dharma mahamatras) throughout his empire and beyond, including to Sri Lanka (via his son Mahinda) and the Hellenistic world.

But unlike Christianity and Islam, Buddhism lacked:
- A continuous centralized sending authority
- Sacred texts commanding universal conversion
- Exclusive truth-claims requiring conversion

Buddhist spread was primarily through cultural diffusion, monastic migration, and philosophical appeal. The 20th-century Japanese concept of fukyo (propagation) adopted modern proselytizing methods, but this was a recent development.

## Comparative Patterns

| Tradition | Sending Authority | Agent Type | Primary Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Christianity (apostolic) | Divine commission | Apostles | Itinerant preaching, community formation |
| Christianity (medieval) | Papal/monastic | Monk-bishops | Institutional establishment |
| Christianity (Jesuit) | Papal/Society of Jesus | Professional missionaries | Cultural accommodation, education |
| Christianity (Protestant) | Mission societies | Professional missionaries | Bible translation, institutional replication |
| Christianity (Missio Dei) | Divine Trinity | Local congregations | Participation in God's action |
| Islam (da'wah) | Individual obligation | Sufis, traders, individuals | Personal invitation, Sufi networks |
| Buddhism | Royal/monastic | Monks, emissaries | Textual transmission, philosophical appeal |

## The Structural Arc

Across traditions, "mission" evolves from:
1. **Divine/charismatic sending** (apostles, Buddha's first disciples)
2. **Institutional expansion** (papacy, monastic orders, Sufi tariqas)
3. **Bureaucratic professionalization** (mission societies, colonial structures)
4. **Decentralized/participatory models** (Missio Dei, diffuse da'wah)

The religious history of mission traces a curve from personal calling to global institution and back to distributed participation. This arc mirrors the evolution of modern organizational forms, from charismatic leadership to corporate bureaucracy to networked autonomy.
