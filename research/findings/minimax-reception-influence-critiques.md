# Minimax Theorem: Reception, Influence, and Critiques

## Key Points

**The reception arc: ignored by economists, adopted by the Air Force, canonized by a central bank.** The 1944 von Neumann-Morgenstern book saw slow uptake (Hurwicz 1945, Marschak 1946 reviews; the mathematics forbidding, the zero-sum focus seemingly irrelevant to price theory). The first institutional home was RAND, 1946 onward, funded under Air Force contracts for nuclear strategy. Schelling's "The Strategy of Conflict" (1960) turned the framework inside out: the interesting problems are non-zero-sum coordination, focal points, commitment. The 1994 Nobel to Harsanyi, Nash, and Selten "for their pioneering analysis of equilibria in the theory of non-cooperative games" (verified wording) functioned as institutional consecration fifty years after the book.

**Economics absorbed Nash, not the saddle point.** Auction theory (Vickrey 1961; 1996 Nobel), matching (Gale-Shapley 1962; Roth-Shapley 2012 Nobel), and mechanism design (2007 Nobel) rebuilt microeconomics on strategic foundations and moved the field from describing behavior to designing institutions, the strongest rebuttal to the "cannot predict" critique: in engineering mode the theory is prescriptive by construction. Minimax itself is nearly invisible in this absorption; the zero-sum core was scaffolding.

**Evolutionary game theory answered the rationality critique before economists fully posed it.** Maynard Smith and Price ("The Logic of Animal Conflict," Nature 246:15-18, 1973, verified) introduced the ESS: a strategy uninvadable by mutants once fixed in a population. Equilibrium becomes a population statistic enforced by selection, not a cognitive achievement. Axelrod's tournaments then made game theory legible to political scientists and biologists simultaneously.

**Five critiques, five targets.** Rationality: ultimatum games and Goeree-Holt's "Ten Little Treasures" (2001) show equilibrium is a poor one-shot predictor for naive subjects; the honest verdict splits by setting, weak for one-shot low-stakes play, better with experience, stakes, and institutions. Zero-sum framing: Schelling from inside, 1960, calling zero-sum the degenerate case. Cold War provenance: Mirowski's "Machine Dreams" (2002, verified), game theory as cyborg science inseparable from military patronage; the theorems are not false, but the questions chosen and the audience funded were not innocent. Biographical politics: Leonard (2010) locates the Cold War inside the theorist. Predictive failure: Green and Shapiro's "Pathologies of Rational Choice Theory" (1994, verified), rebutted in the 1995-96 Critical Review symposium, repaired by Ostrom's behavioral middle path (1998; 2009 Nobel).

**Where minimax is genuinely load-bearing today.** Security games (Tambe's ARMOR at LAX since 2007, GUARDS, Coast Guard PROTECT, verified): Stackelberg leader-follower games, recognizably minimax in intellectual core, deployed and bounded. Robust optimization (Ben-Tal/Nemirovski, Bertsimas/Sim). Adversarial ML (Madry et al.). The seventy-year retreat from "theory of all strategic interaction" to "right tool for a specific class of worst-case problems" is maturation, not failure.

**The agent-systems angle: proven versus aspirational.** Proven: in well-defined two-player zero-sum games, regret-minimizing dynamics converge to minimax equilibrium, and this runs real systems (CFR poker AIs, AlphaZero's self-play lineage, some security allocations). Aspirational: AI-safety-via-debate and scalable oversight frame oversight as a zero-sum game whose equilibrium surfaces truth, but no one has proven debate equilibria are truthful in general (Barnes-Christiano 2020 shows the picture is subtler). Von Neumann's theorem is doing real work in AI again, but the alignment community's use of it is a bet, not a theorem.
