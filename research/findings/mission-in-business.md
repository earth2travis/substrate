# Mission in Business: From Religious Sending to Corporate Purpose

## The Etymological Bridge

The word "mission" entered English around 1598, originally of Jesuits sending ("missio," Latin for "act of sending") members abroad. For centuries, a "missionary" was assumed to be a person on a specifically religious mission. The word's migration into business discourse is recent and telling: it represents the secularization of a concept that originally described divine sending, and its application to a domain where purpose must be manufactured rather than received.

A mission statement is a short statement of why an organization exists: what it provides, to whom, and how it is distinct. It aims to communicate purpose and direction to employees, customers, vendors, and other stakeholders, and creates a sense of identity for employees. Organizations normally do not change their mission statements over time, since they define continuous, ongoing purpose and focus.

According to Chris Bart (McMaster University), a commercial mission statement consists of three essential components:
1. **Key market**: the target audience
2. **Contribution**: the product or service
3. **Distinction**: what makes the product unique or why the audience should buy it over another

## Management by Objectives: Drucker's Foundation (1954)

Management by objectives (MBO) is a management style involving the defining of specific objectives within an organization and subsequent deciding how to achieve each objective in sequence. It was first popularized by Peter Drucker in his 1954 book *The Practice of Management*.

In this system, individual goals are synchronized with organizational goals, with employee performance measured against set standards. According to MBO theory, when employees themselves have been involved with goal-setting and choosing the course of action, they are more likely to fulfill their responsibilities.

The five steps of MBO:
1. Review organizational goals
2. Set worker objectives
3. Monitor progress
4. Evaluation
5. Give rewards

MBO was popularized by companies like Hewlett-Packard, Xerox, DuPont, and Intel. In many large Japanese corporations, beginning in the late 1990s, MBO was used as the basis of "the performance-based merit system" (seika-shugi), using clear numerical targets to measure performance.

W. Edwards Deming argued that a lack of understanding of systems commonly results in the misapplication of objectives. Deming stated that setting production targets encourages workers to meet those targets through whatever means necessary, which usually results in poor quality. Point 7 of Deming's key principles encourages managers to abandon objectives in favor of leadership: a leader with an understanding of systems is more likely to guide workers to an appropriate solution than the incentive of an objective.

In a 1991 comprehensive review of thirty years of research, Robert Rodgers and John Hunter concluded that companies whose CEOs demonstrated high commitment to MBO showed, on average, a **56% gain in productivity**. Companies with CEOs who showed low commitment saw only a **6% gain**.

The limitations of MBO led to the development of methods that integrate aspects of MBO but appear significantly more effective: notably, the objectives and key results (OKR) method.

## Objectives and Key Results: Grove at Intel (1970s/1983)

Objectives and key results (OKR) is a goal-setting framework used by individuals, teams, and organizations to define measurable goals and track their outcomes. The development of OKR is generally attributed to Andrew Grove, who introduced the approach to Intel in the 1970s and documented it in his 1983 book *High Output Management*.

OKRs comprise an objective (a significant, concrete, clearly defined goal) and 3-5 key results (measurable success criteria used to track achievement). Objectives should be inspirational. Key results should be measurable on a 0-100% scale or with any numerical value; there should be no "grey area."

In 1975, John Doerr, then a salesperson at Intel, attended a course taught by Grove where he was introduced to the theory of OKRs, then called "iMBOs" ("Intel Management by Objectives"). Doerr, by 1999 working at Kleiner Perkins, introduced OKRs to Google. OKRs are now used at Google, LinkedIn, Twitter, Uber, Microsoft.

The OKR system differs from traditional MBO: objectives are often set at various levels of the organization, are usually ambitious and may not always be fully achieved (expected rate ~70%), and are typically public and transparent within the organization.

## Built to Last: Collins and Porras (1994)

*Built to Last: Successful Habits of Visionary Companies* by Jim Collins and Jerry I. Porras (1994) reports on a six-year research project exploring what leads to enduringly great companies.

A visionary company is defined as one that: is a premier institution in its industry; is widely admired by knowledgeable businesspeople; made an imprint on the world; had multiple generations of CEOs; had multiple product/service life cycles; and was founded before 1950.

Key ideas:

**Clock Building, Not Time Telling** — go beyond a great leader to building a great institution. The visionary company is not about a single charismatic founder; it is about building mechanisms that outlast any individual.

**No Tyranny of the "Or"** — embrace the genius of "and." Visionary companies do not choose between stability and change, between purpose and profit. They find ways to have both.

**More Than Profits** — find your organization's purpose and build the "core ideology." A visionary company has a core ideology that goes beyond making money. This is a strategic choice that shapes every other decision.

**Preserve the Core / Stimulate Progress** — change everything readily, except the core beliefs and values. The core ideology is preserved while everything else is open to change. This is the central paradox: simultaneously conservative and progressive.

**Big Hairy Audacious Goals (BHAGs)** — think big, aim high. A BHAG is a clear and compelling goal that serves as a unifying focal point of effort, galvanizing people and creating team spirit. Examples: Boeing's commitment to build the jet age; Sony's quest to change Japan's image of poor-quality products.

**Cult-Like Cultures** — cult-like adherence to the culture. Visionary companies have cultures that are almost cult-like in their intensity. This is not manipulation; it is about creating an environment where people who do not share the core values self-select out.

**Try a Lot of Stuff and Keep What Works** — try a lot of experiments and keep what works. Visionary companies make more attempts and learn faster than comparison companies.

**Home-Grown Management** — hire leaders from within at dramatically higher rates.

**Good Enough Never Is** — institutionalize the pursuit of self-improvement.

The eighteen visionary companies (with comparison companies in parentheses): 3M (Norton), American Express (Wells Fargo), Boeing (McDonnell Douglas), Citicorp (Chase), Disney (Columbia), Ford (GM), GE (Westinghouse), HP (Texas Instruments), IBM (Burroughs), Johnson & Johnson (Bristol-Myers), Marriott (Howard Johnson), Merck (Pfizer), Motorola (Zenith), Nordstrom (Melville), Philip Morris (RJR), Procter & Gamble (Colgate), Sony (Kenwood), Wal-Mart (Ames).

From 1926 through 1990, comparison companies outperformed the general stock market by 2 times; visionary companies outperformed by **15 times**.

Criticism: Phil Rosenzweig in *The Halo Effect* criticized the genre as the "Delusion of Rigorous Research." Many profiled companies subsequently faltered (Motorola, Ford, Sony, Disney, Boeing, Nordstrom, Merck). Collins responded: "The books never promised that these companies would always be great, just that they were once great." Daniel Kahneman in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* argued that the comparison is to a significant extent a comparison between firms that have been more or less lucky; regular patterns in the presence of randomness can only be mirages.

## Simon Sinek: Start with Why (2009)

Simon Sinek introduced the "Golden Circle" concept: three concentric circles labeled Why (inner), How (middle), and What (outer). Most organizations communicate from the outside in: What they do, How they do it, and (maybe) Why they do it. Inspired leaders communicate from the inside out: Why, then How, then What.

The "Why" is not about making money. It is about purpose, cause, or belief: the reason the organization exists. The "How" is the process or values that bring the Why to life. The "What" is the product or service.

Sinek argues: people don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it. The goal is not to do business with everybody who needs what you have. The goal is to do business with people who believe what you believe.

This maps directly to the mission concept: the "Why" is the mission, the "How" is the method, and the "What" is the output. The religious/military/NASA concept of mission as "being sent with purpose" is structurally identical: the Why (commander's intent, divine sending, scientific question) constrains the How and What but does not specify them.

## The Structural Parallel: Business Mission as Secularized Mission

The business concept of "mission" completes the arc that runs through religion, military, NASA, and diplomacy. In each domain, the word migrated from a specific institutional context to a general term for purpose-driven action. In business, the migration is complete: "mission" is no longer tied to any particular institution. It is a generic term for organizational purpose.

But the structural elements are the same:

| Element | Religious | Military | NASA | Business | Agent-Native |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sender | God | Commander-in-Chief | Congress/NSB | Shareholders/Founders | Human operator/principal |
| Agent | Apostle/Missionary | Subordinate/Unit | PI/Project Team | Employee/Team | AI agent/subagent |
| Mandate | Great Commission | Commander's Intent | Decadal Survey | Mission Statement/Why | GitHub Issue/spec |
| Method | Adaptation/evangelism | Disciplined Initiative | PI-led design | OKRs/MBO/agile | Tool selection/plan |
| Reporting | Letters/epistles | Backbrief/SITREP | Quarterly reviews/DR | Performance reviews | PRs/commits/logs |
| Accountability | Divine judgment | UCMJ/court martial | Budget audit/public | Stock price/KPIs | Evals/slashing |

The business context adds two distinctive features:
1. **Profit as a constraint**: unlike religious or military missions, business missions operate within a profit requirement. The mission must be sustainable.
2. **Voluntary participation**: unlike military hierarchy, business participation is theoretically voluntary. The mission must attract talent.

## From Corporate Mission to Agent Mission

The business literature on mission, purpose, and goal-setting provides the most direct bridge to agent-native operations. In religion, the mission is received. In the military, it is assigned. In NASA, it is selected through peer review. In business, it is chosen, communicated, and incentivized.

Agent-native operations combine all four: the agent receives a mission (like religion), operates within constraints (like military), is selected and evaluated (like NASA), and is incentivized (like business). The business literature provides the most mature toolkit for the last element: how to align an agent's actions with a principal's goals through a combination of communication (mission statement), measurement (OKRs), culture (core ideology), and incentives (rewards).

The unresolved question from the mission arc applies here too: can a system maintain the clarity of charismatic purpose (Stage 1) while operating at the scale of bureaucracy (Stage 3) and the adaptability of networks (Stage 4)? The companies studied by Collins and Porras suggest it is possible, at least for a time. The fact that many have faltered suggests that the answer is not permanent. Mission, even in business, is a dynamic equilibrium, not a solved problem.
