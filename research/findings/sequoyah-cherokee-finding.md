# Indigenous Literacy: Sequoyah and the Difference of Bottom-Up Invention

## The Contrast

Most missionary linguistics is top-down: a foreign missionary creates a writing system for an indigenous people. Sequoyah's Cherokee syllabary (1821) was the reverse: a Cherokee creating a writing system for Cherokee.

## The Impact

Within 25 years, the Cherokee Nation achieved near-universal literacy, surpassing surrounding European-American settlers. The Cherokee Phoenix (1828) was the first Native American newspaper. The syllabary unified a divided nation and enabled political organization, legal documentation, and cultural preservation under pressure of forced removal.

## The Structural Difference

When literacy is imposed from outside, it is an instrument of control. When it is created from within, it is an instrument of self-determination. The Cherokee *owned* their literacy in ways that recipients of missionary scripts rarely did.

## The Shadow

Sequoyah's syllabary is believed to have inspired 21 scripts across 65 languages in North America, Africa, and Asia. A single indigenous invention became a template for literacy movements worldwide.

## Implication for Systems

Bottom-up tool creation produces different ownership dynamics than top-down tool provision. The agent that creates its own tools (or helps a user create theirs) builds different trust than the agent that delivers pre-built tools.
