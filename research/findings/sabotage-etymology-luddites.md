# The Sabot and the Great Enoch

## Key Points

**"Sabotage" was about presence, not destruction.** The English noun dates to 1907 (from 1903 as a French word in English), defined then as "malicious damaging or destruction of an employer's property by workmen." It derives from French *sabotage*, from *saboter*, "to bungle, walk noisily," from *sabot*, "wooden shoe" (etymonline, verified live 2026-09-26). The famous image of workers throwing wooden shoes into the gears is explicitly not supported by the etymology: the documented early senses are deliberately bad work, not broken machines. The Liberty Review (January 1907) glossed it as "scamping work"; Arturo Giovannitti (1907) as "going slow with the process of production when the bosses go slow with the same process in regard to wages." The military sense of clandestine destruction is a World War I extension; the verb dates to 1912. Sabotage-as-recorded is closer to work-to-rule than to frame-breaking.

**The Great Enoch hammers are the sharpest irony in the Luddite record.** The croppers' frame-breaking sledgehammers were named "Great Enoch" after Enoch Taylor of Marsden, whose firm manufactured both the shearing frames the croppers destroyed and the hammers they destroyed them with (corroborated across Stuff You Missed in History Class, the ACM paper "Unmaking as Emancipation" (2023), and documentation of the ballad "The Cropper Lads," Roud TYG 62, 148). The ballad personifies the hammer as a movement leader: "Great Enoch he shall lead the van. / Stop him who dare, stop him who can." A real manufacturer's name marched alongside the invented General Ludd. The mill economy sold both the problem and the protest.

**Both artifacts get misremembered in the same direction.** The Luddites become technophobes; sabotage becomes shoe-throwing. In each case the remembered version centers the machine (smash it, jam it) and the documented version centers the relationship: who is paid, who owns the surplus, who has standing. The folk versions are more cinematic and less threatening, because a story about people hating machines never has to answer the distribution question the real actors were asking. That flattening is itself the finding.

## Relevance

This is a companion node to [[luddites-and-the-agent-loom]], extending its Hobsbawm "collective bargaining by riot" correction into etymology and material culture. Three sharpenings follow. First, the sabotage record gives the [[centaur-principle]]'s judgment-over-translation line a linguistic fossil: even the word for labor resistance was rewritten to center the machine, which is the same move as calling agent-era critics "Luddites." Second, the Great Enoch detail is the cleanest capsule yet of the surplus-capture argument in the parent node and in [[lean-doctrine]]: one supply chain profited from the frame and the hammer alike, the industrial analog of a platform selling both the automation and the anxiety content about the automation. Third, the pairing of a real manufacturer's name with a synthetic General Ludd is a provenance curiosity for [[agent-provenance-graph]]: the movement signed its violence with the name of the man who armed both sides, attribution by accident rather than design.
