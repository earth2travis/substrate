# Protestant Missions: From Individual Calling to Bureaucratic Enterprise

## The Pivot

William Carey's 1792 essay, "An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens," was the pivot point. Carey argued that the Great Commission was still in force and that Christians were obligated to use "means" (organized methods, funding, institutions) to fulfill it.

This transformed mission from a charismatic calling into a planned, funded, administered enterprise.

## The Institutional Explosion

Within three decades of Carey's essay:
- 1792: Baptist Missionary Society
- 1795: London Missionary Society
- 1799: Church Missionary Society
- 1810: American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions
- 1812: First wave of American missionaries (including Adoniram Judson)

## The Modern Missionary

The Protestant missionary became a new social type:
- Professionally trained
- Financially supported by home committees
- Reporting back through printed periodicals
- Deployed to "mission fields" understood as geographic categories
- Often combining evangelism with Western medicine, education, and "civilization"

## David Livingstone: Mission as Exploration

Livingstone (1813–1873) combined missionary vocation with geographic exploration and anti-slavery activism. His famous triad: "Christianity, Commerce, and Civilization." Mission became indistinguishable from Western expansion.

## The Student Volunteer Movement

The 1886 watchword: "The evangelization of the world in this generation." This was mission as mass movement, scaled to the industrial era. Thousands of university students committed to overseas service.

## The Colonial Critique

By the early 20th century, the Protestant mission enterprise faced a devastating critique: was it spreading the Gospel, or spreading Western civilization? The two had become inseparable. This crisis set the stage for Missio Dei.
