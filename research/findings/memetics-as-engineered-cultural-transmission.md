# Memetics: Origins, Evolution, and Cultural Transmission

## Summary

A lineage of thinkers who understood ideas as replicators with their own selection pressures, from Gabriel Tarde's laws of imitation (1890) through Dawkins' selfish gene (1976) to contemporary memetic warfare. The core insight: memes don't serve the interests of their hosts. Like genes, they serve their own replication. The fidelity, fecundity, and longevity of a meme determine its survival, not its truth or usefulness.

## The Lineage

**Gabriel Tarde — Imitation (1890).** Society is not a structure but a process of copying. Ideas propagate from the prestigious to the less prestigious, from the familiar to the novel, cycling between custom and fashion.

**William S. Burroughs — Language as Virus (1962).** Language and images are parasitic organisms that replicate through human hosts. His cut-up technique was an attempt to build immunity to memetic infection through deliberate noise injection.

**Richard Dawkins — The Selfish Gene (1976).** Chapter 11: "Memes: the new replicators." A meme is a unit of cultural transmission. Memes propagate by leaping from brain to brain via imitation. Dawkins intended this as illustration; others took it further.

**Neal Stephenson — Snow Crash (1992).** Depicted a "neurolinguistic virus" that infects through both biological and digital vectors. Made memetics visceral for a generation of technologists.

**Douglas Rushkoff — Media Virus (1994).** A "media virus" is a story or image that spreads through the media ecosystem, carrying hidden memes inside it like genes inside a virus shell. Controversial content goes viral because it attacks the immune system of consensus reality.

**Susan Blackmore — The Meme Machine (1999).** Human brains evolved specifically to be good meme machines. Introduced "memeplexes": groups of memes that co-replicate. Religions and political ideologies are memeplexes. They survive not because individual memes are true but because the complex is good at replicating.

**Daniel Dennett — Consciousness Explained (1991) and Darwin's Dangerous Idea (1995).** Human consciousness itself might be a memetic construct: we are "meme machines" running cultural software on biological hardware. The self is a "center of narrative gravity" constructed from absorbed memes.

**The Internet Era (2000s).** The term "meme" trivialized to "funny image with text." But the trivialization is itself memetically interesting: the academic meaning couldn't compete with the simpler, more transmissible version.

**Memetic Warfare (2010s-2020s).** DARPA funded "narrative networks" research. The 2016 US election brought "meme war" into common usage. State actors deliberately engineer memetic campaigns. The Extropians imagined memes as tools for liberation. The reality is memetic engineering is primarily used for manipulation.

## What Holds Up

- Ideas as replicators with their own selection pressures
- Language and media as transmission vectors with their own dynamics
- Memeplexes: ideas that co-replicate in clusters
- The neutrality of memetic propagation: the mechanism doesn't care about truth

## What Doesn't

- Memetics as a hard science: never developed rigorous measurement or prediction
- The optimistic view that spreading good ideas faster would improve things
- The word "meme" itself: it mutated beyond recognition, which ironically proves the theory

## Connection to Agent Systems

**Agent communication.** Agents passing information between each other are engaging in memetic transmission. When one agent's output becomes another agent's input, the information mutates, gets compressed, gets reframed. Understanding memetic dynamics helps design better agent communication protocols.

**Onchain identity.** Farcaster profiles, onchain attestations, and social graphs are memetic infrastructure. They are the substrate through which ideas about a person propagate. Your onchain identity is a memeplex.

**DAO governance.** Governance proposals are memes competing for adoption. The ones that spread and reach quorum are the ones with the best memetic fitness, not necessarily the best ideas.

**The autonomous organization.** Agent-native DAOs will have their own memetic dynamics. Agents will develop shared representations, institutional knowledge, organizational culture. The question of how to preserve useful organizational memes while allowing evolution is a design problem we'll need to solve.
