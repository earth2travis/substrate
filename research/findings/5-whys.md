# The 5 Whys: Root Cause Analysis

The 5 Whys is a root cause analysis technique developed by Sakichi Toyoda and formalized by Taiichi Ohno at Toyota. The practice has older philosophical roots: Plato's *Meno*, Aristotle's four causes, and Leibniz's iterative questioning. What Toyota did was industrialize it.

## Origins and Context

The technique was born in the Toyota Production System (TPS), a sociotechnical philosophy developed by Ohno and Eiji Toyoda between 1948 and 1975. TPS rests on two pillars:

- **Just in time:** making only what is needed, when needed, in the amount needed
- **Jidoka:** automation with a human touch — when something goes wrong, production stops

The 5 Whys is kaizen's diagnostic tool. In a culture where improvement is the default state, you need a reliable method for figuring out what to improve next.

## The Methodology

1. **Define the problem** — clearly, specifically, without blame
2. **Ask "Why?" repeatedly** — typically five times, but the number is a heuristic not a rule
3. **Identify root causes** — systemic gaps: missing processes, flawed assumptions, absent safeguards
4. **Define corrective actions** — concrete, owned, tracked, verified
5. **Document and follow through** — the step where most analyses fail

**Good "why":** specific, verifiable, points to a condition or process, advances the causal chain meaningfully
**Bad "why":** stops at a person, vague, circular, blames rather than explains

## Complementary Tools

- **Fishbone diagrams:** map breadth across categories; 5 Whys drills depth. Use fishbone first to identify branches, then 5 Whys to drill into them
- **Swiss cheese model:** addresses the tendency toward single-cause explanations by showing how multiple defenses fail simultaneously
- **Fault tree analysis:** formal Boolean logic for complex, safety-critical systems
- **Pre-mortem:** forward-looking complement — imagine failure before it happens
- **A3 thinking:** single-page constraint forcing clarity and structured thinking

## Common Failures

- Stopping too early at proximate causes
- Blame-focused questioning that kills honesty
- Single-cause bias when reality is multi-factorial
- Investigator knowledge limits — you cannot find causes you don't know exist
- Lack of follow-through: analysis without action is theater

## For Human-AI Partnerships

Adaptations needed:
- **AI self-knowledge is limited:** training and context window dynamics are not fully transparent to the AI itself
- **Memory is asymmetric:** the human remembers across sessions; the AI wakes fresh
- **No separate facilitator:** both partners are investigators and investigated
- **The system is a third actor:** platform behavior must be accounted for
- **Documentation serves double duty:** it is both record and continuity

When something goes wrong: notice and name it, gather evidence, run the 5 Whys together, check for multiplicity, define actions, document, follow through, and review.
