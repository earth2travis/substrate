# Craft, Mass, and Lean Production: Three Paradigms Compared

## Summary

Manufacturing evolved through three dominant paradigms: craft production (pre-industrial, bespoke, skill-intensive), mass production (Ford, 1913, standardized scale-driven), and lean production (Toyota post-WWII, waste-eliminating, flow-oriented). Each solved its predecessor's critical failure while introducing new tradeoffs.

Craft production delivered highest quality and complete customization but could not scale. Mass production democratized access (Model T dropped from $850 to $260) but accepted waste as the price of scale: 130 defects per 100 cars, weeks of buffer inventory, and worker alienation. Lean production, born from postwar Japan's capital scarcity, achieved half the labor hours, half the factory space, one-third fewer defects, and fraction of the inventory (2 hours vs. 2 weeks) while maintaining high variety.

The MIT IMVP study (Womack, Jones, Roos, 1990) across 90 plants and 14 countries codified these differences. The current evolution (Industry 4.0) adds digital sensing and real-time data to lean's foundation. The revolutionary next step would solve lean's remaining weakness: culture dependency. Lean fails 80-95% of transplants because the philosophy requires sustained human commitment that most organizations cannot maintain.

Key sources: "The Machine That Changed the World" (1990), "The Toyota Production System" (Ohno, 1988), "Scientific Management" (Taylor, 1911).
