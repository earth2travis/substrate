# SITREP: Origin, Evolution, and Canonical Structure

## Summary

Research tracing the SITREP (Situation Report) term from its military origins through emergency management and intelligence usage, to inform the design of a SITREP pattern for AI agent sessions. The term emerged during World War II in Anglo-American military communications and was doctrinally codified through FM 101-5 (1940-1984) and its successor ATP 6-0.5 (2016).

## Etymology and First Use

"SITREP" is a military portmanteau: SITuation REPort, following the Anglo-American tradition of contracting compound nouns into pronounceable abbreviations (cf. INTREP, MEDEVAC, COMINT). Standardized term emerged in WWII (approximately 1941-1945), driven by the U.S. Army's General Staff officer procedure (G-1/G-2/G-3/G-4 system), combined Allied command structures under Eisenhower, and the rise of HF/VHF radio communications. Appears in 1942-1945 British War Office and SHAEF archives; routine by the 1944 Normandy campaign.

Doctrinal lineage: FM 101-5 (Staff Officer's Manual, first published 1940, the 1984 edition is the Cold War "classic") → ATP 6-0.5 (2016, current Army tactical SITREP basis).

## Canonical Military Format

Standard periodic SITREP (U.S. Army, FM 101-5 / ATP 6-0.5 lineage), sent by a unit to higher headquarters:

1. Header/Identification (unit, classification, DTG, reference to previous report)
2. Situation Overview (operational environment narrative)
3. Friendly Forces Situation (readiness, disposition, significant changes)
4. Enemy Situation (disposition, strength, activity, PIR answers)
5. Operations/Activities (tasks executed, engagements, ongoing missions)
6. Logistics and Sustainment (supply classes I-X, critical shortages, resupply plan)
7. Personnel and Casualties (KIA/WIA/MIA, replacements, current strength)
8. Command and Control/Signal (C2 node status, comms status, planned changes)
9. Assessment and Commander's Comments (threat/risk changes, intent for next period)
10. Next Report (when due)

Format notes: terse, not continuous prose. DTG in Zulu time. Continuity implicit via report numbering and "SINCE LAST SITREP" framing. Typically 24-hour period, can be 6 or 12 hours. Structure mirrors the five-paragraph operations order (SMEAC: Situation/Mission/Execution/Admin+Logistics/Command+Signal).

NATO variant (STANAG 2014): General Situation, Intelligence/Enemy, Friendly Forces, Operations, Admin/Logistics, Conclusions/Assessment.

## FEMA/ICS Variant

Under NIMS, the "situation report" is generated by the Situation/Documentation Unit. No single ICS form named "SITREP" — two parallel forms: ICS Form 201 (Incident Briefing, initial snapshot during command transfer) and ICS Form 209 (Incident Status Summary, ongoing periodic report). ICS derives from FIRESCOPE (1972-1980s, Southern California wildfire interagency protocols), codified into NIMS (2004).

Military SITREP emphasizes enemy; civilian variant emphasizes hazards, evacuations, infrastructure, public information. Military derives from staff organization doctrine; civilian derives from wildfire response protocols.

## Synthesis for AI Agent SITREP

Recommended fields for a Hermes agent SITREP, borrowing from both military (sequential numbered reports, "since last SITREP" framing, explicit timeline) and ICS (explicit summary items paired with narrative assessment):

1. Header — agent identity, profile, session ID, report DTG, reference to previous SITREP
2. Session Mission — brief task statement (references inherited intent)
3. Completed Work — activities since last SITREP
4. Key Results and Outputs — deliverables, tools invoked, successful verifications
5. Anomalies and Obstacles — failures, retries, blockers
6. Self-Assessment — agent's assessment of own state/capability
7. Planned Next Steps / Handoff — continuation for next session

## Why This Matters for the Substrate

The SITREP is the reporting artifact that operationalizes [[mission-command]] at the session level. The "since last SITREP" framing and sequential numbering provide continuity across agent sessions — the same pattern the Substrate's `log.md` already uses for its chronological record. The seven-field agent adaptation maps cleanly to the structured handoff discipline described in [[kanban-doctrine]].

The military/civilian distinction (enemy focus vs. hazard focus) maps to the agent context: an agent SITREP focuses on blockers, failures, and obstacles (the "enemy") rather than external threats. The ICS emphasis on resource shortages maps to rate limits, token budgets, and tool availability.

The section structure mirroring SMEAC connects to [[opord-format-deep-dive]] — the SITREP is the reporting counterpart to the operations order. Both are mission command artifacts. The structured, templated, closed-loop nature validated across centuries of military practice connects to [[multi-agent-coordination-patterns]] — fleet coordination requires standardized reporting formats.
