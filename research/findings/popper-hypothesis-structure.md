# Popper's Structural Pattern for Well-Formed Hypotheses

## Key Points

**The logical form has three components.** Universal statement: "All As are X," logically equivalent to "No A is not-X." The universal prohibits the existence of any A that is not-X. Basic statement: "There is an X at Y," singular and existential, about a specific spacetime region, intersubjectively testable. Prohibition structure: the universal "All As are X" is falsified by the basic statement "Some A is not-X," or equivalently "There is an A at Y that is not-X." The theory says such a thing cannot exist; if found, the theory is false.

**Verified examples.** Conservation of energy as "There is no perpetual motion machine": the putative basic statement "In spacetime region k there is a perpetual motion machine" would, if true, falsify the law. Einstein's light deflection: predicted deflection toward solid bodies, highly improbable under Newtonian physics; Eddington's 1919 confirmation was a severe test because the prediction was risky. "All swans are white": the paradigmatic universal falsifiable by a single counter-instance.

**Empirical content equals the class of potential falsifiers.** The more a theory prohibits, the more it says. A theory that says more has greater empirical content and is more falsifiable. Popper's rule: maximize content by making the theory more prohibitive, not less. "All As are X" says more than "Some As are X" because it prohibits more. Corroboration only counts when it is the positive result of a genuinely risky prediction, one that might conceivably have been false. Confirming evidence is cheap; what matters is whether the theory sticks its neck out.

**Scientific vs. non-scientific structure.** "All swans are white" is scientific: it prohibits non-white swans and is falsifiable by a single basic statement. "All human behavior is explained by unconscious drives" is not scientific: nothing could falsify it; any observed behavior can be reinterpreted as confirming. Popper saw this in Freud and Adler: their theories were amenable only to confirmation, never falsification. The structural difference is that scientific hypotheses have a non-empty class of potential falsifiers; non-scientific ones have an empty class. They explain everything by explaining away every possible counter-instance.

**Severity is a property of tests, not just theories.** A test is severe when the prediction is unlikely given background knowledge, especially if the theory being tested is false. To build severity into the hypothesis structure: make the prediction highly specific and quantitative (Einstein predicted a specific angle, not just "light bends"); make it improbable under the rival theory (if Newton is true, light should not bend, or should bend by half the GR amount); make it novel (predict something not yet observed, not retrofit known data); ensure it derives from the theory's core, not an auxiliary added to save it.

**Hypothesis vs. basic statement.** Hypothesis: universal, conjectural, goes beyond experience; cannot be verified, only falsified or corroborated; a guess, a conjecture. Basic statement: singular, existential, observational (though theory-laden), intersubjectively testable; can contradict a universal hypothesis; serves as potential falsifier. The testing relation: derive basic statements from the hypothesis; if an accepted basic statement contradicts the hypothesis, the hypothesis is falsified. The logic is modus tollens: if H implies B, and not-B, then not-H. Basic statements themselves are not incorrigible: they are accepted by convention, by decision of the research community, and remain open to further testing. Popper compares this to a jury verdict.

**The template.** A well-formed Popperian hypothesis has this skeleton: universal claim ("For all X, if condition C holds, then Y holds"); prohibition ("There cannot exist an X under C that is not-Y"); potential falsifier ("There exists an X at spacetime region R under C that is not-Y"); risky prediction (a specific, novel, quantitatively precise consequence improbable if the hypothesis is false); no escape clause (no auxiliary that would explain away the falsifier if observed).

**Six prescriptions for writing.** (1) Be bold: the hypothesis must go beyond existing knowledge; science starts with problems, not observations. (2) Be specific: use the universal form; avoid vague formulations that absorb any counter-example. (3) Be prohibitive: state what cannot happen; the more you prohibit, the more you say. (4) Make risky predictions: deduce consequences improbable under current theory. (5) Avoid vagueness: a theory that can explain anything explains nothing; the class of potential falsifiers must be non-empty and well-defined. (6) Avoid ad hoc rescue clauses: do not add auxiliary hypotheses solely to save the theory; modifications must increase, not decrease, falsifiability.

## Open Questions

- The basic statement's conventional status creates a regress risk: if falsifiers are accepted by community decision, what prevents immunization by refusing to accept the falsifying basic statement? Popper's answer is methodological rules, not logic; the demarcation is institutional as much as formal.
- Severity is context-dependent: the same prediction is severe against one background theory and trivial against another. This makes severity relational, not intrinsic, which complicates the template's application.
