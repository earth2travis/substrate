# Scientific Management (Taylorism)

## Summary

Scientific management is Frederick Winslow Taylor's system (1911) of analyzing and synthesizing workflows for economic efficiency. Taylor studied tasks scientifically to find the "one best way" to perform each task, then matched workers to jobs based on capability, trained them in optimal methods, and monitored performance against detailed plans. This led directly to mass production and the assembly line; Henry Ford adapted Taylor's principles with the moving assembly line in 1913. However, scientific management was criticized as dehumanizing (treating workers as cogs), creating over-specialization and repetitive stress, and suppressing worker agency. It was eventually superseded by lean production, which retains the scientific analysis of work but adds worker empowerment, continuous improvement, and respect for people.

## Key Claims

- Taylor's "one best way" approach treated human labor as engineerable like machines
- Scientific management created the analytical foundation for mass production
- Its dehumanizing effects (alienation, repetitive stress, monotony) were predictable consequences
- Lean production preserves scientific analysis while restoring human agency and creativity
