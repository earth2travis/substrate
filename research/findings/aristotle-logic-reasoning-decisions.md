# Aristotle: Logic, Reasoning, and Rational Decision-Making

## Summary

Aristotle invented logic as a discipline by separating the form of an argument from its content. The Organon, named by later commentators who held logic to be the instrument of all philosophy rather than a branch of it, contains the first completeness result in the history of logic: the categorical syllogism, defined at Prior Analytics I.1 (24b18-20) as a discourse in which, certain things being posited, something other than what was laid down results of necessity. Aristotle also drew the boundary that decision science keeps rediscovering: formal logic guarantees validity, not wisdom. Sound decisions need true premises, correct perception of particulars, and character that wants the right ends in the first place.

## The Demonstrative Ideal and Its Breaking Points

Episteme, scientific knowledge, requires premises that are true, primary, immediate, better known than, prior to, and causes of the conclusion (Posterior Analytics I.2). Because demonstration cannot ground its own axioms, induction (epagoge) and nous supply first principles (II.19). This architecture held for two millennia, then failed at specific, locatable joints. Hume's problem of induction (Treatise 1739) inverts Aristotle's solution: no reasoning certifies the passage from particulars to necessary universals. Popper accepted the negative result and re-founded method on deduction alone, keeping Aristotle's logic while abandoning his epistemic ambition for empirical science; see [[critical-rationalism]]. Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) exposed the expressive poverty of syllogistic: relational inference and multiple generality were unstatable in the system Aristotle had proven complete.

## Practical Reason: Phronesis and the Practical Syllogism

Phronesis, practical wisdom, is an intellectual virtue concerned with the variable and contingent sphere of action (Nicomachean Ethics VI). It cannot be a science and cannot be an art; ethical generalizations hold only "for the most part," and the decision "rests with perception." Deliberation runs backward in thought from an end to a first actionable step, then forward in execution. The practical syllogism (De Motu Animalium 701a; NE VII.3) concludes not in a proposition but in an action: when the universal premise and the perceptual minor premise unite, the agent acts immediately unless prevented. Akrasia, weakness of will, is analyzed as a failure of that perceptual minor premise, not of the universal rule. The structure anticipates BDI agent architectures by two millennia: desires play the ends, beliefs play the minor premise, intentions play prohairesis.

## The Rules-versus-Judgment Dialectic

The modern afterlife splits along the fault line Aristotle himself marked. The axiomatic method survived and was radicalized in mathematics (Hilbert, ZFC, formal verification), where his form was retained but his demand that axioms be self-evidently true of the world was dropped. The virtue-ethics revival (Anscombe 1957/1958, MacIntyre 1981, Flyvbjerg 2001) restored phronesis against rule-based ethics and rule-based social science. Dreyfus and Winograd carried the critique into AI: expert intelligence is intuitive and context-dependent, not rule-governed symbolic manipulation. Whether alignment can be achieved by explicit rules or requires something like trained judgment maps onto the Aristotle-versus-rules dialectic; the analogy is suggestive, not an established scholarly mapping.

## Where the Mapping Breaks

Intellectual honesty, carried over from the source: the syllogism's expressive poverty is real; the modal syllogistic is broken; epagoge does not deliver necessity; the practical syllogism is not a logic; phronesis resists the very formalization Aristotle invented for theory; akrasia remains contested. The thesis "Aristotle invented logic and practical rationality" holds, but the modern mappings are analogies with known failure points, not identities.
