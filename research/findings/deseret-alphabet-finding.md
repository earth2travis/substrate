# The Deseret Alphabet: Top-Down Linguistic Reform as Cautionary Tale

## The Attempt

In the 1850s, Brigham Young proposed the Deseret Alphabet: 38 phonemic letters for English, designed to replace irregular spelling. It was technically elegant, phonetically precise, and socially rejected.

## Why It Failed

- Costly to typeset
- Looked alien to readers
- Solved a problem most people did not perceive
- Imposed a solution on a functioning system

The Deseret Alphabet was abandoned within 20 years.

## The Contrast

Cyril and Sequoyah succeeded because they addressed genuine gaps: languages without writing systems. The Deseret Alphabet failed because it tried to replace an existing system that was "good enough." New tools succeed when they solve real problems, not when they optimize theoretical ones.

## The Structural Point

Top-down tool reform without user buy-in fails, even when technically superior. Bottom-up tool creation in response to genuine need succeeds, even when crude. The missionary linguist's greatest successes came from filling gaps, not from redesigning what already worked.
